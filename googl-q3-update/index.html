Alphabet (GOOGL) — The Backlog Came With Its Bill

In July we named two showings that would close most of the gap between our value and Alphabet's price. The third quarter delivered half of one: a $561 billion Cloud backlog, with just over 55% of the total backlog due within 24 months; whether the 36% segment margin holds once rented capacity is in use cannot yet be read. It also delivered the bill: $51.4 billion of capital spending, 40% of revenue, and a 2027 the company says will be "significantly" higher. We value Alphabet at $223.64, against the $324.89 close before the release. The largest step, −$33.13 (−$53.32 if applied last, about half the gap to the price), is a choice of method, not a fact of the quarter: fair value is now the company's own cash flows, with peer and segment multiples as cross-checks. The quarter's data, with our reading of them, add +$6.04. Our bull case, $338.17, sits above that close.

On 28 October Alphabet reported the order book our July note was waiting for. Cloud revenue doubled to $30.7 billion, its margin rose to 36.2%, and the backlog grew $47 billion in a quarter to $561 billion, with "just over 55%" expected as revenue within 24 months. July had named two showings that would close most of the gap: a 2027 capital plan below 35% of revenue with a path down, or the backlog converting at least half within 24 months with the segment margin holding above 30% after capacity eases. The same release showed what the order book costs. Capital spending rose 114% to $51.4 billion, more than the quarter's $42.3 billion of operating cash. This note asks what the order book is worth once its bill is counted.

Terms used throughout, defined once. Capex: purchases of property and equipment, the line Alphabet guides. Backlog: Cloud revenue under contract but not yet recognized (accountants call it remaining performance obligations). TPU systems: Alphabet's own AI chips (tensor processing units) sold as complete systems to outside customers; the release reports them as Cloud product revenue. Equity-security marks: gains and losses on the shares Alphabet owns in other companies (SpaceX the largest), most of them unrealized; ex-marks removes the after-tax mark, using the company's own footnote. Free cash flow to equity (FCFE): net income plus depreciation, less capex and working capital, plus net new borrowing; stock-based compensation stays an expense. Comps and SOTP (sum of the parts) value Alphabet at peer multiples on its own figures and at segment-by-segment multiples. Beta: how far a stock moves with the market, which sets its cost of equity. Growth stages: the forecast split into periods, each lasting as long as the thing that drives it. ROIC: after-tax operating income over invested capital (equity plus debt less cash), against the weighted average cost of capital (WACC). Terminal growth: the rate the business grows at forever after the forecast ends.

The case for the price

The case for the price, in the company's terms

On its own terms the quarter's growth was strong. Revenue rose 25% to $127.6 billion, the thirteenth straight quarter of double-digit growth. Search query growth was, in the release's words, "the highest it has been in the last five years." Google Cloud more than doubled, and its operating income tripled to $11.1 billion. The CFO said Cloud's growth excluding TPU systems was again incredibly strong. The backlog rose to $561 billion, and the share due within two years rose from just over half to just over 55%. Alphabet's own models now process nearly 32 billion tokens a minute through its APIs, more than four times a year ago; the Gemini app reached one billion users. TPU systems open a market the July note did not value as a separate line: Google Cloud's chief executive named Anthropic (directly and through Broadcom) and Apple among TPU users and new partnerships with Crux AI and Oracle to deliver TPU systems, and said the systems inventory is built only against committed contracts.

Excluding the marks, earnings per share rose 22% to $2.67 (roughly 8–10% if last year's EC fine is also removed). Return on invested capital is 30.1%, about three times the 10.2% cost of capital, and 39% without the $100 billion or so of private stakes that earn nothing in operating income. The CFO said Alphabet expects the return to stay meaningfully above its cost of capital throughout this investment cycle. On this view the cash-flow model is low because it charges the build against shareholders before the build earns.

The market's own lenses sit closer to the price than to us. At the peers' multiples on 28 October, our sum of the parts gives $369.53, above the price, and comps $291.02. Our own bull case, on inputs the backlog supports, is worth $338.17, above the price, which sits about 88% of the way from our base case to our bull. Fair value reaches the price at a cost of equity near 8.6% (8.2% at terminal), against our argued 10.4% (10.0% at terminal). Waymo raised $16 billion in February at a $126 billion valuation after the money, against the $75 billion at which fair value carries all of Other Bets in the base case ($20 billion bear, $175 billion bull), a house estimate, since Alphabet's share of Waymo is not disclosed.

This note tests four of these claims: the quarter's earnings once the marks are set aside, the backlog against the capital that delivers it, what the price requires, and how far the market's lenses sit from our cash-flow value. Whether those lenses or our cash-flow inputs better measure a business mid-build is a method judgment, argued in §4 and not a finding this note can test; so is the cost of equity. It cannot test, inside a model that runs to 2040, whether the AI products now reaching a billion users earn more per query than the search they change, or whether TPU systems become a second hardware business at chip-company margins. Management sizes neither, so we carry both as tripwires, not verdicts. Nor can it value Waymo on its own. Our sum of the parts values TPU systems at two to four times sales, as hardware, so the chip-company version of the claim sits outside it; valuing them at Cloud's multiples would lift that cross-check to $379. In the cash-flow value, a chip-company margin would arrive only through the terminal EBITDA margin (§5).

The print

What the quarter earned, and what the miss was made of

Revenue was $127.58 billion, up 25% in both reported and constant currency. Operating income was $40.58 billion, a 31.8% margin, up 30%. That growth rate flatters the quarter: a year ago Alphabet booked a $3.5 billion European Commission fine in its operating expenses, and without it operating income rose 17%. Google Services earned a 40.7% margin, against 42.5% a year earlier with the fine removed. Other cost of revenues rose 32%, which the CFO attributed mainly to hardware costs for TPU system sales, depreciation, content and data-center operations; research and development rose 36% on AI hiring and depreciation. Alphabet-level costs, mostly shared AI research, rose to $7.7 billion from $4.5 billion.

The miss, in two parts. Diluted EPS was $2.12 against consensus of $3.01 (MarketBeat) or $3.02 (Investing.com), a gap of about 89 cents. The release's footnote sizes the mark at 55 cents a share: an $8.7 billion loss on equity securities, which the CFO attributed primarily to SpaceX's share price, cut net income by $6.7 billion after tax. Without it EPS was $2.67, against $2.19 a year earlier on the same basis, up 22%; the year-ago quarter also carried the EC fine, about 28–29 cents a share before tax, and without both, growth is roughly 8–10%. If consensus excluded marks, the remaining gap of roughly 34 cents is not explained by them; we cannot tell from the compilations whether analysts modeled a mark at all.

The reported line misleads in both directions (Exhibit 1). In the first half, gains on the same holdings added $2.35 and $6.26 a share; on the July anchor date the reported trailing price-to-earnings ratio was 17 times, against 33 on clean earnings. Excluding the marks, quarterly EPS has run between $2.67 and $2.85 for a year. The reported figure has run between $2.12 and $9.11.

What we do with the stakes. The model counts them. Alphabet's $90.4 billion of marketable equity securities sit inside its $265.1 billion of cash and marketable securities, at the 30 September price, and we add that pile at book, as July did. The $135.2 billion of non-marketable stakes is added after a 22% tax, the rate Alphabet itself applied to the June-quarter gain. Together they are worth about $15.77 a share of fair value, so the quarter's mark moved value by about 54 cents after tax, not by the dollars it moved earnings. Carrying the non-marketable stakes at zero would lower fair value to $215.15. SpaceX has traded since 30 September; we take the filed mark and do not re-price it daily.

The order book

The order book arrived

Google Cloud revenue was $30.71 billion, up 103%, and its operating income $11.12 billion, a 36.2% margin against 23.7% a year earlier (Exhibit 2). The release says the growth was led by Google Cloud Platform and that "TPU systems sales were also a meaningful contributor." Neither gives a figure; the CFO said Google Cloud Platform accounted for the vast majority of the quarter's Cloud revenue. The backlog was $561 billion at 30 September, against $514 billion at 30 June. The CFO said "the majority" relates to "typical GCP contracts," and that Alphabet expects "to recognize just over 55% of the total backlog as revenues over the next 24 months." In June the figure was just over 50%. If the same share holds for Cloud, that is about $309 billion of Cloud revenue scheduled for the next eight quarters, against $93 billion recognized in the last four.

Two cautions sit beside the number. First, the margin faces headwinds. Alphabet began renting third-party computing capacity at the end of the quarter to meet demand it cannot yet serve itself; the CFO said "we expect margin headwinds in the near term as we utilize this capacity." Second, part of the growth is hardware. TPU systems are sold, not rented: the company says it builds them into inventory only after a contract is signed, and it bills customers as it delivers them. Inventory rose to $13.3 billion from $2.4 billion at the start of the year. Unless TPU systems earn chip-company margins, we assume a hardware sale is worth less per dollar of revenue than a cloud contract that renews. We do not know the share, so the sum-of-the-parts cross-check values TPU systems as their own line at an assumed 12% of 2027 Cloud revenue.

We built the 2027 base case from the backlog. Cloud revenue reaches $180 billion in 2027, against $160.5 billion in July's model, and grows 35% in 2028. Over the 24 months to September 2028 that puts about $391 billion through the segment, about 79% of it already under contract on the company's 55% statement; the rest is consumption outside the backlog. Search grows more slowly than in July's base case: 10% in 2027, not 13%. Search & other grew 19%, 17% and 12% in the three quarters of 2026, with currency and tougher comparisons named on the call, and the CFO warned that the fourth quarter laps the acceleration that began a year ago. On the final model, Cloud's path alone is worth about +$6.21: with July's $160.5 billion in the base case, fair value is $217.43. In the bridge, together with the slower Search, the revenue changes net +$0.53.

The bill

The bill

Capex was $51.36 billion, up 114%, or 40.3% of revenue. Operating cash flow was $42.29 billion, so free cash flow was −$9.07 billion, the second negative quarter in a row (Exhibit 3). The CFO noted that operating cash also carried the TPU inventory build and a $5.2 billion payment of a 2018 European Commission fine. For the twelve months, free cash flow was $19.7 billion against ex-marks net income of about $135 billion, so 15% of earnings reached shareholders as free cash. Capex ran 6.1 times depreciation in the quarter.

The guide fixes most of the fourth quarter. The CFO left 2026 capex at $195–205 billion, which after $131.95 billion in nine months leaves $63–73 billion for the fourth quarter, an annual pace of $252–292 billion. She repeated that capex will "increase significantly in 2027," with details on the fourth-quarter call, and gave no figure; press figures near $300 billion are not company guidance. July's engine carried $233 billion for 2027 even though management had already said "significantly higher." We now carry $285 billion in the base case, about 43% above 2026 and close to the fourth quarter's own pace, at a cost of $8.64 a share of fair value.

How to read it. Billions of dollars a quarter, Q3 2025 to Q3 2026, plus the fourth quarter the full-year guide implies (dashed outline; the bracket spans the range). Black is capital expenditures; gold is net cash from operations. Where black is taller than gold, the quarter spent more on capital than its operations produced. The one thing it shows: the bars crossed in the second quarter, and the guide implies the largest capex quarter yet in the fourth.

The bull carries $302 billion, the base's share of revenue on its larger revenue, because more demand needs more capacity. The bear carries the base's dollars on smaller revenue, because a build committed through suppliers and leases does not shrink within the year demand disappoints; the 10-Q's $213.4 billion of purchase commitments due within twelve months is one sign, though it includes items other than capex and the company calls the amounts maximums. A bear that cut faster, reaching terminal intensity over four years rather than six, would add about $2.64 to fair value.

The funding is now visible on the balance sheet. Long-term debt is $125.1 billion, from $46.5 billion at the start of the year, after $28.7 billion of new notes in the quarter. Alphabet sold $12.4 billion of new shares through its at-the-market program (36 million shares) and bought back none, against $39.8 billion in the first nine months of 2025. The CFO also gave the mix: about 65% of the quarter's technical-infrastructure spending was "servers and networking equipment, which generally have useful lives of six years." July assumed 55%. A larger server share means faster depreciation, a larger tax shield and, later, a larger replacement bill.

The commitments run further out still. The 10-Q, filed the day after the call, puts material purchase commitments at $924.4 billion at the end of September, plus $130.5 billion of data-center and energy leases that begin between 2026 and 2031 and $21.9 billion of funding commitments to equity investments. The purchase commitments are more than four times the 2026 capex guide but spread over several years: $213.4 billion falls due within twelve months, and most of the multi-year part by 2031. They cover technical infrastructure, inventory, energy and content licenses as well as open purchase orders, and the inventory part funds TPU systems, which the company says are built only against committed contracts. The CFO called them maximum contractual amounts, a number of them with "variable pricing and volume flexibility, which could result in lower spend."

Why the gap did not close. In the bridge, the new revenue path nets only +$0.53 (faster Cloud, slower Search; Cloud's path alone is worth about +$6.21 on the final model, §2), while the higher 2027 figure takes $8.64 and a slower fall from it takes $23.45 (about $13.53 on the final model), the second our reading of the commitment schedule rather than a fact of the quarter (§4). July's margin leg is still unread. Meeting it at 30% would add nothing to fair value (§7, tripwire 6), because our 44% terminal margin already carries Cloud at about its current 36% segment margin (§5); only two readings at or above 40% raise the number, and two below 30% cut it, so on this model July's backlog showing, with both legs met, no longer closes most of the gap, a change from July's statement. The gap widened mainly on the method: fair value is now the cash-flow value alone (−$33.13). Growth landing on the terminal rate (−$14.21) also lowered value; terminal growth from long-run GDP (+$9.22), the bull's longer Cloud stage (+$7.60), the filed balance sheet (+$7.50), the argued cost of equity (+$7.36), Other Bets carried as an asset (+$7.17) and the share count (+$4.12) went the other way.

How the methods are combined: −$33.13. The methods answer different questions. The cash-flow model asks what Alphabet's own future cash is worth at a cost of capital argued for this company. Comps and the sum of the parts ask what the market pays for peers and for businesses like Alphabet's segments; they inherit the market's discount rates and, in this cycle, a market pricing the same capital boom, so they cannot test whether the build earns its cost, which is the question this note is about. Damodaran treats intrinsic value and relative pricing as answers to different questions; a composite is a choice some practitioners make, and July made it, capping the relative methods at half. Cash conversion weighs against it now: free cash flow was 15% of ex-marks earnings over the last year, about 19% without the $5.2 billion fine payment, and about 27% with the nine-month TPU inventory build of $11.0 billion also added back, though that build is working capital for a product line, not a one-off.

The strongest case against the choice is that 59% of the base's operating value is terminal value, resting on a margin, a capex glide and a replacement cycle Alphabet does not date; our scenarios show that risk but do not hedge it, and averaging with the market's multiples is the usual hedge. We accept the cost because the question is whether the build earns its cost. We would revisit the weighting, as a judgment rather than a rule, once capex runs no more than 3 points of revenue above depreciation for four straight quarters (around 2033 on our base path), when the multiples are no longer pricing a build in progress. July's weights would give $276.96 today, so the choice, applied last, is −$53.32, about half of the $101.25 gap to the price. Dropping only the comps gives $272.27; an even split with the sum of the parts, $296.58.

The quarter's data and our reading of them: +$6.04, to $229.33. Rolling the valuation date to 28 October and booking nine months of filed results, with the larger cash pile and debt, adds +$7.50. The 2026 year and the higher server share move value +$2.05. Shares add +$4.12: we count the third quarter's diluted figure plus the convertible preferred as converted, at the maximum rate that applies below its initial price, 12,422 million in every scenario. July's projected counts added about 59 million at-the-market shares without their proceeds and about 258 million for suspended buybacks on top of stock compensation already expensed. The revenue path adds +$0.53, the 2027 capex figure takes $8.64, and a larger Cloud share of terminal capital intensity moves value +$0.48: Services at 10% of revenue, near Alphabet's whole-company level of 9.6–11.1% in 2021–23, and Cloud at 26%, a house estimate of a cloud provider at steady state.

The cost of equity: +$7.36. We argue the rate from Alphabet's situation. The 10-year Treasury was 5.05% on 28 October (4.67% in July), and we use a 4.5% equity risk premium. For the forecast years we use Alphabet's own 60-month beta against the S&P 500, 1.194, with a standard error of 0.20: a cost of equity of 10.4%. Its 30-month beta rose from 1.12 before the capex surge to 1.34 during it, and the same-cycle peers rose the same way (Microsoft 0.99 to 1.36; Meta 1.01 to 1.56; Amazon 1.39 to 1.55), but on windows that short the difference is within statistical noise (standard error about 0.5) and the build's expected cost is already in the cash flows, so we add no premium for it. Financial leverage adds little: debt is 3% of the equity's market value, and Alphabet's cash and debt securities exceed it by about $50 billion.

For the terminal value we use a beta of 1.10, a cost of equity of 10.0%, reached in a straight line across the 2029–33 fade: a mature advertising-and-cloud business, set from Alphabet's own pre-build 1.12. That treats the pre-build business as the mature one, a judgment the noise finding cannot test. Microsoft's, Meta's and Amazon's 60-month betas describe businesses still mid-build. Their pre-build reads are the strongest case for a lower terminal beta (median 1.01, Meta's 1.01), though their mean, 1.13, sits near Alphabet's own, and cyclical advertising keeps the rate above the market's. The higher Treasury alone would take $12.76 a share; the beta judgments add back $20.12. Those three peers' own 60-month betas would give 9.9%, 10.3% and 11.5%, unused. The other reads:

What changed in the cash-flow model, and why: −$13.05 in total. Six changes, in the order of the bridge.

  1. Terminal growth from long-run GDP, +$9.22. The Federal Reserve's projections of 16 September 2026 give 2.0% real growth and 2.0% inflation in the longer run, so 4.0% in the base and bull cases and 3.0% in the bear (a share-loss case), against July's 3.5%, 4.0% and 2.75%. July listed 4% growth forever among the Street's conventions, labelled there as nominal GDP-plus; this note sets 4.0% from the Federal Reserve's longer-run medians instead, and the Congressional Budget Office's lower 1.8% potential growth (about 3.8% nominal at 2% inflation) is the cross-check.
  2. Growth lands on the terminal rate, −$14.21. July's forecast ended in 2032 with base growth at 7.6% and then dropped to 3.5% in one step. Each business line now falls in a straight line from its 2028 rate to terminal growth by 2033.
  3. A slower fall in capex intensity, −$23.45. Intensity now falls in a straight line from its 2027 peak to terminal over six years, to 2033, rather than July's four, to 2031. The length comes from the company's commitment schedule: the 10-Q says the significant majority of $802 billion of multi-year commitments is fulfilled through 2031, so spending stays high for years after 2027. Six years is also one life of the servers that make up 65% of technical-infrastructure spend, and a server's life does not depend on the scenario, so the bull carries the same shape. Revenue keeps growing, so capex in dollars still rises after 2027, peaking at about $301 billion in 2029 in the base and $334 billion in 2029 in the bull, then falling to about $193 billion in the base by 2033. Management has dated neither a decline after 2027 nor the 2028–29 spending we carry. A reader who expects a faster fall sits below our base on capex; July's four-year shape on today's model gives $237.17.
  4. The bull's Cloud stage runs a year longer, +$7.60. With terminal growth capped at long-run GDP, stage length is where the bull's growth upside lives, and the backlog supports the year for Cloud: about 45% of the $561 billion, some $252 billion, is due beyond 24 months. The other lines, and the base and bear, keep 2033.
  5. Other Bets is carried as an asset, +$7.17. Its revenue and its operating loss, 1.7% of revenue over nine months, leave the consolidated paths, and the business is added at house estimates of $75 billion in the base ($20 billion bear, $175 billion bull), so it is counted once. Charging the losses through the margin path while valuing the assets at zero was inconsistent.
  6. The forecast runs to steady state, +$0.62. Ending in 2033 left the terminal year carrying the build's depreciation, 17.8% of base revenue and above the 17.3% the revenue mix implies, which forced terminal capex up to that level. Seven more years at terminal growth and intensity let the peak vintages finish depreciating; depreciation settles at 12.5% of revenue by 2040, and terminal capex is the mix level (Exhibit 5). The terminal year then reinvests 19% of after-tax operating profit to grow 4%, an implied 21% return on new capital (24% bull, 10% bear) against a trailing 30.1%.

Capex is still the input with the least guidance behind it. Two points lower or higher at terminal moves fair value to $241 or $206, and a 2027 figure of $260 billion or $320 billion moves it to $231 or $216.

The price

Terminal value is 59% of the base case's operating value (57% in the bull, 66% in the bear), so the price, like our value, is mostly a claim about the years after the build. The margin is the lever we can least see. The base carries a 44% terminal EBITDA margin, against 40.0% over the first nine months of 2026. We set it from the mix: Cloud is 46% of base revenue by 2033 and earns a 36% segment operating margin after depreciation, so its EBITDA margin is higher still, less the Alphabet-level AI research cost. Even so, our base ends 2040 at a 31.5% operating margin, below the 31.8% of the third quarter. The 42–46% sensitivity ($215 to $233) is where our judgment sits. Two bets. The price is therefore close to one of two bets. One is that our inputs for the build understate value by enough to reach the price: the terminal margin or the cost of equity, alone or with a faster capex glide. The sum of the parts says so: it is above the price, though comps, at $291.02, are below it. The other is that our own bull path is close to certain: with no weight on the bear the price needs about 88% on the bull, and any bear weight above about 5.4% puts it out of reach on our three cases.

What would change our view

We concede if the evidence tracks the whole bull path: 2027 capex guided at or below the bull's $302 billion, Cloud revenue reaching $205 billion in 2027, Search & other growing at or above 13% and 11% in 2027 and 2028, and Cloud operating margin at or above 40% in the tripwire-6 quarters (the path carries a 46.5% terminal EBITDA margin, 16.0% terminal capex and 4.0% terminal growth). That path is worth $338.17, above today's price, and the bull's inputs would become the base case in the next update. The first two observables alone, in the base case, lift value to about $229 with capex at the $302 billion ceiling, or about $232 with capex at the base's $285 billion; a lower guide takes it higher.

The case hardens on the mirror: Cloud at or below $155 billion with 2027 capex guided at or above $285 billion, Search & other at or below 7% and 6% in 2027 and 2028, and Cloud operating margin below 30% in the tripwire-6 quarters (the path carries a 38% terminal EBITDA margin, 21.2% terminal capex and 3.0% terminal growth). That path is worth $92.95, and the bear's inputs would become the base case. The first two observables alone take value to about $216 with capex at the $285 billion floor, the base's own figure; a higher guide takes it lower.

Short of the bull path, on one lever alone, the price is right on our frame in two observable ways. The levers also combine: at a cost of equity one point lower (explicit and terminal), the base case alone reaches the price at a 48.1% terminal margin. The capex glide does not reach the price alone: July's four-year shape gives $237.17, and even a drop to terminal intensity in 2028 gives about $257.

  • Cloud's EBITDA margin reaches about 74%. A depreciation disclosure alone cannot show that: on the nine months to September, even with all of Alphabet's $22.0 billion of depreciation in Cloud, its EBITDA margin would be about 64%, so this path needs Cloud's operating margin itself to keep rising, read through a segment disclosure if one comes. With Cloud at 46% of revenue and the rest of Alphabet at 40% (the whole company's nine-month EBITDA margin, used as a proxy), that is a terminal margin of 55.6% in the base, about 12 points above ours. The base alone at that margin gives fair value $275.29; fair value reaches the price only if the bear and bull margins rise by the same points.
  • The 10-year Treasury falls to about 3.2% at our forecast-year beta, which gives an 8.6% cost of equity (8.2% at terminal). A lower beta alone would need about 0.78 in the forecast years and 0.69 at terminal, below every read we hold.

A third route, the weighting from July's 50/25/25, reaches the price only alongside the bull path's evidence. Revisited on the §4 condition, it gives $276.96 today, below the price; with that evidence, about $361.51, above it.

The cost of equity has its own tests, each on a read that would survive the noise test that set the build premium aside. The forecast-year beta is re-measured at every update, so it moves value either way on its own, as the Treasury does. We would add a build premium, toward the 1.34 build-period read ($218.88), only on a 30-month beta at or above 1.30 on a window that does not overlap April 2024 to September 2026, or a 60-month beta at or above 1.30. We would lower the terminal beta toward 1.0 ($237.06) on a 60-month beta at or below 1.05 at any update, or a 30-month beta at or below 1.05 on a window that begins once capex runs within 3 points of depreciation.

The call

The call

We value Alphabet at $223.64 a share, from $256.42. The quarter brought half of what our July note was waiting for, and the backlog lifted value. The same quarter set a capital bill that leaves free cash flow negative and has the company borrowing and selling stock. That bill is larger than July's model carried, and we now assume capex intensity falls over six years, in line with the company's own commitment schedule (−$23.45), a decline management has not dated. Our argued cost of equity moved value +$7.36. The largest change is how the methods are combined (−$33.13): we value the company on its own cash flows and show the market's multiples beside them. To reach $324.89 on one lever alone, fair value needs a cost of equity near 8.6% or terminal margins about 12 points above ours in every scenario (the base case alone needs 8.7% or 54.4%); smaller moves in both together reach it too. The other routes are that the sum of the parts, which is above the price, is the better measure, or that our own bull path, worth $338.17, is close to certain. The fourth-quarter call, with the 2027 capex number, is the next test. The Fund holds no position in GOOGL.
