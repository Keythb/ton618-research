Meta (META) — The 2027 Bill Is Already Signed

Meta's third quarter grew revenue 26% and operating income 11% before a $9.9 billion settlement charge. The 10-Q filed the next day schedules $121 billion of non-cancelable commitments for 2027, up $40 billion in three months; management gave no 2027 capital-spending number and announced a non-voting share class that can be used, among other stated purposes, to raise capital. On our July method the quarter would have lifted fair value from $370 to $386. We now value Meta at $312.11, mainly because we no longer weight a price-to-earnings value while its earnings are not turning into cash; each change is set out for the reader. At the $697.52 close on the day of the release the stock traded 123% above it.

In July we valued Meta as a company that pays for its machines twice: in cash when it buys them, and in earnings as they depreciate. The third quarter enlarged both payments. The 10-Q schedules $121 billion of commitments for 2027, mostly cloud capacity, servers and data-center energy, not all of it capital spending; depreciation grew 44%, on a server life the company left at five and a half years. The quarter also brought a third instrument. Meta has bought back no stock this year, ended September with net debt near zero after a $25 billion bond, and on 21 October announced a non-voting Class C stock described as usable for "capital raising, employee compensation, acquisitions, other general corporate purposes." This note asks what the bill for this build is worth to a shareholder at $697.52.

Terms used throughout, defined once. Capex: capital expenditures including principal payments on finance leases, the basis on which Meta guides. Ex-charge: a figure with the disclosed one-time legal and severance charges removed, as Meta itself presents them. The dose: capex divided by net income for the same period, the ratio our depreciation series carries for every company. Free cash flow to equity (FCFE): net income plus depreciation, less capex, plus net new borrowing; share-based compensation stays an expense and is never added back. Re-mark: the house restatement of server depreciation onto a three-year life, against Meta's five to 5.5 years; a model estimate, not a restatement, and Meta's reported figures comply with GAAP. Growth stages: the forecast is split into periods, each lasting as long as the thing that drives it; every length is argued in the stage table. ROIC: after-tax operating income over invested capital (equity plus debt less cash), trailing twelve months, against the weighted average cost of capital (WACC). Terminal growth: the rate a business grows at forever after the forecast ends, set here from long-run GDP projections. Fair value: the FCFE value, probability-weighted across three scenarios at 25 / 50 / 25; the price-to-earnings value July weighted at 40% is shown as a cross-check (§4).

The case for the price

The case for the price, in the company's terms

On its own terms the quarter was strong. Revenue of $64.6 billion beat the top of the $61–64 billion guide and the $63.37 billion LSEG consensus, on 10% more ad impressions and a 14% higher average price per ad. Operating income excluding the charge rose 11% to $22.88 billion while absorbing 45% higher cost of revenue and 32% more research spending, with headcount down 15% to 66,775. The fourth quarter is guided to $73–76 billion, 22–27% growth, and management wrote that the AI investments "position us to sustain strong overall revenue growth throughout 2027." Reality Labs losses are guided down to about $15 billion next year from $18–19 billion.

Management called Muse "one of our most successful launches ever": more than 10 million people in the US and Canada set up the personal agent in six weeks, and about a quarter use it daily. Meta One passed 15 million subscriptions and trials; partnership ads passed a $15 billion run-rate, up 97%. The chief executive expects Muse's cost efficiency to improve "more than 10x in the next year" even as total serving costs rise with its users, which is what would turn a compute-hungry product into a profitable one. The balance sheet carries $82.4B of cash against $83.7B of debt, the teen mental-health claims of 52 attorneys general are settled, and the Class C stock lets the company fund the build without giving up control.

The arithmetic offered for the price is simple. On our own base-case 2027 earnings of $32.02 a share, $697.52 is 21.8 times earnings, the top of the 16–22 range our price-to-earnings cross-check uses, for a company growing revenue 26% with a 34% return on capital. Price each scenario's 2027 earnings as reported at our multiples and the weighted result is $598, 14% below the price. On this view the cash-flow model is low only because it charges seven years of build-out against equity before the build earns. And the build is not a fixed cost: in 2023, which management called its "year of efficiency", Meta cut capital spending from $32.0 billion to $28.1 billion, and it could do so again. Nor is the cost of equity a constant: the 1.35 beta behind it was measured on two years of daily prices, and a reader who believes Meta's risk is closer to the market's will discount at a lower rate.

This note tests four of these claims: the quarter's ex-charge earnings and their basis; the capital bill against the cash the business generates; the price-to-earnings case on reported and re-marked earnings; and the growth the price implies through 2033. It cannot test, inside a seven-year model, whether Muse and the enterprise platform become revenue lines large enough to carry the capital committed for them. Management gives them no size, and neither do we beyond a point of growth in the base case and four to five in the bull. That is where the price's case lives, and it is carried as a scenario and a tripwire, not a verdict.

The print

What the quarter earned, and what the beat was made of

Revenue was $64.60 billion, up 26% on both a reported and constant-currency basis. Costs and expenses were $51.61 billion, up 68%, of which $9.89 billion was the charge for the August agreement with 52 attorneys general across US states, territories and the District of Columbia to resolve teen mental-health litigation. Reported operating income was $12.99 billion, a 20% margin. Excluding the charge it was $22.88 billion, a 35.4% margin, against 40.1% a year earlier and 36.8% in the June quarter on the same basis. Net income was $9.86 billion, or $3.83 a diluted share; excluding the charge, $7.40.

The beat, like for like. The brokerage feed shows $7.40 against a $5.74 consensus, a 29% beat. The two figures are not on the same basis. The settlement was announced in August, and the compiled figures show analysts split on whether to model the charge: Investing.com carried a GAAP-basis consensus of $3.57, CNBC reported $5.75 and said it was "not clear" the $3.83 was comparable, and MarketBeat showed $6.54. A pool between $3.57 and the ex-charge level mixes both treatments and cannot measure a surprise. The charge is $3.57 a share after tax, so analysts behind the $3.57 GAAP consensus who carried it would have been near $7.14 ex-charge, close to the June quarter's $7.18 consensus (our inference, not a compiled number): a beat of about 26 cents, roughly 4%. MarketBeat's pool can sit only at or below a pure ex-charge consensus, which caps the beat at 13%. Either way it is a fraction of 29%; on revenue, where the basis is clean, the beat was 1.9% against LSEG. Two more distortions: the charge is mostly non-deductible (a 24% tax rate reported, 17% without it), so it cost $9.19B after a tax shield of only $0.70B; and the reported 265% EPS growth compares with a year-ago $1.05 that carried a one-time tax charge (an 87% tax rate that quarter). Against the year-ago $7.25 excluding that charge, ex-charge EPS grew 2.1%.

The house values the company on the anchor close. One session says more about positioning than about the bill, which is where the quarter's real news sits.

The bill

The bill: the floor, the fourth quarter, and 2027

The 2026 capex guide has moved one way all year: $115–135 billion in January, $125–145 billion in April, $130–145 billion in July and, on 21 October, $135–145 billion, "narrowed from our prior outlook." The ceiling has not moved since April; the floor has risen $20 billion. Nine months of spending, $85.82 billion, leave $49.2B to $59.2B for the fourth quarter alone, $54.2B at the midpoint, 55% above the third quarter's $34.91 billion. Even the floor implies a 41% step in one quarter.

For 2027, management gave words, not a number. The written outlook says the company is "still developing our budget and capacity plans for next year, but we expect to continue investing aggressively to support both our core business and emerging AI initiatives," and that the investments in Meta Superintelligence Labs "will take longer to generate returns than those in our core business." On the call the chief financial officer said the company is "evaluating a lot of investment proposals that have attractive return profiles over different time horizons," with nothing "more specific or quantitative to share until we get to the Q4 call."

The 10-Q supplies the numbers. Non-cancelable contractual commitments were $454.52 billion at 30 September, up $105 billion from $349.31 billion at 30 June; $121.41 billion fall due in 2027, against $81.65 billion three months earlier. They "mostly relate to third-party cloud capacity arrangements and investments in servers and network infrastructure, energy service agreements for data centers, and consumer hardware products in Reality Labs," so not all of it is capex: third-party cloud is an operating cost, and the CFO named it as one driver of the growth in infrastructure costs. Leases signed but not yet started reached $355.73 billion, from $278.99 billion; the June 10-Q had disclosed about $68 billion of further data-center leases signed in July for 2027–28, and the rise is consistent with them, though the September filing does not itemize it. Construction in progress, assets bought but not yet depreciating, stood at $89.50 billion, from $50.52 billion at the start of the year.

The second payment. Depreciation and amortization was $7.15 billion, up 44%; servers-and-network depreciation, reported separately in the 10-Q, was $5.34 billion, up 48% on the year and 16% on the quarter. The 10-Q discloses no change in estimate and no material change to accounting policies, so the five-to-5.5-year server life in the 2025 10-K stands, as it did in June. On the house three-year clock, re-run with this year's capex, the 2027 depreciation the longer schedule defers rises to $15.17B pre-tax from $13.55 billion in July: $4.89 a share of 2027 earnings after tax, from $4.38. Both payments are growing faster than the earnings that should cover them, which raises the question of who pays.

The funding

Who funds it

In 2025 operating cash covered the build with $43.6 billion of free cash flow to spare, and the company bought back $26.2 billion of stock. In 2026 it does not: Meta issued $24.91 billion of notes in May, has repurchased nothing, and has $11.05 billion of free cash flow for nine months. On the guide midpoints the fourth quarter consumes about $26B of free cash flow to equity before any borrowing (the house measure, with share-based compensation charged as a cost; about $18B on the company's own definition). The September 10-Q hardens a sentence from June: where the company "may also seek" to raise capital, it now says, "From time to time we expect to seek to raise additional capital through debt, equity, or other financing arrangements." The Class C stock gives the equity part of that sentence an instrument. Its stated purposes include "capital raising, employee compensation, acquisitions, other general corporate purposes"; it carries no vote, will be distributed one-for-one to existing holders (in effect a two-for-one split) once the amended charter takes effect on 19 January 2027, and was approved by shareholder written consent, which the founder's majority of the vote (a risk factor the 10-Q restates) makes possible. The CFO said: "We don't have any specific financing plans to announce at this time."

The model funds each year's shortfall as July's did: net borrowing on a stated path, with any remaining negative free cash flow charged against equity, as if raised at fair value. On that basis the base case needs $69B of external capital across 2027–29 before borrowing, the bear case $116B, and the bull case only $9B. Issuing Class C above the house value would, on the house's numbers, transfer value from new buyers to existing holders; issuing below it would do the reverse. The note does not forecast an issue; the first one is a tripwire.

The settlement is a claim on the same cash. About $12.7 billion is payable to the states in annual installments over ten years; a further $5.3 billion is contingent and not accrued, payable state by state only if peers such as YouTube and TikTok adopt the same teen protections and each such peer with more than $10 billion of annual profit is obliged to pay the same amount. Legal accruals rose to $19.88 billion from $7.32 billion at year-end. The model deducts the accrued charge after its tax shield ($9.19B) in every case and adds the contingent amount in the bear case. With the bill sized and its funding read, the question becomes what the shares are worth.

The value

What Meta is worth after the print, and why the number moved

The engine is the July note's cash-flow-to-equity model. Share-based compensation is expensed and the diluted share count (2,573 million) is held flat, which is neutral here: buybacks, or Class C shares issued, at fair value move no value a share. The cost of equity is rebuilt on the 21 October ten-year Treasury yield of 5.13% (from 4.67%), with the July beta of 1.35 and a 4.5% equity risk premium: 11.21%. The forecast now runs from 2027 to 2033 with every stage length argued, and the return on capital is set against its cost quarter by quarter. Three things changed in how we value Meta, and the reader should be able to separate each from what the quarter did.

How the cash-flow model changed, and why. Two changes to how the model is calculated, each with its own row near the end of the attribution table. 1. Terminal growth now comes from long-run GDP. July used 3.5% (3.0% in the bear case) as a house convention. This note uses the Federal Reserve's September projections for the longer run, 2.0% real growth and 2.0% inflation: 4.0% nominal in the base and bull cases, and one point less in the bear case, where Meta loses share of the economy. Effect: +$9.83. 2. Every fade now lands on its terminal value. July's growth and capex paths stopped short of terminal and stepped down at the horizon. Here revenue growth and capex intensity fall in a straight line from the end of each stage to their terminal values in 2033. Effect: −$5.64. On July's method the quarter's data alone would have moved fair value from $370 to $385.51; with these two changes and July's weighting, it is $389.71.
A separate judgment: why the price-to-earnings value is no longer weighted. This is a decision about which methods count, not part of the cash-flow changes above. July weighted a price-to-earnings value on 2027 earnings at 40%. An earnings multiple is a fair lens only when earnings turn into cash a shareholder can have: capex near depreciation, most of net income becoming free cash flow, the rest paid out or bought back. At Meta that condition does not hold. Free cash flow was 72% of net income in 2025 (57% before that year's one-time tax charge), 5% in the June quarter, 21% for the first nine months of 2026 (about 20% with the legal and severance charges added back and the first quarter's one-time tax benefit removed) and negative in the third quarter; capex ran 4.9 times depreciation in the third quarter (4.4 for nine months), against 3.9 in 2025; no shares have been bought back this year. Nor is it about to return: $121 billion of commitments fall due in 2027 and $287B after it, not all of it capital spending, but base-case capex in 2027 alone is 1.94 times net income. The problem is not the multiples' growth. On the house's own base-case path, with every dollar distributable, 2027 earnings would be worth about 18 times, inside the 16–22 range, and about 14 times once the terminal years reinvest enough to grow. The problem is conversion: the base-case cash flows value the same earnings at about 9 times, because so much of them is spent on capacity. The condition was already weak when the July note was written, on a quarter that converted about 5% of its earnings; the 10-Q's 2027 commitments make it a judgment rather than a timing question. The lens stays as a cross-check. Effect: −$77.59, to $312.11. The weight comes back if earnings turn back into cash (tripwire 9).

Why these lengths, and why these fades. Once its driver stops, above-average growth rarely lasts: across US companies from 1951 to 1998, sales growth stayed above the median five years running for 6.3% of firms and ten years running for 0.9% (Chan, Karceski and Lakonishok, 2003). Hence five years of fade wherever stage 1 ends, in a straight line, because nothing in the filings dates a faster or slower step down. Advertising's two years rest on what the company has itself dated; a third year is the single input most in the price's favor, and it is priced below. The capital build's one year is the shortest the filings support; a longer build is the assumption that most hurts value, and it is priced too.

Two levels the reader should see argued. The cross-check's 16–22 range is July's, and it brackets what the market itself has paid on our base-case 2027 earnings: 17.9 times at the July close, 21.8 times on 21 October. Read on a single stage, 18.5 times next year's earnings implies about 5.8% growth in perpetuity at the 11.21% cost of equity, 22 times about 6.7% and 16 times about 5.0%; on the house's own two-stage path the base-case multiple is about 18 times, so the lens is set aside for conversion, not for growth. Terminal capex is the other level. At 22% of revenue, against depreciation of 13.5% and 4.0% growth, the base case's new capital earns about 13.1% in perpetuity, a little above the 11.21% cost of equity: modest excess returns persist. The bull case's 16% lets them run at about 35%; the bear case's 26%, at 3.0% growth, leaves them below the cost of capital, at 7%. A reader who thinks the build's returns persist should look at the bull column.

How to read it. Trailing-twelve-month return on invested capital, percent, December 2023 to September 2026. Gold adds back the disclosed one-time charges at a 17% tax rate; gray is as reported and swings with one-time tax items (a charge in the third quarter of 2025, a benefit in the first quarter of 2026). The dashed line and shaded band below it mark the 10.9% cost of capital, held flat. Compare the gold line's slope with its distance above the dashed line. The one thing it shows: the return is three times its cost, and the gold line has fallen in five of the last six quarters as invested capital outgrew operating income.

Return on capital: strong, and fading. Meta's return on invested capital is 33.5% on a trailing basis against a 10.9% cost of capital: by the house's mechanical rule, a strength regime turning to fading, still far above the hurdle but with the spread down 4.4 points in four quarters. With the one-time charges added back the decline is steeper, from 45% to 34%, as average invested capital grew 50% in a year and operating income did not. A return this far above its cost is why the price's case deserves a hearing; its direction is why the house does not extend the stage that produces it.

What the price requires. Hold every other base-case input and solve for the one that makes the cash-flow value equal $697.52. Revenue would have to compound 25.0% a year from 2027 to 2033, against 10.9% in the base case: $1,219B of revenue in 2033, against $528B (Exhibit 5). Or the cost of equity would have to be 7.6%, an equity risk premium near 1.8% on today's Treasury yield. Or perpetual growth would have to be 8.3%, far above long-run nominal growth of the economy. No level of terminal capex gets there, even zero. Scaling every scenario's growth together, the base case has to compound at 23.1%; at a cost of equity of 10.2% the price still needs 2.0 times the base case's growth rates, and at 12.2%, 2.6 times. At the next session's $648.85, the revenue requirement is 23.7%. The price rests not on one heroic input but on the 2026 growth rate continuing for most of a decade at a scale where it has never been sustained, or on the new AI lines becoming a second business the size of the first.

The tripwires

What carries the story to the next update

$49.2B–$59.2Bthe fourth-quarter capex the guide implies — the first test, on the next print, of whether the floor is a commitment or an intention

Each tripwire carries its basis, its tolerance and what the model does with the answer, so the next update applies rules written now. The fourth-quarter release is expected around 27 January 2027 (unconfirmed on the brokerage calendar).

  1. Fourth-quarter capex. Basis: the Q4 release's capex including finance leases, against the $49.2B–$59.2B the full-year guide implies. Inside the range: the base case's 2027 build stage holds. Below $49.2B: the year finishes under the floor set in October. The base case's 2027 capex then moves halfway toward the bull's 48% of revenue, and the house reads the floors as intentions, not commitments. Above $59.2B: the build stage is re-run at two years ($295).
  2. The 2027 capex guide (Q4 call). Basis: the guided range against base-case 2027 revenue of $307B. At or below 40% of it, about $123B, with a stated glide path: the July trigger to move toward a neutral view fires and the build stage is removed ($329). Between that and $148B (the bull case's 48% of revenue), a guided midpoint moves base-case 2027 capex halfway toward the bull's ($316; one year of capex moves little). From there to $160B: the base case holds. Between $160B and the bear case's 55% of revenue (about $169B), a guided midpoint moves base-case 2027 capex halfway toward the bear's ($309). Above 55%: the build stage moves to two years ($295). A guide with no range — "invest aggressively" again — is read as above 55%.
  3. Commitments due in 2028. Basis: the FY2026 10-K's commitments schedule (filed about late January to February 2027). If the commitments due in 2028 exceed the $121 billion now due in 2027, the build has a second signed year and the stage moves to two years ($295). Below $80 billion, the build has no second signed year and the stage is removed ($329). Either reading applies whatever the 2027 guide says.
  4. The first Class C sale for cash. Basis: an 8-K, a prospectus supplement, or the financing section of a 10-Q after 19 January 2027. Record the proceeds and the price against the house value at the time. Proceeds above 2% of the market value in any twelve months mark the funding path as equity-led; the model's net-borrowing path is then cut by the proceeds. Fair value changes only by the difference between the sale price and the house value on the shares sold. Grants to employees in Class C are not a sale.
  5. Ex-charge operating margin, fourth quarter and the 2027 expense guide. Basis: the Q4 release (the guide implies about 33%) and the 2027 expense outlook. A 2027 expense guide that implies operating income growing more than ten points slower than revenue moves the base case's 2027–28 EBITDA margin to the bear's path. Two consecutive quarters at or above 40% excluding charges move it to the bull's. The July concession test (45%+ with ad growth above 20%) stays armed.
  6. The youth-safety line. Basis: the 10-Q and 10-K legal notes and any 8-K. The consolidated personal-injury bellwether opening 28 October 2026, the school-district case in February 2027 and the bellwethers of 16 March and 6 August 2027 are the dated events. Any accrual of the $5.3 billion contingent payment moves it from the bear case into every case. Disclosed legal charges above $2 billion in 2027 complete the July test: the legal line becomes a recurring cost, and the base margin is cut by the 2027 amount spread over five years.
  7. The server clock. Basis: the FY2026 10-K useful-life note. Any change in estimate re-runs the re-mark. A move to four years or less would close most of the $92 spread between reported and re-marked earnings in the earnings cross-check; the cash-flow value depends on the book useful life only through the tax line.
  8. The price. Fair value $312.11. A close at or below it resolves the gap. At $234, a 25% margin of safety, the house would look for an entry if the operating tests above are intact (this replaces the July note's $278).
  9. Earnings turning back into cash. Basis: free cash flow (Meta's definition) over net income, quarterly, and the buyback line. Two consecutive quarters with free cash flow at or above 60% of net income, or a 2027 guide that takes capex back below twice depreciation, or buybacks resuming at more than $5 billion a quarter: the condition for an earnings multiple holds again, and the price-to-earnings value returns at July's 40% weight ($389.71 at today's inputs). Between 20% and 60%: unweighted, but two consecutive quarters above 40% restore half the July weight, 20% ($351). Below 20% for two more quarters: the cross-check stays unweighted and the note says so.

What would change our view

The house would move toward the price on any two of the following. A 2027 capex guide at or below 40% of revenue with a dated glide path (tripwire 2), which ends the build stage. Commitments due in 2028 below $80 billion (tripwire 3). Free cash flow back at 60% or more of net income for two quarters, or buybacks above $5 billion a quarter (tripwire 9), which restores the price-to-earnings weight. Muse or the enterprise platform disclosed as a revenue line above $10 billion a year within 2027, which would justify a third year of stage-1 growth. Two consecutive quarters of 40%+ operating margin excluding charges while advertising grows above 20%. Or a return on invested capital, charges added back, that stops falling at the Q4 print. Any two move the base case toward the bull's inputs and the fair value with it.

The house would move toward the bear case's inputs on any two of the mirror conditions. A 2027 guide above the bear case's 55% of revenue, or no range at all. Commitments due in 2028 above $121 billion (tripwire 3). A second year of $2 billion-plus legal charges (tripwire 6). Or advertising growth below 15% in any quarter of 2027 with capex still above half of revenue. A Class C sale for cash is not on either list: it is information about funding, not about the business, and it is scored on its price.
