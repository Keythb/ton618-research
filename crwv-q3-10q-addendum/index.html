CoreWeave (CRWV) Q3 10-Q Addendum — Who Gets Paid First

CoreWeave's quarterly report, filed the day after our update, answers what the release could not: who holds which claim on the fleet, what falls due before 2028, and what the customer money costs. With three parts of our method rebuilt to fit the company's capital structure, our value moves from $48.57 to $49.55, against $44.91 at the 19 November close.

TON618 Capital Research · As of 19 November 2026 · Download PDF · Download model

Our update of 11 November, The Floor Moved to the Fleet, valued CoreWeave at $48.57 on the earnings release; the Form 10-Q reached EDGAR on 12 November. The filing's four new inputs are small, and rebuilding three parts of our method moves the value more. What the filing changes is the picture of who is paid first: the lenders secured on each fleet, the customers who prepaid for capacity, the landlords of sites not yet built and, last, the shareholders. Nothing due in the next 15 months is a bond; by our estimate about half of it is vendor credit rather than contract-funded loans. $1.9 billion of customer prepayments sat on contracts that became cancellable in a single quarter, without explanation. And the parent's guarantees and its revolver are the two bridges by which one subsidiary's default could become the group's. We trace that path quarter by quarter and list the events that would show it starting.

The questions

What this addendum answers

  1. What did the 10-Q change in the value, and what did we change in the method?
  2. Of the ~$9 billion of prepayments and deposits due over the next three months, how much is fixed, how much waits on delivery or power, and what does that money cost?
  3. How do prepayments sit beside the delayed-draw loans, and who is paid first if a customer defaults?
  4. Why did $1.9 billion move from deferred revenue to customer liabilities, and is it refundable in cash?
  5. Why did recourse debt rise $10.2 billion in a quarter under an asset-level model, and how much of the $13.4 billion due in the next 15 months is amortization paid from customer cash?
  6. How much of the pending leases and commitments reaches the balance sheet within 18 months, and should prepayments on undelivered capacity count as debt?
  7. How do the GPUs' book life and the loans' lives compare, what renewal realization does the price need, and what does the capital cost?

The view. We value CoreWeave at $49.55 a share, 10% above the $44.91 close of 19 November, from $48.57 in our update. The filing's facts take off $1.65 (the share count, debt at principal, the maturity ladder and $1.9 billion of customer liabilities); the rebuilt method adds back more: each case's cost of equity now follows its own leverage (−$1.58), and the terminal value is timed like the other cash flows (+$4.25). Read the value as a range, not a point: the cases run from zero to $147.32, the bull's fifth of the weight carries 59% of the value, and distress weights of 30% to 10% give $39.46 to $59.64. The price needs a distress weight of 27% rather than our 20% (the other weights scaled down in proportion), or base renewals at 24% of the company's figure rather than 60%. Our 20% sits at the favorable end of the 20–30% we take from credit-default-swap pricing (about 60% at risk-neutral odds over five years, which we judge at a third to a half of that in real-world odds). The price's 27% lies inside that range: at its midpoint, 25%, the value is $46.45, 3% above the price, so most of the gap rests on our choice of the range's lower end. The 20% holds on one condition: a parent-only runway finds that within 2027 a default needs two failures together, but by mid-2028 the parent's own market staying shut is enough, so the parent must raise at a workable price by mid-2027. The filing's sharpest new fact is not in the value: in one quarter, about a fifth of the opening prepayment balance became cancellable and moved to a current liability, and the filing does not say why.

Terms used throughout, defined once. Recourse debt: debt CoreWeave, Inc. owes directly or has guaranteed; non-recourse debt is owed by a subsidiary whose lenders can reach only that subsidiary's assets. DDTL (delayed-draw term loan): a loan drawn as GPUs are bought, held in a special-purpose subsidiary and secured on the GPUs and the customer contracts they serve. Vendor (OEM) financing: credit from equipment makers on the gear they sell. Deferred revenue: cash a customer paid before the service, owed back as service. Customer liabilities: prepayments on contracts that became cancellable, held as a current liability. RPO (remaining performance obligations): contracted revenue not yet recognized. Ladder: principal due by year. Treasury method: options and warrants counted as the net shares they would add after their exercise price buys some back at the market price. Realization: the share of the company's own renewal-revenue figure that we credit.

The two cases

The cases on either side of the price, in their own terms

The case for caution, as the filing extends it. Recourse debt rose $10.2 billion in one quarter to $41.6 billion, and the parent guarantees $13.1 billion of the delayed-draw loans called asset-level, so the claim ahead of the equity is larger than the label suggests. The fastest-growing recourse layer is vendor financing: the recourse part rose $2.7 billion in the quarter, vendor financing totals $7.7 billion, and it is short-dated (one to three years in the 2025 annual report; maturities from October 2026 to July 2030 in the Q3 table). About a fifth of the opening prepayment balance sat on contracts that became cancellable in a quarter. Leases signed but not begun rose to $47.4 billion from $35.5 billion, before a 453 MW site capped at $16.9 billion that sits outside that figure. The risk factors add covenants to the debt heading, drop June's sentence "we expect our cost of capital to continue declining", and add a factor for the October "negative publicity and market rumors." The chief executive set up a plan in August to sell up to about $500 million of stock by 31 March, subject to price thresholds. The parent's 9.75% 2031 notes yielded about 15.5% on 10 November (as reported, in our update), above the line at which we would call its market shut. And the value still rests on GPUs earning past their contracts, an economic life the filing cannot show.

The company's case, as the filing extends it. The contracted book is $132.3 billion of RPO, with Meta (up to about $21.0 billion) and OpenAI (up to about $6.5 billion) named as significant customers to come. No bond or convertible matures before June 2030; everything due in the next 15 months is scheduled amortization, and by our estimate about half of it is paid from customer contracts pledged to the loans, as $1.06 billion of the three oldest facilities was in Q3. The secured subsidiaries hold $25.9 billion of assets against their loans ($22.2 billion long-lived, $3.7 billion current), up from $14.5 billion at the end of 2025. Prepayments cost about 3% a year as booked, below any debt the company can issue. GPUs are depreciated over six years while the newest guaranteed loans are repaid within about five, so the fleet comes off contract owned outright, and more than $3 billion of the prepayments due in the next three months relate to re-contracting existing infrastructure. And prices are rising: by our arithmetic the 2026 vintage earns about $13.8 million a megawatt-year against $7.8 million on the legacy fleet, new contracts are quoted at $20–25 million, and an investment-grade facility is in process.

This addendum tests the first case's claims on recourse, vendor credit, the prepayment reclassification and the leases, and the second case's on maturities, amortization, pledged assets and the cost of prepayments, each against the filing. It cannot test whether the $1.9 billion is refunded or served, which the year-end report will show, or how much of the ~$9 billion arrives on schedule. Nor can it test renewal realization or GPU economic life; both are carried from the update, at 60% of the company's renewal figure and 6.1 years, and §6 shows what the price needs of each.

The method, rebuilt where it did not fit. Tested against CoreWeave's own capital structure, three parts of the update's method did not fit.

  • Each case's cost of equity follows its own leverage. CoreWeave's equity risk is mostly leverage: net debt is 4.8 times annualized EBITDA, and the cases diverge sharply from here. By 2030 the bull is at 1.5 times and falling, the base borrows for the build and ends 2036 with debt 1.7 times its equity, and the bear stays above 3 times. The update held 16% for every case to 2030. We now relever one asset beta, 1.25 (1.10 gives $55.33, 1.40 $44.17), at each case's own net debt over its own equity value each year: 16.6% in the first year at today's market leverage, then about 13–14% in the bull, 18–20% in the base and the 30% ceiling in the bear. The rate is the equity's expected return for its market risk, not the notes' promised yield, so the distress case is not a second charge for the same default risk. Effect: −$1.58.
  • The terminal value is timed like everything else. The model discounts each year's cash flow from mid-year, but the update valued the 2036 enterprise as if its first cash flow, the 2037 fleet's, came a full year later, and set the equity against net debt grown by a further year. At the end of 2036 the equity is the enterprise (its cash flows from mid-2037) less the debt owed then. The equity is levered at that point, so both errors fall on it several times over: +$4.25, the largest line in the bridge, and a correction of an inconsistency rather than a change of judgment.
  • The convertibles convert where they would. The update kept them as debt in every case. Settled case by case, the bull's own share value in the maturity years, $165.03–$188.10, is above the conversion prices ($97.85–$119.60) and below the capped calls' caps ($199.70–$230.00), so the capped calls absorb the conversion and the equity gives up nothing beyond repaying principal; the 2033 notes come closest, $188.10 against a $199.70 cap. Effect: none.

What we kept, and why.

  • Weights: distress 20%, bear 25%, base 35%, bull 20%. Credit-default swaps imply about a 60% chance of default over five years at risk-neutral odds; we judge real-world odds at a third to a half of that, 20–30% (our judgment, not a measured ratio). Distress sits at the bottom of that range because no bond falls due before June 2030 and the 10-Q shows $9.8 billion of liquidity (cash, marketable securities and $5.7 billion undrawn under its facilities) with ~$9 billion of prepayments due; against that, the parent guarantees $13.1 billion of the asset-level loans and about a fifth of the opening prepayments became cancellable. §7 tests the 20% against the parent's own runway. The bull's 20% rests on signed price increases, renewals above original prices and an investment-grade facility in process, none of it yet in reported revenue; it carries 59% of the value on a fifth of the weight and credits 100% of the company's renewal figure, and at a bull weight of 15% the value meets the price.
  • Base pricing: $18 million a megawatt-year on new contracts, $16 million on backlog capacity. The step from the legacy fleet's $7.8 million to the 2026 vintage's $13.8 million came with one GPU generation and part of this year's price rises; the capacity arriving in 2027–28 is the next generation, and new contracts are quoted at $20–25 million. So $13.8 million is the floor of the evidence and the quotes its ceiling; at the quotes' low end, $20 million on new contracts and $18 million on backlog, the value would be $57.37.
  • Option value: 20% within-case volatility on the 2036 equity, a mature contracted lessor's level. The company's enterprise is near 40% today, but the cases already carry the spread between outcomes (no option value gives $45.75; today's level throughout, $55.60).
  • The tails. The bear's $4.15 is all terminal value: its enterprise ends 2036 below its debt, so its equity is only the option on a recovery, worth nothing with no volatility, $3.42 at 2% terminal growth and $5.16 at 4%, with its rate at the 30% ceiling in every year after the first. The distress case restructures in 2027, so its equity is worth zero from then and has no terminal value.
  • Growth beyond the contracted 4.6 GW continues while a new site covers its financing, which is how management says it underwrites; ending it in any year moves the base only between $51.61 and $54.89.

The filing confirms the RPO schedule the update took from the deck: $132.3 billion, 36% within 24 months, 38% in months 25–48 and the rest by month 86. The contracted-book floor, which values only signed contracts after the debt and the 2027 build, moves with the same inputs: −$33.32 a share on the signed book alone (from −$29.30), $3.21 with the paid-off fleet and one renewal on new GPUs (from $7.68), $16.52 at the company's full renewal figure, and −$55.71 with half of the pending lease payments stranded, now on $47.4 billion rather than $35.5 billion.

The prepayments

The prepayments: what is fixed, what waits, what it costs

The ~$9 billion. On the call of 10 November the company said that "in the next three months alone, we will collect approximately $9 billion in prepayments and deposits from signed customer contracts", of which "more than $3 billion" relates to "recontracting existing cloud infrastructure". The 10-Q does not split the figure by payment terms, so how much is fixed in amount and date cannot be read from it; what it fixes is the boundary. The $3 billion-plus for installed GPUs does not wait on construction or power. The call does not say what the other ~$6 billion is for; if it is for capacity still to be delivered, it waits: committed contracts "typically include a prepayment from our customers prior to them receiving access to our services", RPO is reduced for "amounts that may not be recognized as revenue due to delivery delays", and Q3 showed that prepaid contracts can become cancellable. So at most about $6 billion of the $9 billion depends on delivery or power. Prepayments averaged 15–25% of contract value across active contracts at the end of 2023, 2024 and 2025; this year the company says it collected them on 70% of deals, some covering up to 100% of contract value.

What it costs. The company books interest on prepayments it judges to contain a financing component: $204 million of non-cash interest in the nine months, about 3.0% a year on the average deferred-revenue balance of $9.1 billion. That is the accounting cost, set when each contract is signed. The economic cost is the price the company gives up for cash up front, which it says it weighs "against the cost of financing" and does not disclose. In our cost of capital we charge prepayments 9%, the secured rate they replace; at the booked 3% the weighted cost of capital would be about 11.9% rather than 12.7%. That rate is the hurdle for the return on capital, not the discount rate in our equity model, so the choice changes the test, not the value.

The $1.9 billion. The filing says $1.9 billion of "the deferred revenue balance from the beginning of the period was reclassified from deferred revenue to customer liabilities, which are included in other current liabilities, as contracts became cancellable during the quarter." It does not say which period: the amount is 20% of the $9.7 billion at 30 June and 23% of the $8.2 billion at 31 December; "during the quarter" points to June, the base we use. Customer liabilities were $137 million at the end of 2025. The filing gives no cause, names no customer and states no refund terms. The classification itself says two things: the amounts are no longer service owed under a binding contract, and they are current, so they could leave within a year. A delivery milestone that gave customers a right to cancel and a renegotiation lead to different places, and the year-end report will separate them: a balance that returns to deferred revenue or becomes revenue was served; one that is paid out was refunded. Our value takes either path at about the same cost; the events in §8 read it.

The order

Who is paid first

Who holds the contracts, and the prepayments. CoreWeave, Inc. signs most customer contracts. Under DDTL 4.0 it assigned the contract to the borrowing subsidiary; under DDTL 5.0 one contract was assigned and the other signed by the subsidiary; DDTL 5.5's five contracts name CoreWeave, Inc., and whether they were assigned is redacted. None of the three credit agreements mentions customer prepayments, deposits or refunds. The documents therefore do not settle which entity holds a prepayment or would pay a refund: on an assigned contract a refund would compete with that subsidiary's lenders for its cash, and on a contract still with the parent it would come out of the parent's own cash.

Inside each subsidiary. The delayed-draw loans are borrowed by bankruptcy-remote subsidiaries "formed to hold the financed infrastructure and the related customer contracts", each secured by a first-priority pledge of its equity and substantially all of its assets: the GPUs, the contracts, the receivables and the reserve cash ($22.2 billion of long-lived and $3.7 billion of current assets at 30 September, which "may be used only to settle the obligations of those entities"). If a customer defaults, the lenders to that subsidiary have first claim on its contract cash and its GPUs. Cash runs down a fixed waterfall (operating costs, interest, principal, reserves, a cash trap if one is running) before anything reaches the parent. DDTL 4.0 tests coverage of debt service at 1.15 times, DDTL 5.0 and 5.5 at 1.35 times; DDTL 5.5 also sweeps all excess cash to its lenders until the loan is down to $1.3 billion. In all three, a customer terminating its contract in full is an event of default with no cure by replacement, and under DDTL 4.0 a site delivered late forces an early repayment.

Guarantees, and the two bridges. CoreWeave, Inc. guarantees $13.1 billion of the loans in full; for DDTL 4.0's $4.6 billion it answers only for bad acts: fraud, misappropriating collateral, wilful misconduct that materially damages the project, or a voluntary bankruptcy filing. The four indentures we read (the 9.75% 2031 and 9.625% 2032 notes and the 2032 and 2033 convertibles) fence the financing subsidiaries off: they are excluded from the significant subsidiaries whose defaults count. Two bridges cross that fence, both at the greater of $200 million and 5% of trailing EBITDA (about $291 million on reported figures).

  • The guarantees. The senior notes cross-accelerate on accelerated debt "the payment of which is Guaranteed by the Company", so an acceleration of DDTL 5.0, 5.5 or any other guaranteed loan reaches the notes directly, and the notes' acceleration reaches the convertibles. Guaranteed debt is the parent's debt, so this is no surprise.
  • The revolver, which reaches further. Since its November 2025 amendment it treats every subsidiary as a restricted subsidiary, so a default on any subsidiary's debt above the threshold, DDTL 4.0's included, is a revolver event of default; an accelerated revolver is parent debt, and the notes and convertibles cross-accelerate on it. A default on more than the greater of $200 million and 20% of EBITDA of drawn revolver debt (about $1.16 billion on reported figures, letters of credit excluded, against $1.2 billion drawn; more on the covenant's larger EBITDA, so likely above today's drawings) is also a trigger event under the DDTL 5.0 and 5.5 guarantees, giving those lenders remedies against the parent.

Only the revolver bridge can be cut: repaying it removes the one path by which non-recourse DDTL 4.0 could reach the group.

Covenants. The revolver tests net leverage at 6.0 times and contracted revenue at $1 billion each quarter; nothing tests the parent's liquidity. The 10-Q says only that the company was in compliance with all covenants, without the ratio. On reported trailing adjusted EBITDA of $5.8 billion (the last four quarterly releases), net debt is about 7.5 times, so compliance points to the covenant's EBITDA running well above reported: its definition lets the company add, at its option, the projected run-rate profit of contracts signed within the past 24 months (and the debt needed to serve them). A terminated contract loses its add-back, so a customer failure tests the subsidiary and the revolver covenant at once.

Do prepayments net down the loans? The agreements describe no netting. DDTL 4.0's draws are subject to "debt-sizing limitations tied to the purchase price of eligible assets" and "projected debt service coverage"; DDTL 5.0 and 5.5 are sized on the depreciable cost of the GPU servers, at 71% and 70% of it. A contract partly paid up front leaves less future cash to cover debt service, so on a coverage test it supports a smaller loan; that is our reading of the sizing terms, not a figure the filing gives.

Of the increase, $5.4 billion is parent-level borrowing (the $4.2 billion of 2033 convertibles and $1.2 billion drawn on the revolver), $2.7 billion is vendor financing classed as recourse to the parent, and $2.25 billion is guaranteed delayed-draw loans, net. The asset-level model is real but narrower than its name: of $17.6 billion of delayed-draw loans, $4.6 billion is non-recourse. Non-recourse debt rose a further $1.6 billion net: DDTL 4.0 rose $1.7 billion, offset by other movements. The risk factors repeat June's warning that "the majority of the debt under our Credit Facilities bears interest at variable rates, the majority of which is unhedged", now prefaced by "Market interest rates have increased". The filing puts the effect of a 100-basis-point move at about $41 million of interest a quarter, which we read as roughly $16 billion of floating-rate debt; it does not say whether that is before or after its $6.8 billion of swaps.

The next 15 months. $13.4 billion of principal falls due from October 2026 through December 2027 ($10.6 billion within twelve months of 30 September). None of it is a bond: the first note matures in June 2030 ($2.0 billion) and the revolver ($1.2 billion drawn) in November 2029. The filing does not split the ladder by facility, so we estimate it. The three oldest delayed-draw loans repaid $1.06 billion in Q3 with no new draws, $5.3 billion over five quarters at that pace. DDTL 5.0 and 5.5 begin monthly amortization in November 2026 and February 2027, and DDTL 4.0 by July 2027 at the latest (earlier at sites that missed delivery dates): about $1.7 billion through December 2027 on level payments. That puts contract-funded amortization at about $7.0 billion. The residual, about $6.4 billion, we attribute to vendor financing ($7.7 billion outstanding), though it also holds any DDTL 3.0 amortization and the $195 million Magnetar loan. The first is paid from the contracts pledged to it; the second from general cash flow, or rolled into new facilities. Our model refinances every asset-level maturity at the new-money rate, so the split moves timing, not value.

The leases

The leases and commitments still to land

Leases signed but not yet begun total $47.4 billion of undiscounted payments, from $35.5 billion at 30 June, starting between 2026 and 2029 for seven to seventeen years. Outside that figure: a 453 MW single-site lease with rent capped at $16.9 billion over sixteen years, and 354 MW at sites where rent follows construction cost. Other commitments: $2.1 billion of data-center construction, about $4 billion of purchase commitments, $1.0 billion of unfunded commitments to data-center partnerships, up to $120 million for the New Jersey joint venture plus a $95 million guarantee, and $0.2–0.8 billion of equipment the company must install in leased sites.

What reaches the balance sheet. Construction and purchase commitments become property, paid in cash or vendor credit, and are inside our capex. The leases arrive as liabilities as each site is handed over. Discounted the way the commenced leases are (their liability is 57% of their undiscounted payments, at a 10% average rate over twelve years), the $47.4 billion is about $26.9 billion of liability. Spread over the contracted megawatts not yet active, outside the two sites above, that is an estimated $12.8 million a megawatt, close to the lease assets added per megawatt in 2026 (more than 850 MW, so that figure is an upper bound). Treating every megawatt added on our base path for active power as leased, about $10 billion arrives by September 2027 and $16 billion by March 2028, taking lease liabilities from $19.3 billion toward $30–35 billion; these are estimates, because the filing does not say when each lease begins.

Is undelivered-capacity prepayment debt? Only the part a customer can take back. The $1.9 billion of customer liabilities sits on contracts that became cancellable, so the cash may have to be returned; we count it as debt. Deferred revenue is settled by delivering service, at the cost of running the capacity, not by repaying principal; counting all of it as debt treats every customer as able to walk away. Q3 says that is not hypothetical for a fifth of a quarter's balance, so we show it as a stress line rather than ignore it. Our cost of capital already charges the full $10.0 billion as capital.

The lives and the cost

Lives, realization and what the capital costs

Book life against loan life. GPUs are depreciated over six years (2025 annual report); the 10-Q reports no change to its accounting policies and does not restate the life. The newest guaranteed loans close and finish within about five years: DDTL 5.0 from May 2026 to May 2031, DDTL 5.5 from August 2026 to July 2031. Their lenders are repaid with roughly a year of book life left, and whatever the GPUs earn after that belongs to the equity: this is the fleet the company says comes off contract "fully paid", and why the renewal question matters more than the depreciation schedule. DDTL 4.0 runs six years, March 2026 to March 2032, about as long as the book life; vendor credit runs one to three years.

Realization. We credit 60% of the company's renewal figure (about $10 billion a year of revenue from GPUs coming off contract in 2027–28) in the base, 35% in the bear, 20% in distress and 100% in the bull. At $44.91, our weighted value reaches the price with base renewals realized at 24%. The filing reports no renewal revenue; the evidence remains the call's "more than $3 billion" of prepayments for re-contracted capacity.

The cost of capital. On its books, CoreWeave pays 7.9% on its debt (Q3 contractual interest and issue-cost amortization over average principal, matching the company's 7.9%) and about 3.0% on prepayments, about 7.0% on the $50.6 billion the two fund together. At the margin it pays more: the next secured facility is guided at 7–7.5%, the 9.75% 2031 notes yielded about 15.5% on 10 November (as reported, in our update), and the equity's own rate starts at 16.6%. Weighted at market values, that is about the 12.7% our update used. The gap between the 7.0% the company books and the 12.7% the market charges is the cost of the equity cushion under the cheap layers, and why the business must earn well above its booked cost of debt to create value.

What the price requires, at $44.91 and our weighted value, holding everything else: base new contracts at $17.2 million a megawatt a year (we assume $18 million); or an asset beta of 1.38 in every case (we use 1.25); or base new asset-level money at 10.5% (we assume 8%, the company guides 7–7.5%); or base renewals at 24% of the company's figure (we credit 60%); or a distress weight of 27% (ours 20%), or a bull weight of 15% (ours 20%). The pricing and asset-beta steps are small; the funding-rate, renewal and weight steps are larger, though the price's distress weight lies inside our own 20–30% range. That is what a value 10% above the price means.

The distress path

The distress path: how a funding problem becomes a default

This is a mechanism, not a new valuation case: it traces how the distress case in our weights would happen, and when. The engine is a quarter-by-quarter liquidity runway for CoreWeave, Inc. alone. The subsidiaries' cash is fenced off; only what their waterfalls release reaches the parent, less DDTL 5.5's share, which it sweeps to its lenders from early 2027 until its loan is down to $1.3 billion. From its cash, revolver headroom, the ATM, the prepayments and those distributions, the parent pays coupons, vendor credit, refunds, guarantee calls, rent, joint-venture funding and its share of each build: the part of capex the delayed-draw loans do not lend. That share is the lever. DDTL 4.0 lends 90% of capex, DDTL 5.0 and 5.5 about 70%, and more than three-quarters of the remaining committed 2027 build is tied to investment-grade and enterprise customers, so we take 85%. Vendor credit is assumed to roll, new credit funding the next equipment as each vendor loan amortizes, as the recourse balance's rise in Q3 shows; if only half of the ~$6.4 billion due through 2027 rolls, the parent needs $4.4 billion beyond the ATM by the end of 2027, and if none does, $7.6 billion. With the parent's market shut, the ATM sells a third below the 19 November price.

The base path. Parent liquidity is about $12.2 billion at the end of 2026, the ~$9 billion of prepayments included, then $7.1 billion at June 2027 and $3.7 billion at September as the build is paid for. The parent sells the ATM and needs about $1.2 billion more by the end of 2027, about $2.8 billion of parent capital in 2027, inside the company's "less than $5 billion", and $6.1 billion beyond the ATM by the end of 2028. At a 90% advance rate throughout, it needs nothing new through 2027; at 70%, $6.9 billion. The path credits all of the prepayments to the parent, which signs most contracts; if half of them land in the subsidiaries' accounts instead, the parent needs $5.7 billion beyond the ATM by the end of 2027, above the company's figure. So our runway matches the company's figure only if most of that cash reaches the parent and vendor credit keeps rolling; the year-end report will show where the cash went.

Five shock channels, each on its own. With the parent able to raise capital: (a) a quarter of the 2027–28 build slips and the $1.9 billion already cancellable is refunded, so parent capital needed by the end of 2027 rises to $2.0 billion; (b) a customer that is 30% of revenue stops paying from the second quarter of 2027, its subsidiaries trap their cash and the guaranteed loans behind it call the parent for the shortfall: $5.7 billion by the end of 2027 and $17.7 billion by the end of 2028; (c) both funding markets shut from January (no new delayed-draw facilities, vendor credit not rolled, no parent raise): the parent reaches its buffer in the first quarter of 2027 and runs out in the third; (d) renewals in the 2027–28 roll-off realize 20% of the company's figure rather than 60%: $2.6 billion and $11.4 billion; (e) floating rates rise 2 points on the unhedged debt: $1.6 billion and $6.8 billion. Only $1.9 billion of prepayments is classified as cancellable today; the ~$10 billion of deferred revenue becomes refundable only through cancellability, so the refunds in these paths are a conditional exposure, not a forecast.

The turning point. The loop starts when the parent cannot pay its share of a build: deliveries slip, slipping deliveries make contracts cancellable, refunds drain the parent, and the next build is harder to pay for. With the loan market open but the parent's own market shut, the parent reaches its buffer of about $2.5 billion in the fourth quarter of 2027, cuts capex from there and runs out of cash in the second quarter of 2028. With both markets shut and a large customer failing, it reaches the buffer in the first quarter of 2027 and runs out in the second. The cross-default amounts then decide how far it spreads: above $35 million inside DDTL 5.0 and 5.5 and $100 million inside DDTL 4.0, and at about $291 million across the two bridges in §3 to the notes and convertibles.

The brakes. The sequence has real brakes, and they are why the base path holds. About $9 billion of prepayments arrives in the next three months, more than the parent's need through 2027 if all of it reaches the parent. No bond or convertible matures before June 2030, so the parent's only hard dates are coupons. The parent can cut the revolver bridge by repaying the $1.2 billion drawn and cash-collateralizing the $0.5 billion of letters of credit, against $4.1 billion of unrestricted cash at 30 September before the prepayments arrive; that isolates non-recourse DDTL 4.0, though not the guaranteed loans, which are the parent's own debt in all but name. A note acceleration is annulled if the underlying default is cured within 30 days. The capped calls limit dilution from conversion, though they add no cash.

Exhibit 5 — The distress sequence, its two inflection points and the brakes

How to read it. Read the eight boxes left to right along the top row and back along the bottom: each is a step that the one before it can set off, with the date or threshold that marks it. The two gold boxes are the inflection points, where a funding problem becomes a delivery problem (3) and a delivery problem becomes a default (6); the dashed red curve is the loop, refunds draining the parent back at step 2. The six boxes underneath are the brakes that slow or stop the sequence. It shows that within 2027 no single step makes a default: it takes a funding failure and a delivery failure together, carried to the group by the guarantees or the revolver; by mid-2028 the parent's own market staying shut is enough.

What it says about the 20% distress weight. It supports 20%, on one condition. In the base the parent needs about $2.8 billion of parent capital in 2027, which the ATM and a modest raise cover, so within 2027 a default needs two failures together (the parent's market and the loan market shut, or a financed contract terminated while the parent is short) and a bridge to carry it to the group. By mid-2028 one failure is enough: if the parent's own market stays shut while the loan market stays open, it reaches its buffer at the end of 2027 and runs out in the second quarter of 2028. That market is open today at a price: the company placed $4.2 billion of 2033 convertibles in the quarter, but its 9.75% 2031 notes yielded about 15.5% on 10 November (as reported, in our update), past the 15% line our events table reads as shut. So 20% rather than 25–30% rests on the parent raising at a workable price by mid-2027, and the test arrives within 2027 if half of the prepayments land in the subsidiaries or only half of the vendor credit due through 2027 rolls. The notes above 15% at 30 June 2027 take the weight to 30%; below 11%, to 10%. The bridges raise how far a default spreads, which the distress case already takes to zero for the equity; they do not raise how likely the first failure is.

The rest of the filing

What else the filing says

  • Customers. Two customers were 30% and 24% of Q3 revenue; no other reached 10%, and four hold 77% of receivables. The filing names neither; OpenAI (up to about $6.5 billion) and Meta (up to about $21.0 billion) are named as expected significant customers. If the 24% customer is the one that was 67% a year earlier, which July's note took to be Microsoft (the filing warns its customer letters may not denote the same customers across periods), it bought about the same dollars ($0.88 billion against $0.91 billion), so its share fell by dilution rather than loss.
  • Risk factors, against June. Added: restrictive covenants in the debt heading; storage and memory suppliers alongside GPUs; local community opposition as a permitting delay; the October "market rumors" factor; open-weight models as a risk to its business and pricing models; the ATM program's collared forwards. Expanded: New York's July moratorium, now described as pausing, for up to a year, discretionary state environmental permits for data centers of 50 MW or more; the warning that the convertibles become current liabilities if their conversion trigger is met, extended to the 2033 notes. Dropped: June's expectation that the cost of capital would keep falling through "asset-backed securitizations and rated parent-level debt."
  • Insiders and shares. The chief executive's plan covers up to about $500 million of sales from 14 November 2026 to 31 March 2027, subject to price thresholds. No shares were sold under the ATM through 30 September. The filing has no subsequent-events note and reports no financing after 30 September.
  • Controls and litigation. The material weaknesses in IT controls, segregation of duties and accounting staff continue. The securities class action is pending and the derivative suits are stayed. The company bought $275 million of surety bonds in August for an electric-service agreement ($154 million of cash collateral). No change to the six-year depreciable life is reported.

What would change our view

The tripwires in our update stand, with their reading rules; this filing moves none of them, and §8 adds six, each with a favorable reading as specific as its adverse one. We would move toward the bull case on the next delayed-draw loan at or below 7.5% with an investment-grade rating, deferred revenue of $20 billion or more at the end of 2026 with customer liabilities back below $0.5 billion, annualized renewal revenue from the 2027 roll-off reaching $4 billion, or the unsecured notes below 11% by mid-2027. We would move toward the distress case on a delayed-draw loan priced above 9% or not at all, customer liabilities above $3 billion or a refund of the $1.9 billion disclosed in the year-end report, renewal revenue below $1.2 billion annualized, the depreciable life cut to five years or less, or the notes above 15% at mid-2027. At about 15.5% on 10 November (as reported, the last reading in our update) the notes are already past that line: the move toward distress needs the level to hold to mid-2027, the move toward the bull case a fall of four and a half points. The notes above 15% at mid-2027, or a loss in the first half of 2028, take the distress weight to 30% and fair value to $39.46; the notes below 11%, or adjusted net income positive in 2027, take it to 10% and fair value to $59.64.
