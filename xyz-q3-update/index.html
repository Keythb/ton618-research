Block (XYZ) — The Margin Arrived, and So Did the Bill

Block now guides a 32% adjusted operating margin for 2027, above July's best case, and the legal charge July called one-time has become a $764 million accrual for a Justice Department anti-money-laundering case. Its credit gauges point opposite ways because the loans got longer. We value the stock at $91.17, from $83 in July, against $76.00 at the close before the release; the next session closed at $87.63.

TON618 Capital Research · As of 3 November 2026 · Download PDF · Download model

In July we called Block the most misread name in the crypto complex and hung the call on one question: was the tripled loss line timing or trouble? Four months on, three things have moved. The margin arrived early: the company now expects to earn in 2027 the margin on gross profit that July thought possible only in its best case. The bill arrived too: the litigation charge July set aside as one-time was the first accrual for a federal anti-money-laundering case, now $764 million and not yet final or paid. And the credit gauges now disagree, for a reason that turns out to be the length of a loan. This note sorts out how much of each is real, then values the business by its parts.

The case for the price

The case for $76, in the skeptic's terms

The case against paying more than $76.00 is not that Block is a bad business. It is that the margin story is told in a figure that excludes the costs that keep appearing, on a lending engine whose losses are rising, in a stock that moves more than twice as much as the market.

  1. The adjusted margin leaves out a bill that comes every year. Contingencies, restructuring and other charges were 3.2% of gross profit in 2023, 3.4% in 2024 and 1.6% in 2025. In 2026 they are 4.2% even after setting aside the DOJ accrual and the layoffs. The DOJ matter is not a stray lawsuit: the charge it will defer is that Block "failed to maintain an effective anti-money laundering program," which is a failure of how Cash App was run. A business whose adjusted figure has left out charges in every year it has reported it should be valued on what it keeps, not on the 32%.
  2. Growth is coming from the riskiest products. Financial solutions, mostly Cash App Borrow, is 41% of gross profit and grew 32%. The company moved Borrow from four-week loans at a 5% fee to six-week loans at 7.5%, with a target loss of 4.2% of each loan against 2.9% before, and Afterpay Post-Purchase targets 4.3%. The 60–89-day delinquency rate rose this quarter, net write-offs over originations have risen three quarters running, and the targets are portfolio averages for customers past their first month, not results.
  3. The market prices it as a high-risk equity. Block's 60-month beta to the S&P 500 is 2.56, with a standard error of 0.34, and its 36-month beta is 2.47. Blended toward peers, the 60-month read is 2.29, a 15.3% cost of equity, at which our own model gives $83.02. Nor need the rate fall as the business matures: financial solutions, mostly lending, is now 41% of gross profit, the newer loan products are young. Held at our own 13.0% for every year, the model gives $74.09.
  4. The comparable trades at eleven times. PayPal, the mature version of this business, traded at 10.8 times its 2025 earnings at the same close. Applied to our base case's 2027 after-tax operating profit, PayPal's multiple gives Block $59.78. The $76.00 close was 14.7 times that profit and 18.3 times the company's own adjusted earnings guide for 2026.
  5. Growth is slowing where it matters. Gross profit growth is guided to +15% in the fourth quarter against +22% in the third. Primary banking actives, the customers who run their pay through Cash App, were 9.7 million, 9.4 million and 9.4 million over the last three quarters; inflows per transacting active grew 6% (per primary banking active, 8%).
  6. The cash is not being returned. Block bought back $636 million of stock in the first quarter, about $65 million in the second and none in the third, with $7.9 billion of cash. Whatever the reason, holders are not getting the buyback July's 585 million forward shares assumed.

This note tests the first four claims and the fifth's arithmetic, and sizes and tracks the sixth (§3, §7). It sets out what the margin is after the recurring charges (§2), what the DOJ case costs and whether the other charges recur (§3), what the credit ratios show once loan length and product mix are counted (§4), and what the beta and the multiples imply (§5). It cannot test, inside a ten-year model, whether regulators will limit the fees on short-term consumer loans, what conditions a final DPA will impose beyond the payment, or when the next recession reaches Cash App's customers. Our bear case carries a credit turn in 2027 at $47.12; it does not carry a rule change that reprices Borrow.

The print

The quarter, and why GAAP and adjusted profit differ

Gross profit was $3.235 billion, up 22% and 3.4% above the $3.13 billion guided in August. Cash App's was $2.07 billion (+27%) and Square's $1.16 billion (+14%). By type, commerce enablement, the fees on Square payments, Cash App Card and buy-now-pay-later, was $1.851 billion and accelerated to +19% from +15% and +18%; financial solutions, mostly Borrow, was $1.332 billion and slowed to +32% from +55% and +43%; bitcoin was $52 million, down 50% after Block cut a fee on some bitcoin purchases.

Operating income was $428 million, 13% of gross profit and up 5%; adjusted operating income was $923 million, 29% and up 92%. Credit costs, transaction, loan and consumer receivable losses, were $602 million, up 66%, or 18.6% of gross profit.

The guide was raised: fourth-quarter gross profit of $3.315 billion (+15%) and adjusted operating income of $1.005 billion (30%); for 2026, $12.626 billion (+22%), $3.519 billion (28%) and adjusted earnings of $4.15 a share (+75%). For 2027 the company gave a preliminary outlook: gross profit up at least 15% to $14.519 billion and adjusted operating income up 30% to $4.575 billion, about 32% of gross profit. Management said "our 2027 growth guidance does not rely on those initiatives," meaning, on the call, Cash App Score, Builderlab and Squaremates; the letter says the same of Neighborhoods and AI sold to other companies. We give them no value either.

The margin

The margin: what 32% is, and how much of it lasts

Bridging the definitions first. July's base case put the normal margin at 20% of gross profit, which is what the adjusted figure was in 2025 ($2.08 billion on $10.36 billion). The two measures are the same: both exclude the one-time charges and both carry stock pay. So the guide and July's base case can be compared directly, and the guide is twelve points higher, three years earlier than July's 24% best case.

Exhibit 2 — The margin three ways, and the 2027 guide

How to read it. Operating margin as a percent of gross profit by quarter, Q3 2025 to Q3 2026. Gold is the company's adjusted operating income; black is the same figure less the contingencies, restructuring and other charges it excludes, adding back only the DOJ accrual and the February layoffs; grey is GAAP operating income. The gold dash at the right is the 2027 guide, and the dotted line July's 20% base case. The one thing it shows: on the company's figure the margin has risen every quarter; once the charges that recur are taken out, it has risen far less, and it fell in the third quarter.

Where it came from. Operating costs outside credit losses, which we can measure as non-GAAP operating expenses less transaction, loan and consumer receivable losses, were $1,832 million in the third quarter of 2025 and $1,723 million this quarter, down 6% while gross profit grew 22%. In February Block cut more than 40% of staff, in a plan that concluded in the second quarter, and expects $800–900 million of annual savings. Those costs have held flat since: $1,694 million, $1,729 million and $1,723 million in the three quarters of 2026. Some is regrowing in other lines: software and cloud fees rose $94 million in the quarter, partly AI tools, and sales and marketing grew 15%. The 2027 outlook implies that 56% of each added dollar of gross profit reaches adjusted operating income, above the 50% the company set at its investor day for 2028. That is a fixed-cost business growing into a smaller cost base, and it can run for a few years. It cannot run forever, and the company says it will "reinvest profit upside" in Neighborhoods and AI.

The fourth quarter's +15%. It is mostly the comparison. Third-quarter growth of 22% came on 18% a year earlier, a two-year total of 40 points; the fourth quarter's 15% comes on 24%, 39 points. The fourth quarter laps the launch of Cash App Green, and Borrow limits were held flat through the migration. Block beat its third-quarter guide by 3.4% and its adjusted operating income guide by 5.5%. We read the +15% as a cautious guide on a hard comparison, not a slowdown in the business, and our base case grows gross profit 15.9% in 2027, a little above the guide.

Where it lands. We set the terminal margin from a mature peer, not from the guide. Block's gross profit is struck before credit losses; PayPal's revenue less transaction expense and transaction and credit losses, which we derive from its 10-K and call its transaction margin, is struck after them. On a matched basis, adjusted operating income over gross profit less losses was 35% in the third quarter and is about 34% on the 2026 guide. PayPal's GAAP operating income was 36.7%, 36.3% and 39.2% of its transaction margin in 2023–25, 41.4% in 2025 before restructuring, on $15.5 billion of transaction margin. Our base case lifts Block's adjusted margin to 34% in 2029–30, two points above the 2027 guide but below the 35–37% that the company's own 50%-plus incremental margins would give by 2029, because management says it will reinvest upside. It lands at 32% in 2036: 39% of gross profit less losses at this year's loss rate, and 37% after the recurring charges (§3), inside PayPal's range. The bear lands at 25% (31% matched) and the bull at 35% (43%), above PayPal's best year, which is the bull's claim that a company that cut 40% of its staff and automates its engineering can run leaner than PayPal does.

The bill

The bill: one matter, three times the size

July read the first quarter's operating loss as largely non-operating, and the $253.8 million legal accrual inside it as one-time. The filings since show what it was. The DOJ opened its inquiry after a short seller's report in March 2023, presented terms in March 2026, and in October reached an agreement in principle to resolve it through a three-year deferred prosecution agreement on a charge that Block "failed to maintain an effective anti-money laundering program in violation of the Bank Secrecy Act." The gross monetary component is about $964 million and the net payment about $764 million, after credit for earlier regulatory settlements. Block accrued $240 million in the first quarter, $526 million by the second and $764 million by the third. The agreement still needs final terms and court approval, and the 10-Q says losses beyond the accrual are reasonably possible. The SEC closed its parallel investigation in March without recommending action.

How to read it. The percent beside each black segment is that segment over the year's gross profit. The one thing it shows: the DOJ case is one matter, but the black segment has been there every year, at 1.6% to 4.2% of gross profit.

One matter, as July said, but three times the size, and not outside the business. The DOJ accounts for $764 million of the $981 million of accrued legal contingencies at September, against $173 million in December. A failure of an anti-money-laundering program is a failure of how Cash App onboarded and monitored customers, which is an operating failure even if the payment happens once. We score July's read as reversed in part (§6).

What else sits in the bill. The other $217 million of accrued contingencies is not itemized. Known matters: a securities class action covering 2020–24 purchasers, where the court denied Block's motion to dismiss in January and the accrual is "not material" but a larger loss is reasonably possible; derivative suits that the defendants agreed in October to settle, with $150 million paid by insurers to Block less a fee award; a multistate settlement in July for an immaterial amount; and $114 million of San Francisco gross-receipts tax already paid and carried as a deposit. Outside the DOJ accrual and the layoffs, contingencies, restructuring and other charges were $389 million in nine months, 4.2% of gross profit, against 1.6–3.4% in 2023–25.

What we do with it. The accrued claims are unpaid, so we deduct them from equity: $764 million for the DOJ and $217 million for the rest, with no tax shield on the DOJ payment, our assumption (the second-quarter 10-Q describes its litigation and regulatory accruals as non-deductible; the third is silent). We add $112 million for the insurers' payment, assuming a 25% fee award. If the DPA is finalized at the gross $964 million, fair value is $90.85; a further $500 million for the class action gives $90.36.

The charges that recur. Because they have run every year, the model deducts 3% of gross profit through 2029, the term of the DPA, falling to 2% from 2032. The step down is a judgment, not a measurement: 2026 carries the monitoring and remediation years of a federal case and an open class action, and once both are behind it we expect the charges to run between the 1.6% of 2025 and the 3.2–3.4% of 2023–24; 2% is our point in that range. Taking the adjusted figure at face value gives $98.03; 3% throughout, $88.77; 4% throughout, $85.72.

The buyback. Block repurchased 4.2 million shares in January, 5.5 million in February, 1.0 million in March and 0.9 million across April to June, and did not repurchase any shares in the third quarter, with $4.6 billion still authorized. It repaid $1.575 billion of notes in May and June and put $1.4 billion into growing its loan book over the year. The company gave no reason for the pause and no analyst asked. The taper began the month the DOJ presented its terms; we note the timing and draw no conclusion from it. We value the stock on 614.8 million diluted shares, the third quarter's count, with stock pay charged as an expense in the margin, so the count does not grow and no buyback is assumed. A buyback at fair value adds nothing; spending the $4.6 billion at $76.00 would lift fair value to $92.83. The gap between July's 585 million shares and 614.8 million is worth −$4.14 a share on July's own method.

The credit

The credit: two gauges, one longer loan

July set two tells: reserve coverage on consumer loans, and the 60–89-day delinquency rate. This quarter they point opposite ways. Coverage fell to 14.54% from 14.73%; 60–89 days rose to 4.13% from 3.90%, still below the 4.66% of March. The book grew 10.1% in the quarter, to $3.94 billion. Read alone, falling coverage on rising delinquency would mean reserves are not keeping up. The boring checks come first.

The book grew on longer loans, not more lending. Held-for-investment originations rose 1.5% in the quarter, and consumer lending originations as a whole 0.5%. Divide the book by the quarter's originations and the loan length rises from 29.0 days in June to 31.8 in September, as the six-week Borrow loans replaced four-week ones. A longer loan sits on the balance sheet longer, so the same lending makes a larger book and a lower coverage ratio. At June's loan length coverage would be 15.9%. Measured against what was lent, reserves rose from 4.09% of the quarter's originations in March to 4.70% in June and 5.03% in September.

Losses per dollar lent are rising by design. The company published its target economics by product this quarter.

The six-week loan charges 7.5% against 5%, targets a 4.2% loss against 2.9%, and earns 2.8% of each loan after losses and processing against 2.0%. The annualized return is that margin times the number of times the money turns over in a year, 2.8% × 365 / 28 = 37%, against 35% for the four-week loan, so most of the higher margin is the longer loan, not a better one. Afterpay Post-Purchase, which more than tripled, targets a 4.3% loss. As the mix moves to these products, losses per dollar lent should rise toward about 4%. Reserves per dollar lent and net write-offs over the prior quarter's originations (2.39%, 2.89% and 3.11%) have risen every quarter; provisions went from 3.14% to 3.57% of the quarter's originations and eased to 3.47% in the third. One caution on levels: the originations line covers all loans held for investment, including commercial and other loans, which are 22% of that book but, being longer loans, a much smaller share of what is lent; we estimate about 5%. The consumer ratios are therefore about 5% higher than shown, net write-offs about 3.3% rather than 3.1%, and we compare them with the targets as a trend first and a level second. 60–89 days over the prior quarter's originations ran 1.21%, 1.54%, 1.31% and 1.45%: up this quarter, below March.

Seasonality. The company's chart of Borrow returns by month of origination, for customers past their third month, shows the weakest loans are made in March to May, at roughly 22–25% annualized in 2025, and the strongest in September, October and January, at 36–41%. The 2026 cohorts with actual results, January to May, ran above the 2025 cohorts in every month but January. Borrow loans are written off at 90 days past due, so most third-quarter write-offs should come from loans made in the second quarter, the seasonally weakest; that is our inference, as the filings give no split by vintage. August and September are the season's best months, and the company projects them above 40%; those projections include its own underwriting model's forecasts, not results.

Our read. Nothing in the quarter shows losses running above what the products are priced for: net write-offs of about 3.1–3.3% of originations sit below the 4.2–4.3% targets of the products growing fastest (the targets are product loss rates for customers past their first month, so the comparison is approximate), and the company says variable profit margin was in line with a year ago and up modestly on the quarter. Nor does anything show they will not: the six-week loans have not been through a full season, and the targets are portfolio averages for customers past their first loan. The single reading that would settle it is net write-offs on consumer loans in the fourth quarter over third-quarter originations, in the 10-K in late February. At 3.2% or less the season's best loans are behaving as projected. At 4.0% or more they already sit near the ceiling of the new products' targets, and we would move the bear case's weight from 25% to 35% (fair value $84.84).

How long lending can compound. Borrow's average limit is $300 against a $1,500 maximum, and the company's six-week buy-now-pay-later products already go to $3,000; it "expect[s] to increase average limits over the next several years." A 50% rise in the average limit over four years is about 10% a year; with low-single-digit customer growth and new products that gives 11–15% a year. We set a four-year stage, 2027–30, at 15.5%, 13%, 11% and 9% in the base case, then a fade to 4% by 2036. Square Loans grew 27% a year for a decade at its 4% loss target, the company's own base rate, which we do not assume repeats. The funding has changed too: warehouse lines are 66% drawn ($1.54 billion of $2.35 billion, drawn plus available), and Square Financial Services began issuing brokered certificates of deposit this quarter, $381 million at 4.09%, beside $521 million of customer deposits. We treat those lines and deposits as operating funding of the loan book and charge the rest of its growth to equity: of the $7.49 billion lending book, $2.45 billion is funded that way and 67% by Block itself. The base case charges 60% of each year's book growth to equity.

The value

What it is worth

The method. July multiplied 2027 earnings by 15, 21 or 26. We value the business by its parts instead, because three things that matter now sit outside one multiple: the loan book that grows with lending and needs equity, the charges the adjusted figure leaves out, and a balance sheet that now carries a $764 million unpaid claim. We discount after-tax operating profit less the equity that loan growth needs, by scenario, mid-year from 3 November 2026, with the fourth quarter of 2026 as a stub, stock pay expensed, and the balance-sheet items below added once. We use the tech stack's method, not the financials stack's: the lending book is $7.49 billion against $22.9 billion of equity, the consumer loans in it turn over about every 32 days, and 57% of gross profit is commerce enablement, mostly fees on payments and cards. Block reports no regulatory capital ratio for the group, so the book's capital is charged as reinvestment in the cash flows rather than valued as a bank's equity.

Gross profit by line. Square grows with its payment volume; Cash App commerce with card and buy-now-pay-later attach; Cash App financial solutions with Borrow's limits and eligibility; bitcoin and other at a small rate. Each stage's length comes from what drives it.

Return on capital. We measure it on the line the model values: adjusted operating income less the recurring charges, taxed at 24%, over equity plus notes less cash and bitcoin. Customer funds are not capital: they sit outside cash and are matched by what Block owes customers. The loan book is, because Block funds most of it; the warehouse lines and deposits that fund the rest are operating funding, and their interest runs through operating costs. On that basis the return is 10.0% against a WACC of 12.1%, up from 6.4% a year ago: the regime is recovering.

What would change our view

We would move the bull path toward the base if the evidence tracks it: 2027 guided in February at a 33% adjusted margin or more with gross profit +15% or more; fourth-quarter net write-offs at or below 3.2% of third-quarter originations and the first quarter's 60–89 days below 1.6% of fourth-quarter originations; a final DPA at or below $764 million with no monitor; and buybacks resumed. That path is worth $124.30, and its inputs then become the base. The mirror moves it the other way: a 2027 margin guided below 30% or gross profit below +13%; fourth-quarter net write-offs at 4.0% or more, or first-quarter 60–89 days above 2.2% of originations; a DPA above the accrual or with a monitor, or none by May; charges outside the DOJ above 4% of 2026 gross profit. Any one of these moves its own input as §7 sets out; together they move the bear weight to 35% and the margin down two points, toward $85.08 and below, and the bear case is worth $47.12. The price is right, and we would say so, if the margin path sits about 5.0 points below ours in every year, which means a 2027 margin guided in the mid-to-high 20s rather than above 30%, or if Block's risk does not come down as the business matures: a cost of equity of 12.8% held in every year gives the price. The margin would show in February's guide. The risk would show in the 24-month beta at the August 2027 update, a window measured mostly at the new margins: above 2.0, the rate would stay at 13.0% through 2036, worth $74.09; below 1.6, the fall stands.

The call

The call

Block's margin arrived three years ahead of July's base case, and its bill turned out three times larger and closer to the core of the business than July read it. We value the stock at $91.17, from $83, against $76.00 before the results and $87.63 the day after. The value is a fixed-cost business growing into a smaller cost base, landed at PayPal's mature margin on a matched basis, less the charges that recur, the equity the loan book needs and the DOJ payment, discounted at 13% falling to 10.5% as the business matures. That fall is the judgment the call rests on: held at 13%, the value is $74.09, below $76.00. The price asks for gross profit growth of 5.7% a year, about a third of what the company guides for 2027. Credit is the condition: losses per dollar lent are rising toward the new products' targets by design, and the fourth-quarter write-offs on the season's best loans, reported in February, are the reading that would move our weights.
