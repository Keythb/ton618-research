Microsoft (MSFT) — The Life Change the 10-Q Leaves Out

Microsoft's September quarter grew Azure 48% and guided the next to 49–50%, while depreciation rose 56% against revenue up 19%. The 10-Q the July note said would confirm the move of building lives from 15 to 25 years does not mention it, though the lease note shifted the way that change predicts. We value Microsoft at $365.64, from $403: a higher cost of equity explains almost all of the fall, the quarter's operating news nets to about zero, and the method changes and the OpenAI stake roughly offset a halved earnings weight. The 28 October close of $531.01 is 45% above fair value.

TON618 Capital Research · As of 28 October 2026 · Download PDF · Download model

In July we valued Microsoft as the best-run machine in the AI buildout, with earnings that run ahead of the bill for building it: a server clock that spreads equipment cost over two to six years, and a new building clock, extended on the July call from 15 years to 25. The July note said it would check the second claim against the first quarterly filing of fiscal 2027. That filing arrived on 28 October with a 48% Azure quarter, $45.2 billion of capital spending, and no sentence about building lives. This note asks what the silence means, what the quarter did to the value of the business, and what $531.01 now requires.

Terms used throughout, defined once. Capex: capital expenditures including finance leases, the basis on which Microsoft guides; where a figure is cash only, it says so. Finance and operating leases: a finance lease is treated like a purchase on borrowed money (the asset sits in property and equipment and counts in Microsoft's capex); an operating lease is treated like rent (it stays out of capex and its cost runs through operating expenses). Free cash flow (FCF): operating cash flow less cash additions to property and equipment. FCFE conversion: free cash flow to equity, after finance-lease principal, as a share of net income, so it runs below free cash flow over net income; share-based compensation stays an expense. Re-mark: the house restatement of server depreciation onto a three-year life (the house clock) against Microsoft's two-to-six-year range; a model estimate, not an accounting restatement, and Microsoft's reported figures comply with GAAP. The 84% terminal conversion is the share of net income left as free cash flow once spending settles at the level that clock implies. Price-to-earnings leg: 24 times re-marked trailing earnings, weighted 20% in the base case. Cost of equity: the return a shareholder requires, here the ten-year Treasury yield plus beta (the stock's measured sensitivity to the market, over five years of monthly returns and adjusted a third of the way toward 1.0) times a 4.5% equity risk premium, the same rule at every update. Remaining performance obligation (RPO): contracted revenue not yet recognized. Growth stages: periods of the forecast, each lasting as long as the thing that drives it. Fiscal calendar: Microsoft's year ends 30 June; FQ2-27 is the December 2026 quarter. In quotations, "3P" is third-party and "H2" the second fiscal half.

The case for the price

The case for the price, in the company's terms

Microsoft has a strong case. Azure revenue was $33.2 billion, up 48%, in the first quarterly report to give Azure in dollars, and it beat the 44–45% the September outlook implied even though, in the CFO's words, "some third-party capacity deliveries we expected in Q1 were delayed." The guide for the December quarter is 49–50% at constant currency. Commercial RPO is $677 billion, up 73%, and the part beyond the next twelve months grew 98%. On the call the CFO said "Customer demand continues to exceed available supply," and, on the efficiency work, "each dollar of capex has the potential to support more revenue and higher returns over time." Operating margin rose to 49.8% from 48.9% while depreciation grew 56%: the company absorbed the bill rather than passed it through. Return on invested capital is 35.1% against a 9.8% cost of equity. And Microsoft owns about 24% of OpenAI, worth about $27.46 a share before tax at the last closed round, which July's cash-flow model did not value separately. Free cash flow stayed positive at $18.6 billion while cash capital spending doubled.

On the clock, the evidence that favors the company is real. GPU rental markets still price the 2020-vintage NVIDIA A100 at $1.54 an hour (Silicon Data's A100 rental index for neo-cloud providers and GPU marketplaces, 3 October 2026), six years after launch, which is what a long economic life looks like. If servers earn for five years or more, the house re-mark overstates the expense. The cost of equity is a measured number, not a law: raw beta is 1.086 (1.058 after the house adjustment) with a standard error of 0.17; one standard error lower, the fair value would be $393, and at July's 8.9% cost of equity, $416. At $531.01 the stock trades at 27.7 times trailing earnings, below the roughly 37 times it briefly paid in 2025 (July note), for a company growing operating income faster than revenue. The note runs one multiple, July's 24 times (§4 argues it); each turn moves fair value by about $3.32.

This note tests four of these claims: the building clock against the filed record; the depreciation wedge against revenue; the capital bill, on and off the capex line, against the cash the business earns; and the growth and discount rate the price implies. It cannot test, inside an explicit model ending in fiscal 2033, whether AI consumption turns Azure into a business that grows faster than the economy for a decade, and it cannot verify the server share of capex beyond what the filed property table and the CFO's statement show. The Azure stage length is carried as a priced sensitivity and a tripwire, not a verdict.

The filing

The building clock: what the 10-Q shows, and what it leaves out

The claim. On the 29 July call, as reported in three articles by two news outlets and cited in the July note, the CFO said datacenter and office buildings would be depreciated over 25 years instead of 15, effective fiscal 2027. She said more future datacenter leases would be classified as operating as a result, which was why calendar-2026 capex including finance leases was now expected near $175 billion rather than about $190 billion. The July note counted it as the fourteenth life extension across the large cloud companies in its comparison group since 2020 (thirteen on servers, this one on buildings), against one reversal, and said the first fiscal-2027 10-Q would verify it and quantify the benefit.

What the 10-Q says. Nothing. It carries no change-in-estimate note, no useful-life table and no mention of 25 years; "useful lives" appears once, in the standard list of estimates, and property and equipment is not among the critical accounting estimates. Depreciation was $11.1 billion for the quarter, against $7.1 billion a year earlier. The fiscal-2026 10-K's policy still reads "buildings and improvements, five to 15 years," and the October 8-K that recasts that 10-K for the new segments does not update it (it states that it does not reflect events after the 10-K was filed). The 28 October call did not mention useful lives.

What the record does show. Two things July did not have. First, the company's own written record of its July outlook: the 2 September 8-K reproduces the first-quarter guidance "As provided on July 29, 2026", with capex "Expected to be over $50 billion including the impact of the useful life update." That is a furnished document, not a filed financial statement. It confirms an update sat inside the guidance, but it names no asset class and no number of years; the buildings and the 15-to-25-year detail still rest on the call reports. Second, the lease note moved the way such a change predicts. A lease that runs for most of an asset's economic life is a finance lease, so a longer building life moves datacenter leases to operating treatment, outside capex. Operating-lease assets added in the quarter were $6.5 billion, from $1.4 billion a year earlier and more than all of fiscal 2026; finance-lease additions fell from $9.1 billion to $5.2 billion; and the average remaining operating-lease term rose from six years to eight in one quarter, the strongest sign (lease extensions would lengthen it too). The 10-Q gives no cause; delayed third-party capacity could account for part of the fall in finance leases, but not for the longer operating-lease book.

Why the 10-Q is silent. Not yet effective is unlikely: the July outlook already included "the impact of the useful life update" for the first quarter. Not adopted: the lease note argues against it, though no filed statement confirms adoption. Immaterial fits best. US GAAP requires a change in an asset's service life to be disclosed with its income effect when material, and described when immaterial now but reasonably certain to be material later, in interim reports too (ASC 250-10-50-4 and 50-5; ASC 270-10-50-1; the house's reading of the standard). Silence is consistent with the company judging the effect small, as the CFO's July guide of "a minimal benefit" to fiscal-2027 operating income said. That guide does not cover the larger consequence July reported, about $15 billion moved out of calendar-2026 capex through lease classification, which no filing describes. Exhibit 1's small depreciation step is consistent with a small benefit but does not prove one: late third-party capacity also means less equipment in service.

Exhibit 1 — Depreciation in the quarter against growth in the server book

How to read it. Billions of dollars a quarter, September 2025 to September 2026. Gold bars are depreciation expense as filed; outlined bars are the growth in the gross book of servers, network equipment and software over the quarter (net of retirements). The label under each gold bar is the increase in depreciation from the quarter before. Compare the step with the outlined bar beside it. The one thing it shows: the September step (+$0.8 billion) matched the smallest of the year while the server book grew by a record $29.0 billion.

How large could it be? The filing bounds it. If the 25-year life applied to the whole $182.7 billion book of buildings and improvements at June, and those assets were on average two years old, annual depreciation would fall from about $12.2B to about $6.9B, a benefit near $5.3B a year, about 2.9% of the quarter's annualized operating income. That is an upper bound; "minimal" implies a much narrower set of assets, such as new buildings or shells. Depreciation is not cash, so in the cash-flow model the change is worth nothing; in the price-to-earnings leg the upper bound is worth $2.73 a share of fair value at the 20% weight. We carry it at zero, the company's own description.

What it means. The building-life change stands as the July note described it: sourced to the call, now backed by the company's written outlook and a lease note that moved as predicted, but with no filed statement of the asset class, the years or the effect. July's test, that the 10-Q would quantify it, failed as written, most likely because the company treats the change as immaterial. The fiscal-2027 10-K, due around late July 2027, is the first filed statement whose policy table can show the new life (tripwire 5).

The wedge

The wedge: Azure accelerates, and depreciation runs faster

The wedge. Depreciation grew 56% against revenue up 19%; it reached 12.0% of revenue from 9.1%. Operating margin still rose, because operating expenses grew 6%, headcount fell 2% and, in the company's account, engineering work let it "monetize underutilized capacity." The December quarter will be harder: cost of revenue is guided up 26–27% against revenue up 18–19%, and operating margin "down slightly." July's model path for fiscal-2027 server depreciation was about $25.6B on Microsoft's own schedule. On the fiscal-2026 capex actual, the guided run-rate and the server share measured from the filing (§3), the same engine now gives $33.7B, and $50.7B for fiscal 2028. Total depreciation is already running at $44.4B a year before any further step.

Azure. The September restatement gave the history the dollar figure needs: on the new definition Azure grew 40%, 39%, 40% and 42% through fiscal 2026, to $101.9 billion. The September quarter's 48% and the 49–50% guide are a real acceleration, achieved while capacity was short. The CFO tied the guide to "continued efficiency gains across our CPU and GPU fleet helping to offset some 3P GPU as a service contracts that have been delayed to H2", and she warned that later quarters face "a strong prior-year comparable." Outside Azure, revenue grew 6.9%.

The rest of the quarter. Revenue was $92.3 billion, up 19%. Operating income was $45.9 billion, up 21%, a 49.8% margin. Net income was $36.6 billion, or $4.92 a diluted share; excluding a $207 million net gain from the OpenAI investment, $4.89, up 18%, against $4.13 a year earlier. Agents and Infra, the new segment that holds Azure, Microsoft 365 cloud and licensing, grew 25% to $76.8 billion. Devices and Consumer fell 3% to $15.5 billion as Windows OEM revenue dropped 27%. Revenue from commercial arrangements with OpenAI was $10.0 billion, 10.8% of the total, the 10-Q discloses.

The bill

The bill: what the capex line counts, and what it leaves out

$45.2B
capital spending including finance leases in the quarter, against a guide of more than $50 billion: short on delivery timing, by the company's account, not on intent

Capex including finance leases was $45.2 billion, with $7.4 billion of finance leases; cash paid for property and equipment was $39.4 billion, double the year before. The company's capex measure is not a cash figure, so the three do not add, and the 10-Q's finance-lease assets obtained ($5.2 billion) are measured differently again. The company now expects calendar-2026 capex of approximately $170 billion, "due to the 3P capacity delays," against about $175 billion in July. For the December quarter it guides more than $50 billion, and for the second half of the fiscal year it expects spending "to accelerate." The model reads that as fiscal-2027 capex of about $202B including leases ($96B in the first half, the second half 10% higher), of which about $176B is cash. The bear case's second half runs 20% above the first ($212B for the year); the bull case's is level with it ($192B).

What sits outside the line. Because of the building-life change, more datacenters will arrive as operating leases, which never enter capex. The 10-Q shows how large that pipeline is. Leases signed and not yet commenced, primarily datacenters, were $364.6 billion at 30 September (the 10-Q does not split them between finance and operating), up $35.5B in the quarter and more than three times the $106.2 billion of a year earlier, with commencement now running to fiscal 2034. The capex figure the house's calendar-2026 trigger tracks is therefore partly an accounting artifact, and the next update reports an all-in figure beside the company's (tripwire 6). For the valuation the shift changes line items more than economics. Operating-lease rent is paid out of operating cash flow, while finance-lease principal is repaid below the free-cash-flow line, so the reclassification, if anything, makes reported free cash flow a fuller measure of the bill.

How much of the spend is servers. The re-mark needs one number the filing does not give: the share of capex that is short-lived equipment. July assumed 45% and called it unverifiable. Three readings now say it is higher. The gross book of servers, network equipment and software grew $83.0 billion in fiscal 2026, 62% of the growth in all property and equipment, and $29.0 billion in the September quarter, 69%. On the call the CFO said "Roughly three quarters of our capex was for short-lived assets, primarily CPUs and GPUs." The update uses the filed 62% for fiscal-2026 and later spending, the lowest of the three readings, and keeps 45% for earlier years, where the July model was checked against Microsoft's disclosed 2023 life change. On that basis the house three-year clock defers $21.5B of fiscal-2027 depreciation ($13.3B in July) and $33.2B in fiscal 2028. Trailing GAAP earnings of $19.15 a share, including investment gains and losses, re-mark to $17.70, an overstatement of 8.2%, from 5.8% in July. The price-to-earnings leg uses $16.59, the same figure without the net OpenAI gains, because the stake is valued on its own; other investment items stay in. Because the price-to-earnings leg now carries a 20% weight, the share assumption moves fair value only from $367 at 45% to $365 at 75%.

Who pays. Free cash flow fell 27% to $18.6 billion, 51% of net income excluding the OpenAI gain. Shareholders received $10.2 billion in dividends and buybacks, and repurchases under the buyback program fell to $2.9 billion from $4.0 billion. The CFO's frame for the year is that Microsoft will "remain free cash flow positive in FY27" and "access additional sources of funding when we believe it is attractive." On the base path, fiscal-2027 free cash flow to equity is about $33B, or 23% of net income.

Exhibit 4 — Return on invested capital against the cost of equity, twelve quarters

How to read it. Trailing-twelve-month return on invested capital (after-tax operating income over average equity plus debt less cash), percent, December 2023 to September 2026; the dashed line and shaded band below it mark the 9.8% cost of equity used in this note, held flat. Compare the gold line's slope with its distance above the dashed line. The one thing it shows: the return is more than three times its cost and has fallen about ten points in three years.

Return on capital: strong, and fading. Return on invested capital is 35.1% on a trailing basis (56% with acquired goodwill and intangibles removed), 40.0% a year ago and 45.1% at the start of the window. On the house's rule the trend reads as fading: well above the hurdle, with the spread down 4.8 points in four quarters. The cause is the capital base. Average invested capital roughly doubled, from $182B to $373B, while after-tax operating profit rose 60%, because servers and datacenters go on the books before the revenue they will carry. A return this far above its cost is the strongest fact in the company's favor. The direction is the question the next updates answer.

The value

What Microsoft is worth after the print, and why the number moved

The engine is the July note's: revenue, a net margin excluding investment gains, and the share of net income that becomes free cash flow to equity, discounted at the cost of equity, with a terminal value on the last year's earnings and a price-to-earnings leg at 24 times re-marked trailing earnings. Share-based compensation stays expensed and diluted shares (7,446 million) are held flat, as in July: repurchases have roughly offset stock issued to employees, and the diluted count fell 0.3% in a year (7,466 million to 7,446 million).

The cost of equity, the largest change. It is rebuilt on the house rule: the ten-year Treasury yield of 5.05% on 28 October (4.67% in July) plus a beta of 1.058 (five years of monthly returns against the S&P 500, raw 1.086, adjusted a third of the way toward 1.0; July used 0.90 from a year of daily prices) times a 4.5% equity risk premium (4.75% in July): 9.8%, from 8.9%, worth −$37.37. Measured the July way today (one-year daily beta 1.015, 4.75% premium), the cost of equity would be about 9.9% and fair value $362.10, so the rule itself adds +$3.55 a share; the move comes from the higher Treasury yield and a higher measured beta, partly offset by the lower equity risk premium. The house now measures every cost of equity the same way at every update; the alternatives are in the sensitivities below.

The stage ends when the contracted book is delivered, not when demand ends; after that, Azure grows with enterprise IT and AI budgets against competitors' capacity. Above-average growth rarely persists once its driver stops: across US companies from 1951 to 1998, sales growth stayed above the median five years running for 6.3% of firms (Chan, Karceski and Lakonishok, 2003). Hence a four-year fade, steepest in the first year after the stage, when, on average, about half of today's contracted book has been delivered and the comparison base is largest, landing on terminal growth by fiscal 2033. The Azure stage is the input most in the price's favor; at one year longer the fair value is $388, at one year shorter $341.

Together the two parts give company revenue growth of 18.0% in fiscal 2027 (July: 15.5%), on Azure of $147.9B (+45%), and $646B of revenue by fiscal 2033. Net margin excluding investment gains is 37.3% in fiscal 2027, the fiscal-2026 level of 37.6% less the September quarter's year-on-year decline (0.2 point) and a further 0.1 point for the December quarter's operating margin, guided "down slightly." In fiscal 2028 it carries half of the extra depreciation step the new capex path brings ($9.8B more than July's), to 35.9%: the September quarter absorbed all of its depreciation increase, so half is the middle reading; the bull case absorbs all of it, the bear case none. FCFE conversion starts at 23% in fiscal 2027 (July: 48%), from the capex path above and finance-lease principal against operating cash flow at fiscal 2026's ratio to net income, and rises to the house-clock terminal of 84% by fiscal 2033.

How the cash-flow model changed, and why. Four changes, each with its own row in the attribution table. 1. Terminal growth from long-run GDP. July used 4.0%, 4.25% and 3.5% (base, bull, bear) as house judgments. The Federal Reserve's September 2026 longer-run projections, 2.0% real growth and 2.0% inflation, give 4.0% nominal for the base and bull cases and one point less for the bear, where Microsoft loses share of the economy; the base is unchanged, so the effect on fair value is +$0.00. 2. Every fade lands on terminal. July's growth path stopped at 8% in fiscal 2031 and dropped to 4% in one step; the explicit period now runs to fiscal 2033, and growth and conversion both land on their terminal values: −$0.52. 3. Mid-year discounting. July discounted each year's cash at its year end; the house convention discounts it at the middle of the year from the 28 October valuation date: +$3.44. 4. Finance-lease payments are a cost. July's free cash flow subtracted only cash spent on property and equipment. Finance-lease payments are cash too, booked as financing, so the model now deducts their principal in all three cases: $4.6B in fiscal 2027, the September quarter's $1.0B plus the scheduled payments less interest at the 4.5% lease rate. The path still lands on the terminal conversion (84% base, 90% bull, 80% bear), which we now read as covering all capital outlays, a judgment, so the deduction is confined to fiscal 2027–2033: −$1.59. Together: +$1.33, for $367.94 on July's weights.
The OpenAI stake, counted once. Microsoft owns about 24% of OpenAI on an as-converted basis (as if all preferred shares were common), held on the equity method, which books a share of OpenAI's results rather than a market value; $12.0 billion of a $13.0 billion commitment is funded (10-Q). July's cash-flow model excluded the stake and its gains, but its price-to-earnings leg ran on GAAP earnings that included them: $0.67 a share in fiscal 2026 and $1.12 over the last twelve months, mostly the $7.6 billion net gain in the December 2025 quarter, which the 10-Q attributes primarily to the dilution gain from OpenAI's recapitalization. This note takes those gains out of that leg (−$10.71) and values the stake once (+$22.03). The base case marks it at OpenAI's last closed round, $852 billion including the new money on 31 March 2026, less 21% federal tax on the gain over cost, since any sale, sell-down or distribution realizes it: $22.03 a share. The bull case holds it untaxed, as a holder who never sells: $27.46. The bear case takes today's 24% of the roughly $500 billion implied by the value Microsoft stated at the October 2025 recapitalization ($135 billion for about 27%), after tax: $13.07, a value the company stated before $122 billion of new capital, carried here as a floor, not a current value. A round price is what new money paid, not a quote for Microsoft's block, and $35 billion of Amazon's $50 billion commitment is contingent on an IPO or on reaching artificial general intelligence (Bloomberg); the $852 billion counts it. A round of up to $30 billion at $1.25 trillion before the new money, reported by CNBC and TechCrunch on 22 October, had not closed at the valuation date. It would lift the stake to about $32.17 a share after tax ($40.29 untaxed) and the bull case to about $468, still below the price; the stake alone would close that gap only at an OpenAI value near $3.2 trillion. Each $100 billion of OpenAI value moves fair value by about $2.55, and a closed round or listing is re-marked on that slope at each update. Net: +$11.32 at July's weights, for $379.26.
A separate judgment: why the price-to-earnings weight is halved. This is a decision about which methods count, not part of the cash-flow changes above. July weighted 24 times re-marked trailing earnings at 40%. An earnings multiple is a fair lens to the extent that earnings become cash a shareholder can have. At Microsoft that is true for about half of them, on GAAP net income: free cash flow was 50%, 55% and 51% of net income in the last three quarters, capex including leases is 3.8 times depreciation, amortization and other non-cash charges on the cash-flow statement, and the base path takes conversion to 23% this fiscal year. The house applied the same test to Meta in October: the earnings lens carries no weight until free cash flow exceeds 40% of net income for two consecutive quarters, which restores half of July's weight, and two quarters at 60% or more restore all of it. Microsoft's last three quarters sit in the middle band, so the weight is 20% (−$13.62). If the base path's 23% conversion arrives, tripwire 7 removes the weight within two quarters. At July's 40% the fair value would be $379.26; with no earnings weight, $352.02.

The tripwires

What carries the story to the next update

9.8%the cost of equity behind the fair value — the input that moved it most this quarter, re-measured at every update on the same method

Each tripwire states its basis, its tolerance and what the model does with the answer, so the next update applies rules written now. The fiscal second-quarter release is expected in late January 2027 (date not yet confirmed by the company).

  1. Azure in the December quarter. Basis: the FQ2-27 release, Azure growth at constant currency, against the 49–50% guide. At or above 49%: the base second-half path holds. At 52% or more with second-half commentary of acceleration: the base second half moves to the bull's ($368). Below 46%: it moves to the bear's ($362). A quarter moves little; the length of the stage moves a lot (tripwire 2).
  2. The length of the Azure stage. Basis: Azure growth in the June 2027 quarter and the fiscal-2027 10-K's RPO split. Azure at 40% or more with the part of RPO beyond twelve months still growing above 50%: the stage runs one year longer ($388). Azure below 30% with that part of RPO growing below 20%: one year shorter ($341). One signal without the other is recorded and changes nothing, in either direction: the stage is set by delivery against contracted demand, so both legs must agree.
  3. Capex in the December quarter, and calendar 2026. Basis: the FQ2-27 call, capex including finance leases, against "over $50 billion" and the ~$170 billion calendar year. Below $50 billion a second time: the base second half is held level with the first ($370); with Azure at or above the guide the house reads it as the efficiency the CFO describes, with Azure below the guide as a capacity constraint, which also moves Azure's second half under tripwire 1. Above $55 billion: the second half is set 20% above the first ($362).
  4. The depreciation step. Basis: the FQ2-27 10-Q, depreciation for the quarter. A step of $1.1 billion or more over September's $11.1 billion: the half-absorption margin path holds. A step of $0.8 billion or less again, while the server book adds more than $20 billion: the company is absorbing the bill and the base case moves to full absorption ($372); the building-life benefit and assets not yet in service are the two things to test first. A step of $1.6 billion or more: no absorption ($359); delayed third-party capacity entering service in the quarter is the first thing to test.
  5. The building clock in the fiscal-2027 10-K. Basis: the policy table and any change-in-estimate note (about late July 2027). Buildings shown at up to 25 years: the fourteenth extension is confirmed in a filed statement, and nothing in the model changes. An effect quantified at $1 billion or more: the house re-mark adds it back to earnings, worth up to $2.73 in the price-to-earnings leg. Policy still "five to 15 years" with no note: the change rests on the call reports and the July outlook as reproduced in the September 8-K, and the house's count of life extensions marks the fourteenth as not confirmed in filed statements.
  6. Operating leases and the all-in bill. Basis: each 10-Q's lease note, operating-lease assets obtained in the quarter. Above $5 billion again in FQ2-27: the house reports an all-in capex figure (capex including finance leases plus operating-lease assets added) beside the company's in every update, and scores the calendar-2026 trigger on both. Below $3 billion: the September jump is read as a one-off.
  7. Earnings turning into cash. Basis: free cash flow over net income, quarterly, from the cash-flow statement. Two consecutive quarters at 60% or more: the price-to-earnings weight returns to July's 40% ($379.26 at today's inputs). Two consecutive quarters below 40%: the weight goes to zero ($352.02). Between: 20%, as now.
  8. The price. Fair value $365.64. A close at or below it resolves the gap. At $274, a 25% margin of safety, the house would look for an entry if the operating tests above hold (this replaces the July note's $400).

What would change our view

The house would move toward the price on any two of the following. Azure at 40% or more in the June 2027 quarter with RPO beyond twelve months still growing above 50% (tripwire 2), which lengthens the stage. Two quarters of free cash flow at 60% or more of net income (tripwire 7), which restores the earnings weight. Two quarters of depreciation steps at or below $0.8 billion while the server book keeps growing (tripwire 4), which would show the bill being absorbed. A disclosed server-fleet life or utilization study supporting five years or more, which would cut the re-mark. Capex below $50 billion again in the December quarter with Azure at or above the guide (tripwires 1 and 3), which would show the efficiency the CFO describes. Or a raw five-year beta one standard error lower, near 0.92 (0.94 adjusted, a 9.3% cost of equity), which alone is worth about $393; or the ten-year Treasury back at July's 4.67%, worth about $387. At July's 8.9% cost of equity the bull case is about $508, still below the price. All of the model-moving items together (that lower beta, a one-year-longer Azure stage, full absorption of the depreciation step and the earnings weight back at 40%) give about $424, still below the price.

The house would move toward the bear case's inputs on any two of the mirror conditions. Azure below 30% in the June 2027 quarter with RPO beyond twelve months growing below 20% (tripwire 2). Two quarters of free cash flow below 40% of net income (tripwire 7). Depreciation steps of $1.6 billion or more (tripwire 4). Capex below $50 billion again in the December quarter with Azure growth below the guide, which would mark a supply limit rather than a timing slip (tripwires 1 and 3). Separately, a raw five-year beta one standard error higher, near 1.26, would on its own lower fair value to about $342, without touching the scenario inputs.

The call

The call

Fair value $365.64 a share (expected value $353.58; bear $228.14, bull $454.90), from $403 on 31 July; the $531.01 close of 28 October is 45% above it, and the $547.30 after-hours print is 50% above it. The quarter confirmed the operating case: Azure is a $33 billion-a-quarter business growing near 50%. It did not shorten the bill: depreciation is growing nearly three times as fast as revenue, and the signed lease pipeline more than tripled in a year. The value fell because the cost of equity we measure rose, on a higher Treasury yield and a higher five-year beta, an estimate whose one-standard-error band runs from about $342 to $393, not because the business weakened. The quarter's facts, the method changes and the OpenAI stake (+$13.51 together) and the halved earnings weight (−$13.62) roughly cancel; the quarter's gains were offset by the cost of building for them. The price asks for the bull case's operating path and a discount rate below July's.
