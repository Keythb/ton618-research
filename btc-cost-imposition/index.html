AI Makes Attacks Cheap. Can Bitcoin Make Them Expensive?

Since 2009 anyone can take a final payment from an anonymous stranger with no bank in the middle. Charged as a fee, or held as a deposit lost on misuse, it puts a cost on an attacker that he cannot push onto a stolen machine. By the arithmetic it would add value now where attacks mostly fail and the failure shows at once, among users who can pay. Where attacks mostly succeed and stay hidden, which is where the money is, it would, on the mechanism, reduce the damage but deter only under conditions not yet met: to deter today, honest users would post about twice what an attacker could take.

Defending a computer system has always cost the defender more than attacking it costs the attacker. Filters, passwords, monitoring and staff are the defender's bill; the attacker pays for a connection and his time. Artificial intelligence is now cutting the attacker's bill further, and a growing body of reporting from the AI laboratories and from governments says so. One proposed answer has a long history and new advocates: make the attacker pay, at a gate built on Bitcoin. A 2023 thesis from MIT, Jason Lowery's Softwar, argued that Bitcoin's real significance is as a way to impose physical cost on adversaries through the internet. In April 2026 the commander of US Indo-Pacific Command told the Senate that Bitcoin, through its proof-of-work, "imposes more costs than just the algorithmic securing of networks". Anthony Scaramucci, the founder of SkyBridge Capital, asked in April what bitcoin would be worth as a global requirement for cybersecurity against AI, and in a post dated 15 November, as reproduced on a third-party account, called it a white swan in the making, writing that AI had made hacking free and that bitcoin would make it expensive. This note sets out how such a gate would work, as a fee or as a deposit the attacker forfeits, and where it would add value: for whom, at what size, against which attacks, and how much bitcoin it would hold if it spread. It states no view on the price of bitcoin.

The questions

What this note answers

  1. Where does the idea come from? The thirty-year history of charging for access, what Softwar proposes, and what it leaves for a builder to supply.
  2. How much cheaper has AI made attacking? The published dollar costs, per attempt and per success, and the evidence that the fall is overstated.
  3. What already charges for access, and in what? Twenty systems, from puzzles to fees to deposits, and which of them use bitcoin.
  4. What would a deposit have to do to deter an attacker? The decision rule, the review window, and what both cost the honest user.
  5. Where does it work? Six uses, each seen by the operator, the honest user and the attacker.
  6. Why bitcoin and not a dollar token? Including the security of the ledger the gate stands on.
  7. How much bitcoin could it hold, what would have to be true for that to be large, and what would change this note's answer?

The read. A cost the attacker must pay in his own money is a real defense, and bitcoin is one of the few ways to charge it to a stranger with no bank, card network or token issuer in the middle. The claim holds, in a narrower scope than its proponents give it. The test is whether a system is better with the gate than without it, net of what it costs honest users: an attack that stops paying is the best result, and less damage or load also counts. Fees would work. A final payment of a fraction of a cent to a dollar would remove the profit from bulk abuse; it is live in bitcoin on a small scale and in dollar tokens on a larger one. But honest users need a wallet: 10% of American adults used any cryptocurrency in 2025 and 2% paid with it, so a gate open to the general public has to keep a second route or shut out or burden most of them, and a general-public login gated against guessing with no second route would be not better. Deposits deter when a machine can check the offense. Where a failed attempt shows at once, a deposit of the prize times the odds of success is enough, and the largest forfeitable deposit system found works this way. Where a person has to judge the damage, they reduce it. A deterring deposit must exceed the attacker's whole prize, so an honest user posts about twice the prize in bitcoin to hold it through a review, and the bitcoin exchange that launched with a neutral arbiter's key removed it in 2019. An operator whose users already trust it with their balances can rule on forfeits itself, leaving them the risk of a wrongful seizure by a party that profits from it. Smaller charges still make every attempt cost coins, though not the attacker's own if they are stolen, and make breadth need capital, which squeezes the low-budget operator AI has newly equipped, not one holding stolen coins; and a gate reaches an exploit of a software flaw only where it is hard-coded in front of a private system. The judged case is where the value is. No access deposit holding bitcoin was found; the largest bitcoin bond for good behavior found, JoinMarket's at about $30 million, cannot be forfeited; the forfeitable bonds on bitcoin exchanges cover single trades and publish no total; and six conditions, none fully met, stand between that and a large number. The first two findings rest on arithmetic and on systems in use, though no bitcoin fee system publishes its volume; the third rests on few cases and no deployment, which is itself the finding.

Terms used throughout, defined once. Proof-of-work: a puzzle that takes real computing effort to solve and almost none to check; Bitcoin uses it to order its ledger, and websites use it to slow down bots. Denial-of-service: flooding a system with requests until it cannot serve real users. Botnet: a fleet of other people's computers that an attacker controls. Sybil attack: one party posing as many, to outvote or out-shout the real users of an open system. Stablecoin: a token on a public blockchain that its issuer promises to redeem for one dollar; USDC and USDT are the largest. Lightning: a network built on Bitcoin that settles small payments in about a second. Hold invoice: a Lightning payment the receiver can accept and then either keep or hand back. Multisig: a bitcoin address that needs several keys to spend; "2-of-3" means any two of three key-holders. L402 and x402: two ways for a web server to answer a request with "payment required" and be paid on the spot, the first in bitcoin over Lightning, the second mostly in stablecoins. Fee: a payment that is final. Deposit: a payment held and returned unless a stated condition is met. Forfeit: the deposit is kept. Review window: the time a deposit is held before it is returned. Dwell time: the days an intruder is inside a network before being found.

Both sides, first

Both cases, each at full strength

This note's answer cuts against both the widest version of the claim and the flat dismissal of it. Each is set out here in the terms its best advocate would use, before any of it is tested.

The case that this is Bitcoin's next use.

  1. The cost of attacking has fallen where it matters. One operator, per one vendor's interim report, attacked hundreds of online retailers in late summer 2026, with open-source attack tools driving a commercial AI model, at a mean of $25.46 a target scanned. A frontier laboratory's model built a working exploit for a known flaw for under $1,000 in half a day. The laboratories now write that the economics of attacks have changed, and five governments' cyber agencies said jointly in June 2026 that the timeline "is not years, it is months".
  2. Every existing defense is paid for by the defender. Filtering, monitoring and identity checks raise the defender's costs, and a puzzle can be solved on machines the attacker stole or rents by the hour. The only cost that lands on the attacker himself is money he has to part with.
  3. Bitcoin is the largest asset that can charge that cost to anyone, with nobody in the middle. A card payment can be disputed for 120 days, and for some disputes up to 540, and a card hold may not be used as a damage bond. A dollar token has an issuer that can freeze the operator's takings. A bitcoin payment is final and has no issuer.
  4. The gate stands on the ledger with the most computation behind it. About 985 quintillion hashes a second of computation secure bitcoin's ledger, and no single party holds a key that can change its rules, pause it or freeze a coin. A dollar token's rules can be changed: its issuer holds keys that mint, freeze, pause and upgrade it, and the ledger beneath it is secured by the host chain's stake, not by work. A deposit is only as safe as the ledger it sits on, and a gate meant to stop the best-funded attackers should sit on the one with the longest record.
  5. Forfeitable deposits already secure very large sums. Ethereum's validators have about $82 billion of ether locked that is cut for provable misconduct. Systems built on Ethereum hold claims for about seven days under bonds that are lost if the claim is proved false. On Bitcoin, peer-to-peer exchanges bond every trade over Lightning.
  6. The value scales with what is guarded. A fee is spent and resold. A deposit sized to the asset it protects is held, so demand for the coin would grow with the value of the networks behind the gate.
  7. It only has to pay for itself. No security layer stops every attack. A deposit earns its place if the losses it prevents and the forfeits it recovers exceed what honest users give up while their money is held.
  8. A cost in energy cannot be faked by whoever writes the software. This is Lowery's own argument. Rules enforced by code, including money put at stake under code, are only as trustworthy as the people who control the code. Proof-of-work is the one cost a program cannot counterfeit, which is why he rejects stake and builds on Bitcoin.
  9. Serious people are looking. A US combatant commander told the Senate in April 2026 that Bitcoin has "really important computer science applications for cyber security".
  10. Users need not run a wallet of their own. Wallets built into apps people already use can pay: Cash App's help page says it pays a Lightning invoice scanned with its camera, from its cash balance (with a small processing fee) or a bitcoin one, up to $999 every 7 days. And the first services to gate are those whose users already hold bitcoin.

The case that it is not.

  1. Dollar tokens do the same job, and do one thing bitcoin cannot. USDC and USDT can be held and sent without an account, and settle finally. The payment standard built for software agents has recorded more than 138 million transactions on one chain alone, in stablecoins. When funds are stolen, Tether freezes them and reissues them to their owners or to law enforcement, about $1.2 billion so far by its own count. A deposit in dollars does not lose a quarter of its value in a bad month.
  2. The controls that exist are cheap and working. Multi-factor authentication cut the risk of account compromise by 99.22% in Microsoft's own study of 2022 accounts. Filtering a million web requests costs sixty cents. Password attacks fell 26% in a year.
  3. Intrusions are found late. More than a third of intrusions investigated in 2024 were not found within 30 days, espionage cases took a median of 122 days in 2025, and one survey puts the mean time to identify a breach at 183 days. A deposit that is returned before the damage is found deters nothing.
  4. Nobody does it. No production system was found that takes a forfeitable deposit for network access. Where forfeiture was designed in, builders have retreated from it: a bitcoin peer-to-peer exchange removed its arbitrator's key in 2019, and a messaging network that specified forfeiture shipped without it.
  5. The cost collapse is overstated. The United Kingdom's cyber agency judged in November 2026 that human operators "highly likely remain essential". The best model failed seven attempts in ten on an undefended test network, at a derived cost of $8,000 to $42,000 per success. Defenders get the same tools.
  6. Attackers increasingly do not log in, and they are not short of coins. Exploiting a software flaw is now the most common way into a breached organization, at 31%, and it involves no session to attach a deposit to. Thieves took more than $3.4 billion of cryptocurrency in 2025.
  7. Deposits are lost around the ledger, not on it. The most recent theft from a bitcoin deposit system on this note's record, 11.59 bitcoin from ten Bisq users in May 2026, came through a missing check in the code that builds the trade's deposit transaction; an earlier one, in April 2020, took about 3 bitcoin and 4,000 monero from seven users through a flaw in the trade protocol. Ledger security does not protect the gate's software, wallet or keys.
  8. Few people can pay. In 2025, 2% of American adults paid for anything with any cryptocurrency, and 10% used it at all.

Of these, this note tests the cost evidence on both sides, and whether attackers are in fact going around the login and what a gate in front of the whole system changes (§2, §4); where deposits have actually been lost, against Lowery's objection to stake enforced by code (§1, §3, §6); what card rules and token issuers actually permit, and what the ledger's security does and does not protect (§3, §6); the record of twenty systems that charge for access (§3); the size of deposit that deters, the gentler test of whether it pays for itself, and what both cost honest users (§4); each use in turn, including how well the existing controls are working and how many users can pay (§5); and the scale (§7). It cannot test whether the fall in attack cost continues, which is a forecast; whether users and operators would accept the friction, because there is no deployment to observe; how far wallets built into apps would close the gap in who can pay, which no primary count measures; how a regulator or court would treat the deposit, which the governing texts leave open; what a state-backed attacker would budget for forfeits, on which no primary source exists; the thesis's larger claims about war and national strategy, which are outside a note about access control; or what Lowery has argued since 2023, which was not found in any primary source.

The idea

An old idea: make the sender pay

1992the year a computational charge for access was proposed, as a substitute for a payment that did not yet exist

The idea that abuse is a pricing problem is as old as the commercial internet. In 1992 Cynthia Dwork and Moni Naor proposed fighting junk e-mail by making each sender compute "a moderately hard, but not intractable, function" before a message would be accepted. Their paper considered charging money first, and set it aside: usage fees, they wrote, "may be a deterrent", but they did not want a note between friends to cost as much as a postage stamp. Computation was the stand-in for a payment that could be small, automatic and universal, and no such payment existed. Adam Back built the working version in 1997 and called it hashcash; his 2002 paper is titled "Hashcash - A Denial of Service Counter-Measure". Satoshi Nakamoto's Bitcoin paper cites it, and eight days after Bitcoin's first release, in January 2009, Nakamoto wrote that the software "can already be used for pay-to-send e-mail".

The puzzle version was tested against real data in 2004 and lost. Ben Laurie and Richard Clayton worked out how hard the puzzle would need to be to make spam unprofitable, then how hard it would need to be once spammers "steal processing cycles" from infected home computers, and compared both with the mail a large British internet provider's customers actually sent. Against a million infected machines the puzzle needed to take 346 seconds, and at the difficulty levels that would stop spam, 0.13% to 1.56% of legitimate senders could no longer send their normal mail on a daily count, and 1% to 13% on an hourly one. A 2006 reply conceded the central point, that "a uniform POW mechanism will not work", and rescued the idea only by adding reputation, which is to say identity.

The money version was tried in the same years. Researchers at Michigan and Boston proposed an "attention bond" in 2004: the sender posts a bond with each first message, and the recipient "may, at her sole discretion, seize the bond or release it". A company called IronPort ran one. Its Bonded Sender program had e-mail senders post $250 to $4,000 or more at an escrow bank and debited $20 for each complaint above one per million messages. Microsoft used the list for Hotmail from late 2003. Two features of that program matter for what follows. The senders were identified, certified companies, not strangers. And the forfeits did not go to the people who complained: "In order to avoid conflicts of interest, forfeited bonds are distributed to an independent, disinterested non-profit organization."

What Softwar says

Lowery's thesis, submitted at MIT in February 2023, is a 400-page argument that security has only ever come from the ability to impose physical cost, that software has lacked any way to do so, and that Bitcoin's proof-of-work supplies one. Its design proposal takes up a few pages. All that is needed, it says, is to "create a firewall-style application programming interface (API) which utilizes a physical cost function protocol like Bitcoin", a "proof-of-power wall", that rejects any request arriving without proof of work. The cost is tuned "by adjusting the difficulty of the hashing algorithm or requiring more proofs of power". The aim is deterrence, stated exactly: attackers are stopped "because they find it impossible to justify the physical cost of performing an attack". The thesis is candid about its standing. "As of now," it says of its closing theory of Bitcoin as a planetary-scale computer, "these ideas are nothing more than theories". Its policy chapter recommends that the United States treat Bitcoin as a security system first and money second, consider stockpiling it, and consider supporting domestic mining.

What the thesis supplies is the principle and the ledger; what it leaves to a builder is the rest of this note. The cost it specifies at the wall is computation, and the history above shows why computation alone does not carry the idea: a uniform puzzle falls lightly on an attacker with other people's machines, a case the thesis does not take up. It leaves open that the "stamp" presented at the wall could be a coin instead of fresh computation, without saying whether the coin would be paid, held or returned, to whom, or on what condition; no fee, deposit, refund or arbiter appears in it. A deposit forfeited on misuse also goes a step past the book, which argues that money put at stake under software rules cannot do what proof-of-work does (point 8 of the case above). The gate this note builds keeps the principle and the ledger and adds the missing half: a fee or a deposit, paid in bitcoin, with rules for who keeps it.

Two of the thesis's critics made points that any working gate has to answer. Jameson Lopp, a security engineer who runs a hashcash gate on his own website and reports it leaves him with "far fewer" unwanted messages, called the wall "basically hashcash" and asked what would check the proof of work: "If you guessed 'software' then you're correct!" The writer Shinobi made the sharper one in 2024: "A money that anyone can get their hands on does nothing to restrict access to a system to only specific people that you want to access that system." Payment is not authentication. It cannot keep an intruder out; it can only change what an attempt costs him.

Puzzles, meanwhile, went into wide use with no bitcoin in them. Tor added one for its hidden services in August 2023, designed to help defend against a lone attacker and "small botnets"; its designers wrote that a large botnet would need more tools. A tool called Anubis, which makes every visitor's browser solve one, spread across open-source sites in 2025 after AI companies' crawlers overwhelmed them. A security researcher, Tavis Ormandy, priced it: counting one site for every star the tool then had on its code page, solving a week's puzzles for all of them would take a free-tier cloud machine "about 6 minutes", so "the cost of unrestricted crawler access to the internet for a week is approximately $0". The attacker of 2025 does not need to steal the computers. He owns a data center.

So the principle holds, and computation alone does not deliver it. Money can, and the rest of this note shows what happens when the cost at the gate is a bitcoin payment or deposit.

The cost of attacking

What AI has done to the cost of attacking

2.5× to 1,400×how much more a success costs than an attempt in the studies that report both: 2.5 to 3.3× where three in ten or more attempts succeed, about 1,400× in a laboratory test where successes were rare

The claim to test is that AI is driving the cost of attacking toward zero. The published dollar figures, all of them, are in Exhibit 1.

The direction is not in doubt. A personalized phishing e-mail, researched and written by a model, costs about five cents and, in a study of 101 people, was clicked as often as one written by a human expert, 54% against 12% for a generic one. Scanning a newly deployed smart contract for an exploitable flaw cost $1.22 a run in a laboratory's December 2025 test. In April 2026 the same laboratory reported that its strongest model, which it does not sell openly, turned a known flaw into a working exploit for under $1,000, and ran a thousand bug-hunting passes over an operating system for under $20,000, finding a flaw that had sat there for 27 years. The one figure from the wild comes from a security firm that recovered a criminal's own server in September 2026: the operator's cost review showed a mean of $25.46 for each retailer scanned, card-stealing code was ordered against at least 27 named victims, the firm put the whole campaign's spend at $12,000 to $18,000, and it concluded that "the economics no longer filters anyone out". That report is one vendor's, interim and uncorroborated, and this note treats it as the best single measurement available and no more.

"Toward zero" is a different claim, and the same sources do not support it. Every cost on the exhibit is positive, and the cost of a success depends on how often attempts succeed. Where four in ten succeed, as with 2024's exploits of known flaws that the model was told about, a success costs 2.5 times an attempt. Where successes are rare the gap is wide: $1.22 a scan but $1,738 for each vulnerable contract found, about 1,400 times, leaving $109 of profit per exploit. The United Kingdom's AI Security Institute found that the best model took over an undefended 32-step test network in 3 attempts out of 10; a researcher who priced those attempts at published rates put a success at $8,000 to $42,000, and observed that for that money an attacker "could easily hire multiple human operators". Google's threat group wrote in September 2026 that the cost of model access "is one of the primary barriers" for attackers, which is why they steal other people's AI accounts.

Volume is rising in some places and falling in others. One large vendor counts malicious automated traffic at 40% of what it sees in 2025, up from 37% a year earlier, and account-takeover attacks up 70% between July 2024 and July 2025. Cloudflare measured crawling by AI agents acting for users up more than fifteenfold in 2025. A study of one e-mail filter's catch judged 51% of spam to be machine-written by April 2025. Against that, Microsoft's 2026 report says password attacks are "down 26% year-over-year" and credits multi-factor authentication and passkeys. Verizon's study of more than 22,000 breaches finds stolen credentials falling to 13% of the ways in, or 16% on the previous year's definition against 22%, and says the share of breaches that begin with phishing "has barely moved". The share of Cloudflare's traffic it mitigated, as attack traffic or under customers' own rules, was 6.2% in 2025, a quarter of a point lower than in 2024. The United Kingdom's cyber agency, in its review of the year to August 2026 published on 3 November, still judged that AI increases the "speed, scale and efficiency of existing attack methodologies rather than autonomously conducting end-to-end attacks" and that "human operators highly likely remain essential". The same tools serve the defender: a US government competition in 2025 had AI systems finding and patching flaws at an average of about $152 per task.

Two facts in this evidence decide a good deal of what follows. The first is where the change is largest. It is in finding and exploiting software flaws, which Verizon now counts as the most common way into a breached organization, 31% of cases against 20% a year earlier. An attacker who comes in through a flaw never logs in. He meets a payment only where a gate stands in front of the whole system, so that nothing answers before payment (§4). The second is what kind of cost has fallen. It is first the attacker's labor and skill, the cost of trying, and the prize is unchanged. The chance that an attempt succeeds is rising too, from a lower base: the laboratory's newest model produced 181 working exploits of one browser engine where its predecessor managed two in several hundred attempts, and success rates in the studies above run from a fraction of 1% of scans to three or four attempts in ten. The difference between the cost of trying and the odds of success is the hinge of §4.

One more finding, about words. A foreseeable risk with relatively predictable consequences is called a white swan, and Anthony Scaramucci applied the label to this case in a post dated 15 November 2026, as reproduced on a third-party account. An academic writing in November 2025 had called AI security risks "grey swans", rare but more foreseeable events, because "we can see them coming but arguably don't (or won't) prepare for them". The discussion is being had by an investor, and by the laboratories, five governments' agencies and the largest incident-response firms in their own plainer words.

Read by family, the ledger says three things.

Effort is free to the well-resourced. The first three rows use no bitcoin and impose no cost that a data center notices. They filter out the laziest automation, and their own builders say so: Anubis's documentation calls its puzzle "a hack" and a placeholder for better detection.

Fees are live, small, and mostly in dollars. Charging per request is the part of the idea that already works. A paid Nostr relay charges 18,888 sats, about $12, to join and markets itself as spam-free. Stacker News raises its posting fee tenfold for each repeat within ten minutes, a rule any forum could copy. But the largest fee system with a published volume, x402, settles in stablecoins. Its governing foundation's premier members include Visa, Mastercard, American Express and Stripe, and its Bitcoin Lightning method has been a written specification since 23 September 2026 with no code in the official toolkits as of this note. Cloudflare, which said in September 2025 that its network sends over a billion "payment required" responses a day, prices pay per crawl in US dollars and bills it through its own accounts. Sixteen months after launch the product is still in closed testing, is being reworked into payment per use, and has published no payment figure.

Deposits work at scale when a machine can check the offense, and stay small or retreat when a person must. The largest forfeitable deposit found is Ethereum's, about $82 billion. A validator is slashed, losing part of its deposit and its place, for signing two conflicting messages, a fact anyone can verify from the signatures themselves; there is no judge, and what is forfeited is destroyed, not paid to a victim. The systems built on Ethereum hold claims for about a week under bond, and a disputed claim is settled by replaying the computation. On the other side of the line are the systems where forfeiture needs a human finding. Bisq, a bitcoin exchange, launched with exactly that design and removed it. "Bisq's new trade protocol moves to 2-of-2 multisig escrow from 2-of-3," its October 2019 release note says. "The third key for arbitrators was removed." Its stated reasons were that a hacked arbitrator could take every locked deposit and a dishonest one could side with a trader to defraud the other, that the role did not scale, and that holding a key made arbitrators look like financial intermediaries. Bonded Sender kept its judges and sent the forfeits to a non-profit so that nobody who ruled on a complaint profited from it. Waku, a messaging network, specified a deposit that would be seized from spammers and shipped a contract whose specification reads: "This specification does not include slashing."

Judged forfeits do survive in two settings. One is where the judge is the venue and the stakes are small: Hodl Hodl still holds a third key to every trade on its own platform, and RoboSats' coordinator decides who loses a bond worth 3% of a retail trade that lasts hours. The other is on Ethereum, where UMA settles disputed claims by a vote of its token-holders, a panel neutral by design whose bond sizes and volume were not found published. No bitcoin system was found in which a neutral party rules, over weeks, on harm done to a system it does not run, and that is what guarding a network with a bitcoin deposit would ask of a judge.

Two rows need a caution, because they are easily read as more than they are. JoinMarket's bonds are the largest pool of bitcoin found locked for good behavior, 460 coins, and they cannot be forfeited at all. Their own documentation says "the valuable thing being sacrificed is the time-value-of-money", and adds that a long-term holder can post one "essentially for free". They make a fake identity expensive; they punish nothing. And the idea of a refundable bitcoin deposit for access is not new with this note. A Lightning software company published it as developer guidance in October 2025: "a user pays a Hold invoice to start a session or request a resource. If they behave, you cancel it with zero cost to them. If they abuse it, you settle the payment as a penalty." A hackathon team built it for code contributions in June 2026, on test coins. What does not exist is anyone running it in production to guard a network.

One more entry belongs here, because it bears on the premise. On 1 May 2026 an attacker took 11.59 bitcoin from ten Bisq users, in trades of bitcoin for other coins, through a flaw in the code that builds the deposit transaction, the second time the escrow plumbing itself was the way in; in April 2020 a flaw in its trade protocol had let an attacker take about 3 bitcoin and 4,000 monero from seven users. Bisq's own review found the flaw with AI assistance "significantly faster than the rest of the team" and said it believed the attacker may have done the same. The precedent for bitcoin deposits may already have met the cost collapse this note is about.

The deposit and its rule

The deposit, and the rule it has to beat

prize ÷ odds of forfeitthe deposit that removes an attacker's profit when only successful attacks can be punished
How the gate would work. The gate is hard-coded in front of the system: nothing behind it answers, not even the password prompt, until a bitcoin payment or deposit has arrived from whoever is connecting. A fee is a small final payment per attempt. For a deposit, whoever opens a session posts bitcoin first, to an address that needs two of three keys to spend: the user's, the operator's and an independent arbiter's. The transaction also carries a deadline after which the user can take the coins back alone. If nothing goes wrong, the user reclaims the deposit when the review window ends, or sooner if the operator co-signs. If the operator finds damage traced to that session, it must persuade the arbiter to co-sign a transfer before the deadline. An intruder holding a stolen password has to pay or post from his own wallet before he can type it; he cannot spend the victim's. The aim is to move risk and cost onto whoever misuses the gate, not to earn revenue, so whatever an authenticated user puts up can be returned.

That is the design under test. Its parts are older than they look: the deposit posted by whoever makes contact is the attention bond of 2004, the deadline that returns the coins is an example printed in Bitcoin's own technical standards, and the third key is the one Bisq removed. Two standards apply. The stricter asks whether the attack stops paying, and this section applies it first, because a forfeit that leaves a profit in the attack taxes the attacker without changing his decision. The gentler, which its advocates would set, asks whether the gate pays for itself: whether the losses prevented and forfeits recovered exceed what honest users give up. A third effect sits between them. Across many attackers, a charge removes every one whose margin per attempt it exceeds, so a gate that fails the stricter test against the best-funded can still cut the number of attempts and the load they put on the defender. The puzzles of §1 fit that pattern: Tor designed its puzzle to help against a lone attacker and small botnets, and says a large botnet needs more tools, and a security engineer who runs a hashcash gate on his own site reports "far fewer" unwanted messages. Fewer attempts need not mean less damage, though. A charge leaves the best-funded and those after the largest prizes (§5), so damage can fall by less than the count, and the defender's load then includes the gate itself: its payment path, its refunds and its own code as a target. Exhibit 6 keeps the stricter test and the thinning apart. Its size column is the stricter test; its rating gives credit for thinning, ranked below deterrence, most where the load comes from volume and least where the damage comes from a few.

An attacker decides by comparing what an attempt is worth with what it risks. Write the chance that an attempt succeeds as s, the prize as G, the deposit as D, the chance that a successful attack is found, tied to its session and awarded before the deadline as q, and the chance that a failed attempt is caught and forfeits as f. An attempt pays when s × G is more than the expected forfeit, (s × q + (1 − s) × f) × D. The line leaves out the attacker's cost of capital and the cost of mounting the attempt; both would lower the deposit needed a little, and the second is the cost AI is removing. The deposit that removes the profit is therefore

D = s × G ÷ (s × q + (1 − s) × f).

Two cases fall out of that line, and they are the whole argument.

When only success can be punished, the deposit must exceed the prize. If a failed attempt looks like ordinary traffic, f is zero, the chance of success cancels out of the line, and D = G ÷ q. An attacker who expects to gain $100,000 and to forfeit one time in two is deterred only by a deposit of $200,000. No amount of cleverness in the design changes this: the deposit has to be worth more to him than the attack, and every honest user has to post the same amount, because the gate cannot tell them apart. That is the premise of having a gate.

When failure is punished, the deposit shrinks to the odds. If every failed attempt forfeits, as a wrong password can, D is about s × G. A guess with one chance in a million of opening an account worth $20,699, the average loss in a complaint to the FBI's internet-crime center in 2025, is unprofitable against a forfeit of two cents. At one chance in ten thousand the forfeit needed is $2.07. Honest users, who rarely fail, almost never pay it.

There is a third effect that the rule does not capture, and it favors the deposit. The rule assumes the attacker can fund it. The one campaign measured in the wild ran for about seven weeks on a total spend the investigating firm put at $12,000 to $18,000; in nine of those days its scanner ran against 138 hosts. It scanned public websites for weaknesses, so a session deposit would not in fact have met it; the arithmetic shows what a gate does where every target requires a session. Had each of those 138 targets held a refundable deposit of $1,000 at once, the operator would have needed $138,000 locked, about eight to twelve times his whole budget, before forfeiting a cent. A deposit turns an attack that is cheap to repeat into one that needs working capital in proportion to its breadth. That squeezes the low-budget operator AI has newly equipped. It does not squeeze an attacker holding stolen coins, of which §5 finds no shortage, or one who never passes a gate.

This is why §2 separated the two things AI changes. A falling cost of trying is exactly what a fee or a forfeit replaces: the attacker's labor is free, so charge him money. A rising chance of success is what a deposit cannot answer cheaply. Panel B is that statement as a picture. As attempts go from one-in-a-million guesses toward the laboratory results in Exhibit 1, the deposit that deters climbs from a rounding error toward the whole prize.

Those figures lean in the deposit's favor in three ways, and against it in one. The record counts only intrusions that were eventually found and investigated; those never found are absent. And the record is moving the wrong way: the median time to find an intrusion rose from 10 days in 2023 to 11 in 2024 and 14 in 2025, with espionage cases at a median of 122 days. An interview survey of breached organizations gives a mean of 183 days to identify a breach; that is a different measure of a different population, and it is slower still. The one way they lean against it: a response firm reports a median of three days across 661 cases, with the fall particularly notable among the clients it monitors continuously, and an operator who logs every session for a deposit gate is closer to such a monitored network than to the average.

The mechanics add limits of their own. The self-executing deadline can be set at most 65,535 blocks ahead, about 455 days. On Lightning, a deposit routed through the network can be held about 14 days at the outside and in practice hours, because the specification caps how long a payment may stay pending and every node along the route has its funds tied up, unpaid, while it does. The team that built the code-contribution bond found this out and wrote it down: "Lightning's timeout mechanics make long-lived review periods difficult to support." They fell back on simply holding the money, which is the arrangement that raises the regulatory question in §5.

No single formula

The rule above is the test a pricing scheme has to pass: over the attempts an attacker plans, what he expects to pay must exceed what he expects to gain. It is not the scheme. The operator can price access in many ways, and combine them.

  • A fee or a forfeit per attempt, the cases above.
  • A limit in time. Five attempts an hour on one account allow 43,800 a year, so a guess with one chance in a million takes about 23 years on average to come good, whatever is charged.
  • A price that rises with each attempt. Start at one cent and double it with each failure on the same account: ten failures cost $10.23, twenty $10,485.75 and thirty $10.7 million. An honest user who mistypes twice puts up three cents, which, if held rather than spent, is refundable once the session proves clean, provided the refund rests on something a guesser lacks, such as a recognized device or a review window that closes with no damage found; a refund on a correct password alone would also refund a guesser who gets in. Long-odds guessing is priced out: a guess with one chance in a thousand needs about a thousand attempts, and the doubling puts that far beyond any prize. Short-odds guesses, such as a common password, stay cheap and need separate handling, for instance refusing common passwords when they are set. And the doubling works against depth, not breadth: a guesser who spreads a few tries across many accounts meets only the first price on each, which still has to pass the rule above.
  • Free at first, then priced. The first visits from an address, a device or an account cost nothing, and the price rises with use. A public site can run this way: a reader who looks at a few pages may never reach the price, and a scraper that keeps one identity and comes back for more does. One that changes address or device each time avoids it, as below.

Each rule is only as strong as the factor it counts, because the attacker multiplies whatever is cheap to multiply. A new internet address is cheap. Residential addresses that rotate are sold at about $3 to $20 or more a gigabyte, which at an assumed 10 kilobytes an attempt is about a fiftieth of a cent at $20, so a price that rises per address resets for almost nothing. A price that rises per target account, or per resource, cannot be reset that way, because the attacker cannot multiply the account he is after, when he is after one. That has a cost of its own: a price keyed to an account lets an attacker raise what the real owner pays at the next login, so the operator has to spare a recognized device or a correct password from the increase. And no rule of this kind stops a stolen password that works the first time; that is still priced at one entry, or at nothing inside a free allowance.

The purpose of every one of these rules is to transfer risk and cost to the attacker, not to raise revenue, so the operator can return what any authenticated user puts up. The honest user's lasting cost is then having the money and a wallet to pay from, the price risk and interest forgone while it is held, and the miner fees for each cycle; where the operator rules on forfeits, also the risk of a wrongful seizure or of an intruder riding his session (§5). These are worked examples, not observed schemes: the operator chooses and combines a free allowance, a rising price, a limit in time and a deposit, matched to what the gate guards. The arithmetic says which combinations deter, not which one is right.

Hard-coded in front of everything

The strongest form of the gate is not a login page that asks for money. It is a system that cannot be reached at all without it: the first message, before any password prompt, must carry proof of a bitcoin payment or deposit, and anything else is dropped unanswered. Three things follow from the mechanism. A stolen password no longer opens the system by itself: the thief must also pay or post from his own coins. But like a flaw that works the first time, it costs him one entry, so the deposit rule above still sets what deters him. Guessing is priced per guess, the case above where every failure costs and the charge shrinks to the odds. And an exploit of a software flaw behind the gate has to pass the gate first, so the most common way into a breached organization in Verizon's count is reached too, for the systems that sit behind one.

The limits are as plain. The design suits systems that are not meant to answer the public: administrative interfaces, remote access, private interfaces for software, internal networks, custody systems. A public website can answer first visits free and price what follows, which holds against a visitor who keeps one identity and not one who changes address each time; but a flaw or a stolen password that works on a free first visit meets no price, so the gate in front of everything stays a design for private systems. And there is a cheaper way to hide a private system: answer only known parties, through a private network or a list of allowed addresses. The gate adds value where the operator cannot or will not know in advance who is connecting, the same place §5 finds deposits strongest. The gate's own code becomes the thing to attack, so it has to be small: checking a Lightning payment is one hash of the preimage compared with the invoice's payment hash, and a deposit is checked against the bitcoin ledger itself, which is where that ledger's security (§6) enters, because the gate takes its answer from the ledger, not from the party connecting. These consequences are reasoned from the mechanism; no such gate is in use to observe.

Set by value and time, with no judge

A deposit does not need a machine to check the offense, or a neutral arbiter. The operator can set its amount and its hold from the value behind the gate, and keep the decision to forfeit for itself. Both choices change what the gate asks of its users.

Size the deposit to what one session can reach. In the rule, the prize G can be no larger than the value a session can move. An operator who caps that value, as exchanges already cap daily withdrawals, caps the deposit with it, and needs no estimate of any attacker. That caps the prize only where it is money the session can move. A session into an administrative interface or internal network, the systems the hard-coded gate suits, reaches data and a foothold that no withdrawal limit bounds, and there the ransom-sized prizes of §5 apply. Take a session that can move at most $10,000 and a 30-day hold. If the operator sees a loss at once, as it would a withdrawal it did not expect, the deposit equal to the cap deters, and the bitcoin to post is about $13,470 on the record since 2018, or $12,410 since 2024. If the loss is found only as often as Mandiant's cases are found in 30 days, the deposit is $15,950 and the posting $21,480, or $19,790. The rule is unchanged; what changes is that the operator, not the attacker, sets the size of the prize, where the prize is money.

Let the operator decide. The attention bond of 2004 had no judge: the recipient "may, at her sole discretion, seize the bond or release it". Here the honest user bears the risk of a wrongful seizure, and that is the trade the neutral arbiter was meant to avoid. It is a small trade where the user already trusts the operator with more than the deposit: an exchange's or custodian's customers, whose balances already sit under the operator's control. For them a deposit the operator can keep adds less trust than it would for a stranger, but it adds some: a custodian owes a balance back, while an operator that rules on a forfeit keeps what it rules. Two of the reasons one bitcoin exchange removed its arbiter carry over. A stolen operator key reaches every deposit at once, and a party with a stake in the outcome can rule for itself, which is why the e-mail bond program sent forfeits to a non-profit (§1). A sole power to keep the deposit is also closer to what the model state law calls control (§5).

Or lock it and never forfeit. A deposit that is only locked for a set time costs its holder the interest, about $36 a month on $10,000 at 4.32%, plus the price risk if he counts in dollars. It removes no attacker's profit. What it does is the working-capital effect above: an attacker holding a hundred sessions open at once needs $1 million locked for as long as the gate says. That is a limit on breadth and duration, scaled to the value guarded, and it binds hardest on the low-budget operator.

None of this shortens the time it takes to find an intrusion, which is set by the attacker and the defender's monitoring, not by the gate. It does remove the judge from the cases where the user already trusts the operator, and it makes the deposit a number the operator sets from its own books.

A free allowance that prices only heavy use eases the condition for honest users who stay inside it, but holds against bulk abuse only if it is counted on something the abuser cannot multiply cheaply: an allowance per new account or per address is free volume for a spammer who opens more accounts or rotates addresses (§4).

How the limits might be overcome, and what the tests show. The limit is the honest user's wallet. The nearest primary count is the Federal Reserve's survey of American adults: in 2025, 10% used any cryptocurrency, 9% held it as an investment and 2% used it to buy something or make a payment, shares the survey says its online sample may overstate; bitcoin, and Lightning within it, can only be a part of those shares. A gate with no second route would shut out or burden about nine in ten American adults today, which is why the design keeps one, though that route stays open to the abuser at its old price unless it is an identity check. How far the wallets already built into apps would close the gap is untested. Targeted fraud, the attacker's retreat, can be priced where it starts, with the first message to someone who has never replied costing more than a reply, as the attention bond of 2004 proposed. The price that removes a targeted fraudster's profit is his chance of success times the prize: against the $20,699 average loss in the FBI's 2025 complaints, $2.07 at one success in ten thousand first messages and 21 cents at one in a hundred thousand. A well-aimed message succeeds far more often; at one in a hundred the price is $207. Honest strangers writing for the first time would pay it too, so the design holds only if the charge comes back when the recipient replies, the release the attention bond left to the recipient. On the arithmetic, the priced first message holds against fraud sent at volume and long odds; it weakens where odds are short, and the refund on reply also returns the charge to any fraudster whose target answers.

Is the system better? Deters, for those who can pay. Bulk abuse stops paying at a few cents a message, up to about five on an assumed 4.5-fold AI lift in purchases, taken from one click study. The open condition is that honest users can pay; the allowance and the second route ease it, and the second route stays open to the abuser at its old price unless it is an identity check.

A flood meant only to fill the pipe is the other kind. How the limits might be overcome, and what the tests show. The free allowance can be tied to something harder to multiply than an address, and one such key already exists. Cloudflare's signed agents, introduced in August 2025, let software agents prove who they are with a cryptographic signature, and the first cohort included ChatGPT agent and Block's Goose. An allowance per signed agent or per signed-in account cannot be reset by rotating addresses; an allowance per address can, at a fiftieth of a cent each (§4). Users behind a shared address escape the address count by signing in. Floods that never ask to be served stay outside any payment gate and with upstream filtering. Reasoned through, the remedy works for agents and people who sign their requests or sign in, and leaves anonymous floods to the address price, which the arithmetic above shows a large botnet can partly absorb.

Is the system better? Reduces. Paid crawling becomes a sale and request floods thin; floods that never ask to be served are untouched.

The attacker's adaptation is the limit. A password bought from a thief works on the first try, so it forfeits nothing; the deposit prices guessing and not theft. And where the user is known to the service, multi-factor authentication cut compromise by 99.22% in Microsoft's study of 2022 accounts. How the limits might be overcome, and what the tests show. Two remedies are already standard. NIST's password rules require a service to refuse passwords on a blocklist of commonly used, expected or compromised values, which closes the short-odds guess, and to stop consecutive failures on one account at no more than 100. The gate adds value in two places those rules leave open. The cap counts failures per account, so an attacker who sprays one guess across many accounts never meets it; there a first-attempt charge has to pass the rule on its own: 21 cents a guess at one success in a hundred thousand against the $20,699 prize, or about $207,000 to spray a million accounts, and $20.70 at one in a thousand, which an honest user on a new device would feel until the refund. And NIST warns that the cap lets an attacker lock the real user out, listing a bot challenge before login among the answers; a priced attempt serves the same aim, since running an account up to its lockout means paying a doubling price for every failure. A stolen password is met, not stopped: the refund needs a recognized device or a second factor, so a thief's deposit stays at risk through the review window, though a small deposit is little against a $20,699 prize. The gate sits alongside multi-factor authentication, not in place of it, and the reset process is gated on the same terms. On the arithmetic, the remedy holds against spraying and lockout; against a stolen password it is a charge, not a barrier.

Is the system better? Deters guessing, among users who can pay. Against a stolen password it charges one entry, from the thief's own coins, held through the review window. Where multi-factor authentication is on, the gain over it is slight; the rating applies where it is off, as in 59% of one response firm's cases, and rests on illustrative success rates and an average-loss prize, since no measured rate per guess exists. A login open to the general public, gated with no second route, would shut out or burden about nine in ten American adults, who used no cryptocurrency in 2025, and there it would be not better. The rating holds for services whose users already hold bitcoin, such as exchanges and custodians, and for staff logins the operator funds, as in use 5.

How the limits might be overcome, and what the tests show. Weighting meets the attacker who splits his capital and helps the one who locks a single large bond. Belcher's 2019 design for JoinMarket squared the amount locked, and his design notes say this gives rational participants "a strong incentive to lump up all their coin sacrifices together"; the software JoinMarket ships uses a gentler exponent, 1.3 by default. Capital split across a thousand identities carries a thousandth of the weight of one bond when the amount is squared, and about an eighth of it at 1.3, so weighting that rises faster than the amount discourages a fake crowd wherever influence follows weight, more strongly the higher the exponent. Where misconduct can be shown, as with a package later found to carry malware, a registry can add a separate forfeitable bond under published rules, with its own decision to remove a package as the judge: at $10 a package, the 149,329 malicious packages one firm found in a quarter would have cost their publishers about $1.5 million had all been caught, while honest maintainers lock $10 a package, recover it, and bear the registry's removal errors. It is a charge, not a deterrent, wherever one package's prize exceeds $10 times the chance it is caught. On the arithmetic, weighting that rises faster than the amount works against splitting and not against wealth; the forfeitable bond charges publishers where malware is found and deters only where the prize is small.

Is the system better? Reduces. Fakes cost capital in proportion to their number, and weighting that rises faster than the amount makes splitting expensive; an attacker willing to lock a great deal can still buy influence.

It fails on size. Take ransom as the prize. Verizon's 2026 data have a median payment of $139,875 with 31% of victims paying, an expected $43,361 per successful intrusion. How the limits might be overcome, and what the tests show. Each limit has a partial answer, and each can be sized. The deterring deposit is beyond honest users, so the design does not ask for it: the operator funds its own staff's deposits, and outsiders pay fees, which are final, so the operator holds no one else's money; on the house's reading, which no ruling addresses, that narrows the licensing question. For a thousand staff at $100 each, the operator locks $100,000, gives up about $4,320 a year in interest at 4.32%, and carries a fall of about $25,700 in a bad month, the one-in-twenty 30-day loss. The prize per session shrinks if the network is divided so that no one session reaches everything, and if the money any session can move is capped. Coins linked to sanctioned persons can be screened before a deposit is accepted, which OFAC's 2021 guidance describes as a best practice for virtual currency businesses; a Lightning payment shows no address to screen, so this works for deposits made on the bitcoin ledger itself. Screening catches coins already traced, not coins laundered before they are posted. A hijack from another machine is met by binding sessions to recognized devices and asking again before sensitive actions; an intruder on the recognized device itself is not, and the operator's deposit is then the one at risk. Late detection is the hardest: one response firm's cases, which include monitored clients, had a median dwell time of three days, with the decline most notable in monitored environments. If monitoring brought the median to three days, more than half of intrusions would be found within a week; the deterring deposit would then be under twice the prize and the bitcoin posting under about 2.3 times it on the record since 2018, against 2.55 on Mandiant's record. Sized where the figures allow and reasoned where they do not, the remedies make the gate cheaper to run and an attacker's attempts costlier; none makes a deterring deposit affordable against a ransom-sized prize.

Is the system better? Reduces. By the arithmetic in §4 the use would not deter, because a deposit of the deterring size is beyond honest users; on the mechanism it reduces. A smaller charge at a gate hard-coded in front of a private system would still make every attempt cost coins, though not the attacker's own if they are stolen, and make breadth need working capital (§4), thinning the low-budget attacker and leaving the best-funded and those posting stolen coins. Honest users, or the operator funding them, would pay a smaller charge, not twice the prize, but still bear the hijack, ledger-linkage and sanctions-screening costs above; no such gate is in use to measure by how much.

The table compares the two as they are documented in November 2026 and shows that bitcoin's distinct properties are a ledger secured by work and the absence of an issuer or any key over its rules, and that the dollar token's are a stable value, programmable escrow and recovery of stolen funds.

Bitcoin's distinct property is the ledger itself. A gate is a promise that the money posted at it will be there, unaltered, when the review ends. Bitcoin's ledger is secured by about 985 quintillion hashes a second of computation, and no single party holds a key that can change its rules. Its record is not spotless: in August 2010 a bug let one transaction create more than 184 billion coins, a fixed version of the software was out within five hours, and the transaction was erased when the corrected chain overtook the bad one at block 74691; in 2018 a second inflation bug was found and patched, and the developers knew of no attempt to exploit it. A dollar token's rules sit in its issuer's contract. Circle's own design document lists keys to mint and destroy tokens, to pause all transfers, to block any address and to replace the contract's code, and says "Circle will control the address of all roles"; the ledger beneath is the host chain's, secured by stake, not work. For a deposit, those keys are the issuer's to use, whatever the gate's operator wants. The ledger does not secure the gate itself: the operator's software, wallet and the arbiter's key are attack surfaces of their own, on either asset. Bisq's loss in May 2026 (§3) came through its software.

The second distinct property follows from the first: no issuer stands behind bitcoin. That matters to an operator who cannot rely on an issuer's goodwill: a service in a jurisdiction the issuer's government disfavors, a privacy tool, or any business that has watched Tether freeze $344 million in two addresses on a government's word a day before the formal designation. It also matters, in the other direction, to a depositor who wants collateral that no third party can take. For those parties bitcoin is the largest candidate.

For everyone else the dollar token is the easier instrument, and one cited advantage of it turns out to be weaker than claimed while another is stronger. The weaker one is the freeze as a gatekeeper's tool. Circle's written policy is that it "will not deny access to individual addresses" except for a threat to the token's network, a legal order, or certain urgent law-enforcement, sanctions and national-security requests, so an operator who catches an intruder cannot have his funds frozen on request. The stronger one is recovery. Tether's account of freezing $1.5 billion within 30 minutes of one theft in October 2026, and of returning about $1.2 billion in all, describes a working compensation mechanism, and bitcoin has no equivalent. Card networks, the third candidate, are ruled out by their own rules: Visa's allow a hold of at most 30 days, and that only for hotels, car rental and cruises; say an added hold "must not include any amount covering potential damage, theft"; and give the cardholder, who here is the attacker, 120 days to dispute, and up to 540 for some disputes.

The design question has a twist that favors the dollar chains. The danger of an arbiter with a key is that the key can sign anything. Circle's research arm published a contract in April 2025 that confines the arbiter to two moves, release to the recipient or refund to the payer, with a fixed deadline. Bitcoin can approximate this with pre-signed transactions and an outside party who only attests to an outcome, but the standard for that arrangement says in terms that it "does not offer protection against a party colluding with an oracle".

Put together: for fees, the rails are equivalent and distribution decides, and distribution is with the stablecoin standard. For deposits held longer than a day, stability favors dollars. Bitcoin is decisive where the security of the ledger and the absence of an issuer are the point, and the natural first users are services whose customers already hold it: exchanges, custodians, Lightning and mining businesses. A session deposit in bitcoin, for access to a system whose only contents are bitcoin, has neither the price-risk problem nor the wallet problem.

How much bitcoin

How much bitcoin would it hold?

$30 million and $205 millionthe two pools of bitcoin locked today for good behavior or payment routing, against a supply worth $1.31 trillion

The deposit design's largest claim is that demand for bitcoin would grow with the value of what it guards. Two pieces of arithmetic size it. Neither is a forecast, and nothing here implies a price.

Fees pass through. A fee is received and, by an operator with dollar costs, sold. What it holds is a float: the yearly flow times the days the receiver keeps the coins. If every dollar of the world's cyber insurance premium, about $15 billion a year, were paid instead as bitcoin fees and held for a day, the float would be $41 million, or 630 coins. Held for a month it would be $1.2 billion. Fees are a use, and add little to what is held.

Deposits sit. The bitcoin locked equals the number of deposits outstanding at one moment times their size. Exhibit 8 sets three sets of assumptions beside the pools that exist.

The pools that exist are small. Bitcoin locked as JoinMarket bonds is 460 coins, about $30 million. The whole public capacity of the Lightning network, through which any routed deposit would have to pass, is about 3,151 coins, $205 million, and has fallen from about 4,900 coins in May. Against these, Ethereum's staked ether is about $82 billion and the two large stablecoins $245 billion.

The verdict

The verdict, and what would change it

0 of 6conditions for the large case fully met; three partly met

What holds. The insight in Softwar is sound and is not Lowery's alone: an attack that costs more than it is worth does not happen, and the internet has spent thirty years looking for a way to charge that cost. A final payment that needs no bank is that way, and it did not exist before 2009. Judged by whether the system is better with the gate, net of what it costs honest users, and on reasoning from the mechanism rather than measurement, the gate would be better in each of the six uses examined. Fees just above an abuser's revenue per attempt would end the profit in bulk abuse, and a deposit forfeited on a machine-checked failure would price long-odds guessing out on illustrative success rates, both among users who can pay; fees are live in bitcoin on a small scale. The gate would reduce the damage or load of floods, fake identities and targeted intrusion into private systems behind a hard-coded gate, and may recover a small part of the harm where a forfeit is upheld.

What it still needs. Computation alone, the wall as the thesis specifies it, does not deliver the idea: puzzles fall lightly on attackers with stolen or rented machines, and the puzzles in use today need no bitcoin. Money does, and the deposit design that supplies it builds on parts dating from 2004, 2019 and 2025. It needs users who can pay, or an operator that pays for them. Against a prize of real size it requires honest users to post about twice the prize, it needs a neutral judge of the kind one bitcoin exchange began with and removed, unless its users already trust the operator with their balances, it reaches attackers who never log in only where the gate is hard-coded in front of a private system, and what it recovers is a few percent of the harm.

What it means for bitcoin. As a payment rail for fees, bitcoin is one of several and behind on distribution. As collateral for deposits, it is the largest asset on a ledger secured by work with no issuer or key over its rules, and it carries price risk (Exhibit 5); its natural home is among services and users who already hold it. The holding demand from this use is real, small, and concentrated in the cases where the rule is easiest to meet, not where the value is largest. The claim that it is Bitcoin's next large use rests on six conditions, none fully met.

Qualifications. The detection figures come from one firm's cases and overstate the chance of a forfeit. The one measured attack cost from the wild is a single vendor's interim report. No deployment exists, so the judgments about friction and behavior in §5 are reasoned, not observed. The legal position is unsettled in ways that could move either way. And the evidence on AI's effect is changing by the quarter; the United Kingdom agency's scenario paper of 3 November 2026 says of its own outlook that the prospect for surprise is high.

What would change this note's view

Toward the wider claim. The note would move its verdict from "holds in part" toward "holds" on any one of four observations, each by 31 December 2028: a production system that takes a forfeitable bitcoin deposit of $1,000 or more per session for access to a network or interface, and publishes its forfeiture record; bitcoin locked in access deposits exceeding the 460 coins locked in JoinMarket bonds today; a method of proving intrusion damage that a machine can check, in use to trigger forfeiture, which would remove the judge; or published bitcoin fee volume on L402 or the Lightning method of the Machine Payments Protocol of $1 million a day or more.

Toward dismissal. The note would move toward "does not hold" on any one of four observations by the same date: a stablecoin deposit for access in production with at least 10,000 deposits or $10 million locked, published by its operator, while no bitcoin one exists; the stablecoin standard's cumulative published transaction count at least tripling from 138 million while bitcoin's standards publish no volume or less than $1 million a day; no production bitcoin access deposit existing and no bitcoin fee volume published; or a production bitcoin gate losing deposits through its own software or keys.

On the need, in either direction. Evidence on the attacks themselves moves Exhibit 9's sixth condition, not the verdict on its own. If the next annual reports from Microsoft and Verizon both show password and credential attacks rising, that condition becomes met; if both show them falling, it becomes not met. A second independent measurement of the cost of an AI-run attack in the wild counts the same way: tens of dollars per target supports the condition, thousands or more per target counts against it.

On the ratings. A published before-and-after count at a service that adds a bitcoin fee or deposit moves Exhibit 6, not the headline: attempts down by nine in ten or more with no rise in damage per attack support "deters" for that use, taking the fall in attempts as the observable for an attack that no longer pays, and down by half or more "reduces"; attempts down by less than half move it to "slight"; attempts unchanged, or a gate incident that costs honest users more than the attempts removed, move it to "not better".

What would not change it. The price of bitcoin, in either direction. A further official statement that Bitcoin has security applications, without a system named. Another laboratory result on what a model can do in a test.
