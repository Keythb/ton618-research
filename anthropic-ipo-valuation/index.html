Anthropic (ANT) — What the S-1 Says a Dollar of Claude Is Worth

Anthropic filed to go public on October 26, 2026. The filing shows a company whose revenue rose from $4.6 billion in 2025 to $16.3 billion in the first half of 2026, whose second quarter was the first with an operating profit, and which costs about 15 cents to serve for each dollar it sells. It also shows about $657 billion of named compute commitments landing fastest in 2027–29, a founders' share that carries 50.1% of every general vote, and a trust with no stock that elects a majority of the board. Fair value $50.72 a share, probability-weighted, $833 billion: 14% below the $58.90 price of the last private round and less than half of the $2 trillion the wires discuss.

On the morning of Monday, October 26, Anthropic, PBC filed its registration statement with the SEC for a listing on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the ticker ANT, with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley leading. There is no price range, no share count and no figure for the quarter that ended in September; those arrive by amendment. What the filing does give is three audited years and a half-year: revenue of $41 million, $386 million and $4,585 million in 2023–25, then $16,286 million in the six months to June 2026; a $693 million operating loss for that half that turned into a $974 million operating profit in its second quarter; $131.7 billion of cash and short-term investments; and a net loss of $80.7 billion, of which $72.4 billion is the accounting mark on convertible notes that rose in value because the company did. The last private round, Series H in May and June, was priced at $589.0095 per share before a ten-for-one split, or $58.90 after it, about $968 billion for the whole company on the count this note uses. This note asks what the business the filing describes is worth, what the company has promised to spend to build it, and who decides.

The questions

What this note answers

  1. How did Anthropic get here, and what does it say it is? The founding, the public-benefit purpose, the trust, the model line to Mythos and Fable, and the turn to enterprise.
  2. What is the revenue, really? Growth from $4.6 billion to a $46 billion run-rate, what the blank third quarter implies, who pays and through whom, and circularity as a number.
  3. What does a dollar of Claude cost to serve? The serving margin the filing does not print, built from its own functional table.
  4. What has it promised to spend? Every named compute commitment, by counterparty and by year, against revenue and cash.
  5. Who owns it and who controls it? The share count reconciled, economics against votes, the founders' share and the trust.
  6. What is the moat and what erodes it? Cadence, gating, four chip families, three clouds, open weights and price.
  7. What do the law and the regulator want? The government directive, the export restriction, the FTC, the copyright docket.
  8. How long does it grow faster than normal? Each line's stage, argued from its driver, with the length the price needs.
  9. What is it worth? A multi-stage cash-flow valuation, the three cases, the sensitivities, what each anchor price requires, and the peers.
  10. What would change our view? Dated, observable conditions in both directions, and the two update points.

Terms used throughout, defined once. S-1: the registration statement a company files to sell shares to the public; DRS: the confidential drafts filed before it. Consumption revenue: usage billed by the token (the filing's "pay-for-intelligence"); subscription revenue: prepaid seats (Pro, Max, Team, Enterprise). Compute: the processors, memory, networking and power used to train and run models; the filing books all of it as one operating expense. Cost of revenue: the filing's functional line for the compute and overhead that serve customers; the serving margin is one minus cost of revenue over revenue. CSP: a cloud services provider (Amazon, Google, Microsoft), which here is investor, compute vendor, sales channel and customer at once. Run-Rate Revenue: the company's annualized measure (13 times 28 days of consumption plus 12 times monthly subscriptions). RPO: remaining performance obligations, contracted revenue not yet recognized. NRR: net revenue retention, this year's revenue from last year's customers over last year's. PBC: a Delaware public benefit corporation, whose directors must balance stockholders against a stated public purpose. LTBT: the Long-Term Benefit Trust, five unpaid-in-stock trustees who hold one Class T share and elect a board majority. Class F: one share held by the founders' LLC with 50.1% of the general vote. Class N: non-voting shares held by strategic investors, the Amazon notes and the Public Benefit Fund. FCFF: free cash flow to the firm, the cash the business generates before financing. Cost of equity: the return shareholders require, built here as the ten-year Treasury yield plus beta times an equity risk premium. Terminal growth: the growth rate assumed forever after the explicit forecast. Reverse DCF: solving the model backward for what a given price requires.

The other side, first

The case for the price

The strongest case that the Series H price of $58.90, and the $1.5–2 trillion the wires discuss, is right, in the terms its proponents would use.

  1. No company has ever grown like this, and it is still accelerating in dollars. Revenue went from $41 million to $4.6 billion in two years and to $16.3 billion in the next six months; the second quarter alone was $11.6 billion, up 144% on the first. Customers above $100,000 of annual revenue went from 950 to 11,900 in a year; 250 customers pay more than $10 million; net revenue retention is about 900%. Every company in the Fortune 10 and over half the Fortune 500 are customers, and they spend on average less than 0.1% of their expenses on Claude, so the runway inside existing accounts is enormous. A model that puts 2027 growth at 114% is extrapolating deceleration the data has not yet shown.
  2. The unit economics are software's, not a utility's. Serving a dollar of revenue cost 15 cents in the first half of 2026, down from 60 cents in 2025; revenue per dollar of total compute went from 0.4 to 2.1 in five quarters; Opus 5.5 cut the cost per task by about 40% against Opus 5 and the company says it delivers such reductions "while preserving our margins." The adjusted operating margin went from −221% to +19% in six quarters. The founders' letter calls the $6.6 million of revenue per employee "meaningfully higher than the average for the largest public U.S. companies."
  3. The commitments are a demand forecast made by the people with the best view of demand. The company says it has "consistently operated in a compute-constrained environment where customer demand exceeds available compute." Multi-year contracts are procured 12 to 18 months ahead, so the ladder is what the company expects to sell, bought at the best available price through four chip families. Reuters reported in August that the IPO case rests on a $190–200 billion revenue forecast for 2028; the house base case is 15% below it.
  4. The balance sheet and the partners remove financing risk. $131.7 billion of cash and short-term investments at June 30, $77 billion of undrawn facilities from Google, Amazon and Broadcom, a $15 billion revolver, AMD's commitment to buy up to $5 billion of stock, Google's option to buy $10 billion at the IPO, and whatever the IPO itself raises. No frontier lab has ever had this much capital in hand.
  5. Safety is the enterprise moat, not a tax on it. The company's position is that "safety, alignment, and ethical behavior resonate particularly strongly with enterprises." Ramp's card data had 43.8% of US businesses paying Anthropic against 40.6% paying OpenAI in September 2026, and 60% of token spend in October; the filing cites every Fortune 10 company. The gating of Mythos cost "substantial revenue" and about $100 million of credits, by the founders' own account, and the company grew 144% in the quarter it happened.
  6. The cost of equity is too high. At a 10% cost of equity, which is what the house uses for SpaceX, the same three cases give $74 a share, above the Series H price; the proponent's own inputs, bull growth with a 10% rate and a seven-year stage, give $168.
  7. Control is a feature. The structure the public is being asked to accept is the structure that produced the growth: the founders' LLC and the trust were designed so that the company could hold back a model when it judged it must, and the company's record is that such decisions have not stopped it compounding.

Of these, this note tests:

  • (1) in §2 and §8, where the blank third quarter is bounded from the filing's own text and the stage growth rates are set against the company's reported 2028 forecast and the base rates of growth persistence;
  • (2) in §3, where the serving margin is built from the functional table and the research compute is separated from it;
  • (3) in §4 and §9, where the ladder is scheduled by year and run as a cash floor in every case, so the reader can see what it costs when demand lags it;
  • (4) in §4, where the facilities' strings (conversion at a discount, revenue conditions, ownership caps) are set out;
  • (5) in §6, where the share data, its basis and its gaps are stated, and in §10, where the gating claim has a falsifier;
  • (6) in §9, where every result is shown at 10%, 11%, 12.25% and 13%, and the proponent's case is run on its own inputs.

It cannot test:

  • (7) Whether the control structure will produce good decisions; it is a governance fact the note describes and prices only through the discount rate.
  • Whether the company's 2028 forecast is its own; Reuters reported it, the filing does not state it.
  • What the IPO will be priced at or how many shares will be sold; both are blank, and both are update points.
How it got here

How Anthropic got here, and what it says it is

Anthropic was incorporated in Delaware in January 2021 as a public benefit corporation by seven researchers who had led GPT-3's engineering, the scaling-laws work, RLHF and interpretability research at OpenAI. Its charter's stated purpose is "the responsible development and maintenance of advanced AI for the long-term benefit of humanity," and the filing calls the company "first and foremost a research lab." It launched commercially in the first quarter of 2023 with 43% of that year's $41 million of revenue from one customer, shipped the Claude 3 family in 2024, Claude Code as a research preview in February 2025, and Claude Cowork in January 2026. Today the model line has four classes: Mythos (with Fable), Opus, Sonnet and Haiku. Mythos was first released in April 2026 as a gated research preview through Project Glasswing, a program for vetted cybersecurity work; Fable 5, the generally available configuration "with our production safeguards, which block or limit performance on certain sensitive topics," followed in June, with Mythos 5 for Glasswing participants; Mythos 5.1 and Fable 5.1 shipped in September.

The scale as of September 30, 2026: more than 11,900 customers paying over $100,000 a year, 1,900 over $1 million and about 250 over $10 million; roughly 40 million smaller customers; every company in the Fortune 10, over half the Fortune 500 and over 30% of the Global 2000. Revenue per employee is "over $6.6 million," which with the filing's 170% headcount growth puts the staff at about 5,100–5,200. Over 90% of the contributions to the company's own codebase in September 2026 were written by Claude. Regrettable attrition was about 1% over the trailing year, and all seven founders remain.

What it says it is appears most plainly in the eight-page founders' letter inside the filing. It states two categories of decision that "may run counter to investors' expectations": "forward investments (especially in compute) that may seem irresponsibly large at first glance," defended by the scaling laws and a comparison to pharmaceutical research, and "decisions that depart from profit maximization." Its two examples are the ones this note prices: holding Mythos Preview back from general release "meant forgoing substantial revenue and ceding ground to competitors (we also gave away around $100 million in Claude credits to cyber defenders)," and "we have also committed to keeping Claude free of advertising," which "would likely be a large source of revenue for us." It adds that "there may be times when we choose to advance more slowly than we could in order to make our systems safer, even if that puts us at a competitive disadvantage." The letter's own advice on the offering: "never let your company be worth a dollar more than it is actually worth."

The revenue

What the revenue is, really

Exhibit 1 — Revenue against compute, by quarter

How to read it. Each quarter from the first of 2025 to the second of 2026 has two bars in billions of dollars: gold is revenue, grey is compute and infrastructure expense for every workload. The number under each pair is revenue divided by compute, the dollars of revenue each dollar of compute produced that quarter. The dashed bar at the right is the third quarter of 2026, which the public filing leaves blank: its height is the $16–17 billion range the filing's own text implies. The column at the right lists the company's adjusted operating margin by quarter. Compare the gold bar's rise with the grey bar's; the one thing it shows is that revenue grew faster than compute in every quarter of 2026, which is why the loss closed.

Revenue was $376 million, $787 million, $1,382 million and $2,040 million in the four quarters of 2025, then $4,731 million and $11,555 million in the first two of 2026. Consumption, billed by the token, is 80% of the mix; subscriptions 20%; the United States 72%. The third quarter is blank in the public filing, but two sentences pin it: trailing-twelve-month revenue "increased approximately 1,200%" from September 2025 to September 2026, and revenue per employee went from $1.4 million to over $6.6 million while headcount grew about 170%. With the undisclosed fourth quarter of 2024 bounded at $97–174 million, the growth sentence gives $16.0–17.0 billion for the third quarter of 2026 and $34–35 billion of trailing revenue (the revenue-per-employee route gives $15.3–16.3 billion, and the "approximately" in both sentences can widen either); the Financial Times, via Reuters, reported $16.8 billion on October 15, inside the range. Note what the range implies: growth of 144% from the first quarter to the second, then 39–47% from the second to the third. The company's own run-rate metric was more than $47 billion "earlier in May"; Bloomberg reported $65 billion at the end of July, a figure the company has not published.

Who pays. Customers above $100,000 a year produced 74% of first-half revenue ($12.0 billion); the 40 million smaller customers the rest. Four customers have at times exceeded 10% of revenue: Customer A (12% of 2025, 13% of receivables), Customer B (12% of 2025, 18% of the first half of 2025), Customer C (43% of 2023, 11% of the first half of 2026) and Customer D (11% of 2023). All are unnamed, and the filing does not say whether any is an investor or a compute vendor. No customer exceeded 10% in the nine months to September, and 79% of third-quarter revenue came from outside the top five. Remaining performance obligations were $10.8 billion at June 30, about 75% of it due within a year: a backlog of about one quarter of a year's revenue at the second-quarter pace. The book is short, and the filing says so: most revenue is consumption, and "a growing number" of enterprise customers sign non-cancelable annual or multi-year minimums.

Two deductions the filing makes itself. "Approximately 5% to 7%" of revenue for the nine months to September came from customers later found to be violating the usage policy or commercial terms; enforcement tightened in August and September and the company expects the share to fall. And "for the two weeks ended January 2, 2026, total customer spend declined by approximately 20%," the seasonality the company expects to show as growth slows. Models released in the six months to September produced over 80% of third-quarter revenue: the revenue rides the release cadence.

Circularity, as a number. The filing names no customer, so revenue from the cloud companies that also own, supply and distribute Anthropic cannot be measured. The other three legs can. Money in: Amazon and Google have put about $41 billion into Anthropic's notes and preferred stock through June 2026 (Amazon about $18 billion, Google about $23 billion), with $35 billion of undrawn facilities and Google's $10 billion option at the IPO. Money out: at least $114.5 billion committed to Google for compute, an estimated $125–150 billion more of TPU capacity "from TPU providers, including Google," $161.2 billion of Broadcom lease rent for TPU systems, and at least $110.0 billion committed to Amazon. For every dollar the two have invested, Anthropic has committed to spend about $5.50 with them on compute, more than $8 with the TPU estimate. Money through: 48% of first-half revenue was sold through the three clouds' marketplaces, where the CSP collects and remits and Anthropic pays a channel fee; sales, marketing and partnerships, mostly those fees, is the fastest-growing cost line at 19% of revenue. Revenue from the CSPs as customers: undisclosed; the filing says they "are also our customers" and that their "purchasing decisions may be influenced by their own competing offerings." The filing's own risk factor calls these relationships "complex dynamics that could give rise to conflicts of interest."

The cost to serve

What a dollar of Claude costs to serve

Exhibit 2 — Where the compute goes

How to read it. Four stacked bars, one for 2024, 2025 and each first half, measured as a share of that period's revenue up the side (capped at 100%). The gold segment is cost of revenue, which the filing says consists "substantially all" of the compute that serves customers; the grey segment above it is the rest of the compute bill, the upper bound for research, model development and internal use. The label above each bar is total compute as a share of revenue; the label below is the serving margin, one minus the gold share. Compare the gold segments across the bars; the one thing it shows is that serving a dollar of revenue cost 15 cents in the first half of 2026 against 60 cents in 2025, and that most compute is spent not on serving but on research.

The filing has no gross-margin line: compute is one operating expense that "supports the full range of workloads," and the company says it plans and assesses it "on a total basis." Its functional table gives the serving margin anyway. Cost of revenue, which "substantially all" consists of compute for customer-serving workloads plus some personnel and overhead, was $752 million on $386 million of revenue in 2024, $2,773 million on $4,585 million in 2025 (a 40% serving margin) and $2,380 million on $16,286 million in the first half of 2026 (85%). Compute as a whole was $7,326 million in 2025 and $8,945 million in the half; by quarter it fell from 251% of revenue to 48%. So serving was 38% of the compute bill in 2025 and 27% in the half; the rest, $6.6 billion in six months, went to research, development and internal use, the cost of staying at the frontier. The channel fee is the other cost that scales with revenue: sales, marketing and partnerships, mostly those fees, rose to 19% of revenue in the half from 12% in 2025, $2.7 billion of the increase being fees to the cloud marketplaces.

What the filing says happens next is the hinge of the valuation: "As significant incremental compute is brought online in future periods, the associated expense will be recognized in advance of the full revenue that the compute will ultimately support, and we expect operating margin and Adjusted Operating Margin may compress in those periods." Compute is contracted 12 to 18 months ahead, is "generally fixed in the near term," and "compute and infrastructure expenses as a percentage of revenue will depend primarily on how our revenue scales relative to our total committed compute cost base." Short-term compute, at a higher unit cost, is expected to rise in the near term. The 85% serving margin is real; the operating margin is the ladder in §4 against the revenue in §2.

The other lines: personnel $3,152 million in the half, of which $2,185 million was stock compensation (13% of revenue) and $967 million cash (6%); other operating and administrative $1,714 million (11%), including the donation-matching charges that reached $2.0 billion in the third quarter alone. Unrecognized stock compensation stood at $26.6 billion on restricted stock units (of which $2.9 billion relates to units whose service condition is satisfied or partially satisfied) plus $2.8 billion on options, with $5.8 billion more granted after June. The model expenses stock compensation, at 10% of revenue in 2027 falling to 6%, and does not also dilute for it.

The company describes the reason: it is "building out our own compute and infrastructure and vertically integrating across the compute supply chain," under a program it calls Anthropic Direct that leases chips and hosts them in dedicated facilities; its first data center opened this year.

How it is paid for, and the strings. Beyond the $131.7 billion of cash: Google's $20 billion facility to December 2030 (draws tied to third-party compute agreements and revenue targets, settled in Class N shares at the 30-day average price, a 14.99% ownership cap, reduced if Google buys $10 billion at the IPO); Amazon's $15 billion for 30 months after the IPO, in $5 billion steps as capacity milestones are met, with a 12% cap; Broadcom's $42 billion solely to pay rent under the 2026 lease, at 9% paid in kind and convertible at 90% of the average price, rising to 13% and 85% "during a period in which certain revenue performance conditions are not met," with 150% of principal in cash on a change of control; AMD's up to $5 billion of stock; a $15 billion revolver with a $5 billion minimum-liquidity covenant from the fourth quarter of 2026; and the IPO, for which the wires say up to $100 billion and the filing says nothing. The parent guarantees its two leasing subsidiaries; up to $0.8 billion of data-center guarantees and a $500 million power-contract guarantee sit beside them. The filing says the build-out will "be a significant use of net cash in operating and investing activities for the foreseeable future" and that "net cash used in these activities may increase regardless of whether our financial performance improves."

In the model, compute cost in each year is the larger of a target share of revenue and the cash floor of the ladder, delivery-adjusted by case: the firm contracts (Microsoft, Google, Amazon, the Broadcom leases) in full in every case; SpaceXAI in full through 2027 and then in full, at 85%, or not at all (bull, base, bear) because either party can end it on 90 days' notice; the capacity-dependent agreements (Nscale, Lambda, AMD) in full, at 85%, or not at all; the TPU estimate in full, in full, or at half. The floor binds in 2027–29 in every case and through 2031 in the bear case. In the base case the company loses $24 billion, $47 billion and $17 billion in 2027–29 as the ladder lands, then earns 19% in 2030 and 23% in 2031. In the bear case it loses money through 2031 and burns about $156 billion in 2027–31, more than its cash: that is the case in which the facilities, the revolver and the IPO proceeds are not optional. The floor costs $5.22 a share, weighted, against a model that lets compute be a share of revenue.

Ownership and control

Who owns it and who controls it

Exhibit 4 — Economics against votes, at the IPO

How to read it. Three bars, each the full width: the top is economic ownership of the shares that will exist at the IPO before any new shares are sold, the middle is the vote on matters put to stockholders generally, the bottom is board seats. Gold is Class A held by everyone but the founders, dark gold the founders' own Class A, pale grey the non-voting Class N, black the Class F share (one share, 50.1% of the vote) and the Class T share (one share, four of seven directors). Read each bar left to right as shares of 100%; the one thing it shows is that the public's class holds 58% of the economics, under half of the vote, and elects a minority of the board.

The count. The income statement's weighted average of 2,025 million shares counts only the common. At the IPO the preferred (8,559 million as converted, after the ten-for-one split), the Amazon notes (1,840 million Class N at fixed prices of $2.09 and $5.05, converting "at the lower of" those and the IPO price, within Amazon's 12% cap), the options (1,797 million at an average exercise price of $2.89), the restricted stock units (1,050 million, which vest on effectiveness) and the Public Benefit Fund's 847 million Class N shares join the 2,188 million common outstanding: 16,281 million economic shares, or 16,427 million with the 146 million issued after June (new unit grants, the October donation-matching settlement, two acquisitions). That is the count this note divides by; it reconciles to the filing's own "fully diluted" basis of 16,937 million once the unissued plan reserves are added (847 million is 5.00% of it). Not counted: the IPO's new shares, Google's $10 billion placement, AMD's $5 billion and any facility draws, all blank or future. The Series H price of $58.90 is $968 billion on this count, the "$965 billion" reported in May.

The wedge. Class A (one vote; 9,410 million at the IPO) holds 57.8% of the economics but at most 49.9% of the general vote, by construction. Class N (6,871 million) holds 42.2% of the economics and no vote; it is the strategic investors, the Amazon notes and the fund, and converts to Class A only on transfer to an unaffiliated holder. Class F, one share held by the founders' LLC, carries 50.1% of every general vote, a share the filing says "will not be impacted by future issuances of our capital stock that may cause economic or voting dilution." Class T, one share held by the LTBT, elects the Class T directors, "a majority of the authorized members of our board of directors"; the founders' letter puts it as four of seven. The company is a Nasdaq "controlled company" by virtue of the trust. What a Class A holder can decide on matters put to stockholders generally: nothing the Founder LLC opposes, and not the board majority.

The trust and the sunset. The LTBT is a Delaware purpose trust of five trustees, one seat vacant: Neil Shah (chair; also an initial manager of the Public Benefit Fund), Richard Fontaine, Ben Bernanke (since June) and Christopher Stubbs (since October), each paid $400,000 a year by the company, which funded the trust with $8.4 million this year. Trustees may hold no stock; they choose directors and "neither the LTBT nor the Trustees have decision-making authority in the day-to-day management or strategy setting." The trust's terms change only by 80% of the board and 80% of the trustees, or, over the trustees' objection, the founders' LLC plus a majority of Class A plus a vote of an employee class (Class E) to be issued, or 85% of Class A if the founders' share is gone. The founders' share is redeemed 450 days after fewer than three "Continuing Founders" remain for 90 consecutive days, or earlier if a supermajority of the continuing founders agrees to redeem it or the Founder LLC dissolves; a founder stops counting on resignation, death, removal for cause, an approved leave, or holding less than 2% of what they held at the IPO. Seven today; the structure survives the loss of four. The draft-by-draft record shows the structure was assembled during the SEC process: no Class F existed in the June 1 draft; it entered on July 10; Class E, the amendment threshold and a first founder sunset on August 10; the 90-day test and the 450-day transition in the public filing.

Who benefits most. The non-voting strategic class holds 42% of the economics, Amazon and Google first among them (their stakes are not disclosed; the "21% and 15%" in the press is reporting). The seven founders hold 10.4% of the economics, 1,686 million Class A shares, and have pledged 80% of it to philanthropy (9% of fully diluted shares, the letter says) while holding, through one share, a majority of every general vote. The public holds Class A economics and a vote that cannot decide. The Public Benefit Fund holds 5%, and the board may issue up to 2% more each time the share price closes 50% above the last milestone for 90 of 120 trading days, starting from the IPO price. Prices paid, per post-split share: $3.00 (2024), $5.61 (early 2025), $14.10 (September 2025), $25.91 (February 2026, and the April employee tender), $58.90 (May–June 2026).

The Amazon notes, and the loss they explain. Amazon's $6.2 billion of convertible notes converts at the IPO into 1,840 million Class N shares at $2.09 and $5.05 a share, either side of the $3.00 paid in the 2024 round, 11% of the pre-offering economic count, subject to Amazon's 12% ownership cap, with any excess rolling over and converting quarterly at the lowest of the fixed price, the IPO price and the ten-day volume-weighted average price. At the Series H price those shares are worth about $108.4 billion, against the $108.6 billion the balance sheet carries the notes at; the $72.4 billion charge in the first half is the notes' fair value rising with the company's valuation, from $41.7 billion at the start of the year, with $5.5 billion of notes converting along the way. In the house's reading it is a transfer inside the capitalization table rather than a cost of the business: the operating loss in the half was $0.7 billion, and the second quarter earned an operating profit of $974 million. The charge stops on the portion of the notes that converts; any balance the 12% cap leaves as rollover notes keeps being marked until it converts.

The moat and the gate

The moat, and what erodes it

The filing's own evidence that frontier position is the moat: models released in the six months to September produced over 80% of third-quarter revenue; "with each release, we have seen most customers utilize the latest version"; 250 model versions, features and applications launched in nine months; the cyber benchmark ExploitBench went from 40% (Opus 4.8) to 78% (Mythos 5) to 91% (Opus 5.5) in about four months. Each model helps build the next ("closing the loop"), and Claude writes over 90% of the company's own code. Four chip families (AWS Trainium, Google TPU, NVIDIA through Microsoft, SpaceXAI, Nscale and Lambda, AMD from 2027) run under an orchestration layer the company says "took years and significant investment to build" and "reduces dependence on any single vendor." Distribution runs through all three clouds, systems integrators, over 150,000 certified partner practitioners, and the case studies in the filing (Salesforce: over 60% of 83,000 employees on Claude, output per developer up 151%; Mercado Libre: 40% of 2025 production code; Verizon 33,000 engineers; Lilly 50,000 seats). Ramp's card data had 43.8% of US businesses paying Anthropic against 40.6% paying OpenAI in September 2026, and 60% of token spend in October against 36%; a card-spend sample, not the market.

The same cadence has a brake its owner controls. Mythos was held to a gated preview because of "its advanced capabilities in cyber vulnerability discovery"; Fable ships with safeguards that route some requests "to a less capable Claude model"; the company "recently chose to delay pre-training a new frontier base model for more than a month" for weight security; and in June 2026 the Commerce Department "imposed worldwide export restrictions on our Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models, which we disabled for all customers until those restrictions were lifted." The filing says future models "exhibiting such capabilities may be subject to access constraints" and that the commercial viability of gated programs "may depend on sustained governmental and industry coalition support." This is the one moat whose owner has said in writing it will narrow it when it judges it must.

What erodes it: price, first. On Ramp's token-spend sample, the blended price paid for Anthropic tokens fell 19% in a month to $0.80 per million in the week to October 11; open-weight models price below it (DeepSeek V4 Flash at $0.14 and $0.28 per million tokens in and out, about a quarter of that blended price; GLM-5.2 at $1.40 and $4.40 and Qwen 3.6 at about $0.33 and $1.95 are not cheaper than that blended price on the output leg); the BenchLM index has frontier prices up 61% in a year and mid-tier prices down 41%, the split Anthropic's four-class pricing exploits and open weights attack from below. The filing concedes the mechanism in its own words: open-weight models "could exert pricing pressure on our offerings", and customers "may conclude that incremental performance gains at the frontier do not justify the additional cost". So far the threat is to price, not to share: on Ramp's adoption data from more than 85,000 US businesses, 0.3% paid DeepSeek in September 2026 against 43.8% paying Anthropic, and the model carries the threat as the compute share of revenue, 60% in 2027 in the bear case. Second, the clouds that distribute Claude also "develop and deploy competing AI models," and the distribution deals "give them visibility into our pricing and commercial terms." Third, OpenAI (a reported $852 billion mark in March, seeking about $1.4 trillion, listing "not 2026") and Google. Fourth, talent that can "join or found competing companies immediately."

Law and regulators

What the law and the regulator want

The directors of a Delaware PBC must balance stockholders' pecuniary interests, the interests of those materially affected, and the stated purpose; "there is limited judicial precedent." On February 27, 2026, "in response to our publicly stated position that our technology should not be used for mass domestic surveillance or fully autonomous weapons," the President directed federal agencies to stop using the company's technology and the Department of War designated it a supply-chain risk; the company won most of its case in the Northern District of California on August 27, lost 2-1 in the D.C. Circuit on September 25, and has asked for rehearing. Government revenue is not quantified. The June export restriction (above) "could recur." The FTC issued a civil investigative demand in October on consumer protection, privacy "as well as the safety and security of our products," and Axios reported on October 26 that the FTC has demanded testimony from Anthropic executives. The external evaluators the company indemnifies, "without a cap on our liability" in some cases, are unnamed in the filing; anthropic.com named a six-person METR team with employee-like access on October 15. The copyright docket: Bartz settled for $1.5 billion (approved July; $1.2 billion still accrued at June 30), the first Concord case was argued on summary judgment on October 21, a second wave of music-publisher suits names two executives, Reddit's contract claims survive, four Canadian actions and the Bartz opt-outs continue; none is quantified. The company endorsed California's SB 53, tests with the US and UK AI safety institutes, and faces EU general-purpose-model enforcement from August 2, 2026. The filing mentions lobbying once and gives no figure. The company's own reports to the Senate under the Lobbying Disclosure Act show federal lobbying spend rising from $0.36 million in the first quarter of 2025 to $2.11 million in the third quarter of 2026 ($1.56 and $1.97 million in the two quarters before it, with one dip in the fourth quarter of 2025), with nine lobbyists named in the latest report and defense, energy and consumer safety among the issues listed; reported giving to a political group this year is $40 million.

The stages

How long each line grows faster than normal

Every length below is an argument a reader can dispute; the sensitivities in §9 give the value of each alternative. The value

Method. Free cash flow to the firm over ten explicit years (2027–36) on a 2026 stub built from the first-half actuals and the implied third quarter, discounted at mid-year, with one cost of capital in every case, stock compensation expensed, terminal value by Gordon growth, cash of $131.7 billion plus a $2 billion house estimate of second-half cash generation added, the $1.2 billion Bartz accrual and $0.4 billion of finance leases deducted, divided by 16,427 million shares; the terminal value is discounted from the end of 2036 (discounting it from mid-year too would add $1.85 a share, $52.58). A dividend model has nothing to discount; free cash flow to equity was negative in every year through 2025 and the capital structure changes at the IPO; a multiple on revenue growing 1,300% is a growth assumption in disguise, so it is a cross-check below, never blended.

Cost of capital 12.25%. The ten-year Treasury at 5.00% on October 23 plus a beta of 1.51 times the house's 4.8% equity risk premium; the beta is the midpoint of the hyperscalers' median of 1.24 (Microsoft 1.09, Alphabet 1.15, Amazon 1.32, Oracle 1.58) and the AI pure plays' 1.78 (NVIDIA 1.58, Palantir 1.99, CoreWeave 1.56, Nebius 2.22), two-year weekly betas against the S&P 500 after the Blume adjustment; no interest-bearing debt survives the IPO beyond any Amazon rollover notes held back by the 12% cap, which convert quarterly, and $0.4 billion of finance leases. What would change it

What would change our view

Two update points are pre-registered. First, the amendment that sets the price range and share count: the house re-bases every "versus price" line on the midpoint, adds the primary shares, Google's $10 billion placement if exercised and the vested unit settlement to the count, fills the lock-up ladder's share counts, reads the September-30 commitments table against the ladder in §4 (the first test of the house schedule) and the third-quarter revenue against the $16.0–17.0 billion range, and publishes the gap. Second, the first-day close: one post-print-style update, not a new note, with the close against fair value, the day-one float and days-to-trade. A valuation on the filing day rests on blanks the amendment fills and on a path the first prints test; the number should be expected to move at each update point, and the direction it moves is itself information for the reader.

The house view moves up, toward the price or past it, if:

  • (a) the third or fourth quarter of 2026, when printed, exceeds $17.0 billion or $23.8 billion, the bull case's quarters; the base case is rebuilt on the bull case's 2027 growth. Silence: no quarterly figures by the amendment is scored as not met.
  • (b) the September-30 commitments table shows less than $92.7 billion due in 2027, the house ladder's year; the floor is lowered to the filed figure in every case. Silence: a still-blank table is not met.
  • (c) compute as a share of revenue in the first two public quarters prints below 45%; the base path's 52% for 2027 is cut to the printed figure. Silence: no compute line is not met.
  • (d) the ten-year Treasury averages below 4.5% for a full quarter; the cost of equity falls half a point, about $5 a share. Prices always print.

The house view moves down, further from the price, if:

  • (e) the third or fourth quarter prints below $16.0 billion or $17.0 billion, the bear case's quarters. Silence as in (a).
  • (f) the September-30 table shows more than $160 billion due in 2029, the house's heaviest year, or an aggregate committed spend above $1.0 trillion; the floor is raised. Silence: not met.
  • (g) the 2027 operating margin prints below −22%, the base case's figure, or the company draws the Broadcom rent notes on the 13% and 85% terms that apply when "revenue performance conditions are not met," which must be disclosed. Silence: not met.
  • (h) the supply-chain-risk designation survives rehearing and government revenue is disclosed above 5% of the total, or a second export restriction on a current model lasts more than 30 days. Silence: no disclosure of government revenue is not scored.
  • (i) the Founder LLC's share is used to carry a transaction the Class A majority opposes, or the Class T directors remove the chief executive without the Founder LLC's concurrence: a governance test, not a model input; the control section is rewritten.

Two claims in the case for the price carry their own falsifiers. The claim that model gating is a revenue brake is falsified if the next Mythos-class release reaches general availability within 60 days of its gated preview with no disclosed revenue effect. The claim that the ladder is a demand forecast is falsified if the company discloses the offloading or resale of committed compute, which the founders' letter says it will seek "if we overestimate demand."
