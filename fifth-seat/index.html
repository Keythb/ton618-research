The Fifth Seat

What's Really Happening With NVIDIA's $500 Billion Compute-Financing Machine — and Who Ends Up Holding the Risk

TON618 Capital Research · As of August 14, 2026 · NVDA $225.30 close, August 13, 2026 · market cap ≈$5.46T

A follow-up to The Losing Bets That Built the World. Balance-sheet figures are read from NVIDIA's Form 10-Q as filed; CDS levels are press-reported prints, not terminal-verified, and are labeled as such throughout.

The finding · five seats, one machine

§1 The Verdict

13×the growth of NVIDIA's private-investment book, year over year, while it arranged $500B of other people's capital
NVIDIA is not selling its risk. It is recruiting co-investors so it can run a bigger book. The August 10 financing platforms create a seat at the table no prior infrastructure wave has had — the originator, who earns on the risk without holding it — and the end of the pipe, via investment-grade NAIC designations into insurance general accounts, is the American annuity holder, who will be long the two things nobody can price: GPU residual value and take-or-pay counterparty credit. On the first of those, NVIDIA has already agreed to write a partial floor — residual-value support of up to 25% of a deal — which is to say it has sold a put on the obsolescence its own roadmap schedules.

The risk-offload read — that NVIDIA built this machine to get AI-cycle exposure off its own balance sheet before the music stops — fails on the filings. A seller reduces exposure. NVIDIA grew its non-marketable investment book from $3.2 billion to $42.3 billion in four quarters — $17.9 billion added in the April quarter alone, with $27 billion more already committed through fiscal 2027 — before arranging $500 billion of third-party capital. The motive is not balance-sheet fear; it is that NVIDIA's binding constraint has migrated. Not fab capacity. Not end demand. Its customers' cost of capital. Labs and neoclouds cannot fund a ~$750-billion-a-year buildout from cash flow, so the supplier is manufacturing the financing channel that keeps its own order book — $119 billion of supply commitments, $95 billion of it due within this fiscal year — clearing. Risk transfer is the mechanism. Demand generation is the motive. And the collateral's largest scheduled loss-driver is NVIDIA's own product roadmap.

As of this morning we can also name the positions. NVIDIA's Form 13F-HR, filed August 14 for the quarter ended June 30, discloses $63.4 billion across eight reportable holdings — a different population from the balance-sheet categories above and two months later, so not a like-for-like comparison, but the first public naming of the counterparties. Intel is the largest at $30.0 billion, and the share count settles what that is: 214,776,632 shares, exactly the $5.0 billion NVIDIA committed at $23.28 in September 2025 — a 6.0× gain, $25.0 billion unrealized, on one position. Then SpaceX at $21.0 billion. And then the ones that matter for this note: CoreWeave at $4.7 billion and Nebius at $0.3 billion — equity in the neoclouds whose GPU-backed debt is the working template for the asset class these platforms will originate.

That last pairing is the circularity in two line items. But the position also forces an honest qualifier on our own §1 arithmetic, and it needs its arithmetic shown rather than inferred from a later filing. The $13.4 billion public-equity mark belongs to the quarter ended April 26, not to the June 30 snapshot above. Applying Intel's own closes across that quarter — roughly $45 in late January against roughly $83–85 in late April — to the 214,776,632 shares gives an Intel contribution of about $8.0–8.6 billion, or some 60–64% of the entire $13.4 billion mark. So the ~23% of pretax income attributable to investment marks is indeed dominated by Intel: a strategic and partly political stake in a rival-turned-foundry-partner, not an AI customer being funded to buy NVIDIA chips. The circular-marks reading of that line is weaker than the headline percentage suggests, and we would rather say so, with the workings, than let a convenient number stand unexamined. (As a units check on the filing itself: Intel's June 30 close of $139.63 reproduces the reported $29,989,261,126 to the dollar.)

Station 0 · the pressure gauge · read before the machine

§2 The Gauge

You may have arrived at this note the way most readers did: wondering what NVIDIA's credit-default swaps are trying to say. Four facts, in order of how much they explain:

  1. The market is nine months old — and NVIDIA only recently gave it something to trade. NVDA 5-year CDS began actively trading in November 2025; there was no meaningful market in insuring NVIDIA's debt for the company's entire prior history. Then on June 18, 2026, NVIDIA printed its first bond deal since 2021 — $25.0 billion across seven tranches (2028–2056), its largest ever — roughly tripling total debt to ~$38 billion including leases, and handing the CDS market both deliverable paper and $25 billion of fresh new-issue risk for dealers to hedge. But the raise added the same $25 billion to the cash pile: pro forma, NVIDIA still holds roughly $68 billion of net cash and earned $58.3 billion in a single quarter. In solvency terms there remains almost nothing to insure; in market-structure terms, there is now something to trade — and the widening began weeks after the deal priced.
  2. The move is violent but the level is benign. Roughly 40bps in late June, a record in the low 80s in late July — the largest single-day widening since trading began — and ~80bps into the August 10 announcement. At one point the spread crossed above Alphabet's (~69bps): a spread ordering no credit analyst would defend on fundamentals. Even at the record, ~80bps prices roughly a 6–7% cumulative five-year default probability at standard recovery assumptions — high-yield-tourist pricing on a AAA-shaped balance sheet.
  3. Each widening leg has coincided with NVIDIA adding obligations its old balance sheet never carried — first the $25 billion funded issuance in June, then the contingent stack: the financing platforms, the ecosystem investments, the lease guarantees. The CDS is repricing NVIDIA's entanglement with its customers' leverage — and hedging its newly minted paper — not its standalone solvency.
  4. The dominant flow is proxy hedging. Funds warehousing unrated neocloud, datacenter, and AI-adjacent private paper cannot buy protection on the actual borrowers, so they short the one liquid, correlated name. NVDA CDS is functioning as the AI complex's credit-hedging instrument — the tell being that Oracle, Alphabet, and even SpaceX CDS printed records in the same tape.

The market has noticed the same structure this note describes — Michael Burry called the platforms "a sign of desperation" — but the desperation read requires NVIDIA to be shedding exposure, and the filings show the opposite (§1). The honest reading: the CDS is not a solvency signal; it is the price of the missing instrument. There is currently no way to short AI credit directly, so its price is being discovered in the nearest liquid proxy — and the moment rated compute ABS exists, that flow has a better home. Hence audit point 4 (§7): transfer is real only if NVDA protection tightens relative to the levered AI names once the platforms issue.

Three days after the record print, NVIDIA answered — though not in the way the protection buyers were positioned for.

Station I · intake · announced August 10

§3 The Machine

The announcement was not the launch of a machine. Goldman, Blackstone and Apollo had been working for months to draw up the debt deals that would let AI developers pay for NVIDIA chips, and it was slow progress on those complex negotiations that prompted Huang to change tack and go public — the stated aim being to assure NVIDIA's own investors that deep-pocketed firms stand ready to finance its customers, particularly Anthropic and OpenAI. The $500 billion itself is, in Bloomberg's characterization, "a round figure with no obvious provenance": no set time frame, and a combination of deals already under discussion with forecasts of demand still to come. Read correctly, August 10 is not an origination event. It is a confidence signal, issued because origination was stalling — which is the demand-generation motive of §1 stated in the sponsor's own sequence of moves.

On August 10, NVIDIA announced six independent financing platforms with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR, to mobilize over $500 billion of third-party capital for AI infrastructure — compute as collateral, capital deployed (per press accounts of the structure) through private offerings and bonds issued by special-purpose entities, all of it "subject to execution of the final agreements." Jensen Huang's pitch is the collateral case verbatim: NVIDIA compute is "broadly adopted, flexible across models and workloads, fungible and transferable across customers and operators, and continuously improved through CUDA" — and the release's own copy, a sentence ahead of the quote and not Huang's words, adds the superlatives: the lowest token cost, the highest revenue, and the longest life. Apollo's Jim Zelter supplied the asset-class case: modern compute is "a scarce, mission-critical asset class with compelling investment characteristics." The supplier asserts the collateral is durable; the originator asserts it is investable; neither will hold it.

Separately — and deliberately not NVIDIA's — the price-discovery venue already exists. Compute Exchange, launched January 2025 by DRW's Don Wilson and Suna Said of Woodside AI, runs reserved-capacity auctions across 100-plus providers, offers forward contracts on future capacity, and in July 2026 opened a secondary market in used H100 and A100 systems (by the venue's own account, used B200s are not yet even available). The rails and the tape are being built by different hands — because the supplier does not want to own the printed mark on its own hardware's residual value.

And the collateral case did not stand on its own. In the days after the announcement, Huang disclosed the credit enhancement that makes the paper work: NVIDIA will provide residual-value support of up to 25% of an opportunity — if the resale or reuse value of the installed hardware falls short at the end of a financing term, NVIDIA covers part of the gap. In NVIDIA's own words the mechanism is "assessed carefully on a project-by-project basis," is "substantially lower than other compute-financing arrangements," and is designed to "complement — not replace — independent underwriting." No definitive agreements exist yet — these are memoranda of understanding — and neither the documentation, the loss order, nor the assumed useful life of the hardware has been stated. And the sequence matters more than the number. The announcement itself unnerved credit investors, who read it as NVIDIA absorbing open-ended leverage; the 25% figure was published the following day and the concern eased — which means the cap is doing two jobs at once. It is a credit enhancement that makes the collateral financeable, and simultaneously a disclosed ceiling that bounds the sponsor's exposure for the benefit of the sponsor's own creditors. Both sides of this trade needed convincing. But the direction is now on the record either way: the supplier is writing a floor under the one variable this note identifies as unpriceable.

The distribution machinery moved within days. Goldman — the only bank among six otherwise-asset-management partners, and among the lead underwriters on NVIDIA's own June bond sale alongside JPMorgan and Morgan Stanley — was reported on August 14 to be already in talks with investors, positioned to lead the public debt offerings. The bank that helped place NVIDIA's paper is now placing the paper written against NVIDIA's chips.

One more filing fact frames why the machine had to be built. Three direct customers now account for 21%, 17%, and 16% of NVIDIA's revenue — 54% across three checks — up from two customers at 16% and 14% a year earlier. A financing channel that broadens the buyer base is not a convenience for this order book; it is a structural necessity.

The operators' manual · who sits where

§4 The Seats

The Losing Bets That Built the World established the recurring cast of every infrastructure wave: builders lose in aggregate, suppliers are paid in cash during the boom, consolidators buy the wreckage, users keep the surplus. In every prior wave, the financier was one of the four in costume — Insull was the builder, financing the grid through his own holding-company pyramid; Morgan was the consolidator, financing rail after failure at the reorganization price. The August 10 structure introduces a seat with no historical occupant: the originator — six firms whose economics are fees on origination, structuring, and management, with the risk held only in transit. The closest rhyme is not Insull or Morgan but the mortgage originators of 2003–2007 — originate-to-distribute, industrialized — with one improvement (four of the six own or manage the end-holder, so they eat some of their own cooking) and one aggravation (the collateral depreciates on the supplier's own product cadence).

The end-holder claim is not a forecast. The channel opened in March 2026, when CoreWeave's $8.5 billion delayed-draw term loan closed with an A3 from Moody's and A-low from DBRS — the first GPU-backed facility to reach investment grade, and therefore the first to become eligible for the investment-grade mandates that govern insurance general accounts and pension portfolios. Press expectation is that the platform securitizations distribute among insurers, pension funds, and sovereign wealth funds on the same logic. What has not been established is that the NVIDIA platforms themselves can clear that bar: no rating standard for this paper has been resolved, and the useful-life assumption that would drive it remains unstated by every party.

The seat is already generalizing beyond this issuer. Apollo and Blackstone are anchor investors in a parallel Broadcom structure financing more than 20 gigawatts of compute, with roughly $35 billion of financing already in hand, and Blackstone has sounded out investors on a further transaction of more than $30 billion. Apollo's own economics are described plainly: it stands to unlock more fees as it expands its trading operation, selling larger chunks of the loans it originates to other investors — originate-to-distribute, named. JPMorgan's asset-management arm is reported to be discussing how to participate. The fifth seat is not an NVIDIA arrangement; it is a role the AI supply chain is now building at multiple issuers at once.

Seats one through four are as ranked in the prior note. The fifth is new — and it is the seat that, in every prior cycle that had even a partial version of it, won.

The flaw in the machine · residuals vs the roadmap

§5 The Contradiction

A liquid compute market is the precondition for the asset class and the detonator under it. Rating agencies cannot rate compute-backed paper without observable liquidation values — which is precisely what Compute Exchange's forwards and secondary tape provide. But pre-mark, collateral is worth what the model says; post-mark, it is worth what the screen says. ABX did not cause the subprime losses; it made them instantaneous, public, and undeniable. The compute forward curve will do the same for GPU residuals.

And the residuals have a scheduled adversary: the supplier's own roadmap. NVIDIA needs H100 and H200 spot rates high to support the residual assumptions in the paper its platforms will originate — and it needs to ship Rubin, which obsoletes them, on an annual cadence it cannot slow without ceding the frontier. NVIDIA's product roadmap is the largest single loss-driver in the collateral pool being financed on NVIDIA's collateral narrative. Early price discovery already leans the wrong way: Compute Exchange reports secondary demand concentrated in H100 and A100 systems rather than Blackwell — the residual curve being discovered in real time, sloping against the "longest life" claim. Our depreciation series (The First Invoice) documented the useful-life debate; that debate now has a $500-billion credit structure built on one side of it. And NVIDIA's roadmap is not the only force pressing down: China's domestic accelerator buildout is reported as a second bid-side threat to the same resale market, compressing the very re-rental economics the lenders are relying on.

For context on what the rating agencies will do with this: through 2025 S&P capped data-centre ABS at A+ on the sector's short history, and that ceiling has since given way, with senior GPU-backed tranches reaching higher and Moody's signalling no hard cap while saying in February 2026 it would run its own probability assessments on AI data-centre leases. Residual-value insurance and guarantees are already a routine enhancement in these structures. So the 25% support is not an exotic concession — it is a standard technique, applied at unprecedented scale by the party that also builds the asset.

The lenders' own stated mitigant runs straight into this. Collateral is expected to be some combination of the chips and the offtake agreements, and the answer to a borrower default is that the chips can simply be rented to someone else — which is to say the downside protection is the re-rental rate, and the re-rental rate is precisely what each new generation compresses. Skeptics quoted in the same reporting make the other half of the point: chip valuations are inflated by record demand today, and the buildout risks an oversupply of compute years out. The mitigant and the risk are the same variable.

Which is what makes the 25% residual-value support the most consequential detail in the entire announcement. NVIDIA has agreed to cover part of the shortfall between expected and actual resale value of accelerators whose obsolescence it schedules. It has written a put on its own product cadence. Every Rubin launch that strengthens the franchise weakens the collateral and moves that option closer to the money — and unlike the annuity holder, who is short an option they do not control, NVIDIA is short an option it controls entirely and has every strategic incentive to exercise against itself. Held flat across a $500 billion target the exposure would be on the order of $125 billion — an upper bound that compounds two separate hypotheticals, since it assumes both that the entire uncommitted target is reached and that every dollar of it is support-eligible, neither of which is established. Nobody should quote it as a commitment: the support is deal-by-deal, covers a gap rather than a principal amount, and no agreement has been signed. The order of magnitude is the point, not the figure.

The guarantee question is not hypothetical either. NVIDIA's current filings already disclose a template: beginning in fiscal 2026, the company guarantees partners' facility-lease obligations in the event of their default, in exchange for warrants — maximum gross exposure $3.5 billion, terms of five to seven years, $712 million escrowed by the partners, the guarantees classified as credit derivatives with fair-value changes running through Other income. This is the exact shape of the question that decides the whole thesis — and Huang has now answered its first half in public. Residual-value support of the kind the lease guarantees already demonstrate is stated policy for the platforms. What remains open is how it is documented, where it sits in the loss order, and whether it lands on NVIDIA's balance sheet as a guarantee, a credit derivative, or nothing at all until it is drawn. To the extent it exists, the $500 billion is distribution, not transfer — the risk rides third-party balance sheets while a defined slice of it economically remains NVIDIA's.

The strongest case against this reading

A note this opinionated owes its best counterargument a fair hearing, and the best one on the tape is Bank of America's. Vivek Arya: "The burden sits with the consortium, not NVDA's balance sheet. NVDA guarantees asset quality, not the debt, turning the bear's depreciation worry into the enabling feature." The argument is genuinely strong, and it runs like this: NVIDIA is not underwriting anyone's credit, only the quality of the machine — and the party best placed to warrant that a GPU still works and still rents is the party that built it and controls its software stack. On BofA's numbers, prior vendor-financing exposure ran to roughly 15% of NVIDIA's ~$470 billion of projected 2026–27 free cash flow; shifting that load to third-party capital releases cash for buybacks. On this reading the platforms are a de-risking, and the 25% cap is the proof: NVIDIA kept the sliver it is uniquely qualified to price and gave away the rest.

We think that is right about the debt and wrong about the residual — and the distinction is the whole note. Guaranteeing "asset quality" is precisely the guarantee whose cost NVIDIA determines, because obsolescence is not a defect in the machine but a decision about the next one. A warranty that the GPU still functions is cheap; a warranty on what it still rents for is a short position in your own product cycle. Arya is correct that the consortium carries the debt. Our claim is narrower and survives his: NVIDIA retained the one exposure nobody else could price, and it retained it in the one variable its own roadmap moves.

The dissent is not ours alone. Wells Fargo and Mizuho have flagged NVIDIA's continuing exposure, UBS the circular-financing question, and Seaport's Jay Goldberg the risk that investors eventually ask whether the spending cycle makes economic sense. The equity's own verdict on announcement day was a decline.

The frame is not ours either

Six weeks before any of this, the Bank for International Settlements described the mechanism in its June 28 Annual Economic Report: circular financing in which chip manufacturers and cloud providers invest in AI laboratories and smaller computing firms that then commit to buying hardware from those same investors. The five largest hyperscalers are set to spend over $1 trillion across 2025–26, outrunning their own earnings and free cash flow, and the sector's net economic surplus, on the BIS's estimate, is declining and could turn negative. Direct lending funds, it notes, "have quadrupled their lending to the AI and IT sectors in the past five years, to about 15% of their portfolios." Its warning, in its own words: a repricing of risk "has the potential to be similarly disruptive by triggering a corporate credit freeze with wider implications for aggregate investment" — similarly meaning the large, synchronised corrections it invokes two sentences earlier, of which 2008 is the cited precedent. An earlier BIS bulletin (No. 120, January 7, 2026, Aldasoro, Doerr and Rees) had already named the transition: financing the AI boom must shift "from operating cash flows to debt, with private credit playing a rapidly increasing role," with equity prices running "far ahead of debt market pricing." Its footnote supplies the scale of the channel now being widened — funds originated over $40 billion of loans to AI-related companies in 2025, against about $3 billion in 2010.

The August 10 platforms are that shift, institutionalized, eight months later. We are not claiming novelty for the diagnosis. We are pointing out that the machine built to answer it routes the risk to insurance general accounts and, per its own architects, possibly to retail.

The return line · conjecture, labeled

§6 The Return Line

Everything above rests on filings and announcements. This section is conjecture, labeled as such — our best guess at how the machine behaves, stated before the SPE documents exist so it can be scored later.

The clean-agency story is the no-free-lunch story: the terminal holder of the paper earns the spread because they accepted the downside — you do not get the cash flow from the hottest asset class in the world without owning its risk. We think that story is directionally right and wrong in three compounding ways.

The buyer will own the risk but not be paid for it. "Accepting the downside" and "being compensated for the downside" are different claims, and the securitization apparatus exists to open a gap between them. The 2006 AAA CDO buyer also accepted the downside — for 30 basis points over Libor. Tranching, an investment-grade NAIC designation, and a collateral class with five quarters of operating history are how a fat, unpriceable risk is sold at a thin, priceable spread. The yield will be calibrated to the rating; the risk is calibrated to reality. The gap between those two calibrations is the fifth seat's margin.

The buyer is short an option the counterparty controls. The feature with no historical precedent. A 2006 mortgage-bond buyer took house-price risk that nobody controlled. The compute-paper buyer takes obsolescence risk whose timing is set by one company — NVIDIA decides when Rubin ships and how hard it deprecates the pool backing the paper — and that same company sponsors the financing channel and sells the collateral. The downside's trigger sits in the seller's product-roadmap meeting. No rated spread prices that correctly, because ratings have no cell for "the sponsor schedules the impairment."

In stress, the risk boomerangs to the center. Structured investment vehicles were off-balance-sheet until December 2007, when Citigroup took them back rather than let the franchise die. Every sponsor here has the same compulsion: Apollo cannot let Athene-held compute paper impair without damaging the machine that funds its entire model, and NVIDIA cannot let a major offtaker default and print a loss onto the collateral curve under $500 billion of paper — it is already backstopping partner leases in exchange for warrants. So our base case is a three-layer ownership: the annuity holder owns the risk contractually, the sponsors own it reputationally, and NVIDIA owns it economically — because any default in the pool impairs the collateral narrative its order book runs on. The 25% residual-value support (§3) means the third layer is no longer purely economic: a defined slice is contractual too, by the sponsor's own statement, before a single agreement has been signed. That is the boomerang arriving early — not in stress, and not, as we first wrote, simply as the price of getting the paper sold. The reporting is more specific: the cap was published to calm credit investors alarmed by the sponsor's own exposure. The risk returning to the center and the sponsor advertising a limit on how much can return are the same act, four days after the announcement and before a single agreement is signed. The music-stops scenario is not a clean loss eaten by a distant bondholder; it is the discovery that the risk was never severed from the center. Every prior cycle ended with the same discovery.

Questions we cannot yet answer, listed so they can be checked off as the documents surface: Who holds the equity and first-loss tranches — retained by the GPs, sold to yield-hunters, or quietly taken by NVIDIA in the warrants-for-guarantees shape it already uses? First-loss placement is the honest map of who believes what, and the 25% residual-value support raises rather than settles it: support that pays only at term-end on a resale shortfall is a very different instrument from first-loss on the pool, and the two are easy to conflate in a press release and hard to conflate in a waterfall. What residual-value assumption clears a rating committee for an asset whose secondary tape already trades off "longest life"? Is there any structure in which NVIDIA can credibly decline to support a failing offtaker — and if not, is the $500 billion economically recourse to NVIDIA regardless of what the documents say? If the paper prices wide, the platforms fail to scale; if it prices tight, the risk is mispriced at $500 billion of scale — is there any spread at which this paper is both sellable and fairly priced? And when the first impairment prints, does the NAIC re-designate the class — and what does a forced insurance-general-account seller do to a collateral curve with a nine-month history?
