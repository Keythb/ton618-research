BlackRock (BLK) — Defending the ETF Fee Premium Against Commoditization?

BlackRock earned $15.93 a share as adjusted in the third quarter, on $205 billion of net inflows. About half of what it earns after tax comes from ETFs and index funds, where competition is fought on price: Vanguard, after two fee cuts, has gathered more new ETF money than iShares this year. Yet iShares' fee rate has risen over two years, mostly because its asset mix has shifted toward pricier funds. At $1,134 the market values BlackRock at about $193 billion once the shares still owed for its 2024–25 acquisitions are counted, and pays about 26 times after-tax earnings for the index business, more than for any listed peer group. Fair value $1,031 a share, 9.1% below Friday's close: the price needs the move to index funds to run about four years longer than the house expects.

On Wednesday 14 October BlackRock reported third-quarter revenue of $7.75 billion, up 19% on a year earlier, as-adjusted earnings of $15.93 a share against a consensus of $14.25, and $205 billion of net inflows, $135 billion of them into iShares. Assets under management reached $15.5 trillion. Base fees grew at an 11% annualized organic rate, the ninth straight quarter at or above the firm's 5% target, and the operating margin rose 320 basis points to 47.8%. The chairman told the call that "for the first time in my career … I'm seeing genuine shortages of capital". A full year after HPS and Preqin joined (two after Global Infrastructure Partners), BlackRock is, he said, "at the center of many of these conversations" about where capital goes. The shares opened 2% higher and gave most of it back by midday, at $1,068.36, on a day the S&P 500 fell 1% and the listed private-markets managers about 3%. Two days later, on Friday 16 October, they closed at $1,134.38, the price this note uses. This note asks whether BlackRock can defend the fee premium on its largest business — measured here by iShares' own base-fee rate (§5), not a rival-by-rival spread — as commoditization has slowly eaten at ETF fees, and what the price already assumes.

Terms used throughout, defined once. AUM: assets under management. Base fees: the management fees BlackRock earns as a percentage of AUM, including the securities-lending revenue it allocates to products; the fee rate is annualized base fees over average AUM, in basis points (bp), hundredths of a percent. Organic growth: growth from net client flows, excluding market moves. ETF: exchange-traded fund; iShares is BlackRock's ETF brand. Non-ETF index: index mandates and funds that are not ETFs, mostly institutional. Private markets: infrastructure, private credit, private equity and real estate funds — the business GIP (Global Infrastructure Partners) and HPS (HPS Investment Partners) were bought to build. Fee-paying AUM: assets that earn management fees now. FRE: fee-related earnings, management fees less the costs of earning them, the measure private-markets managers are valued on; P/FRE is price over FRE. Performance fees: fees earned when funds beat hurdles; volatile. Aladdin: BlackRock's investment-management software; ACV is annual contract value, the forward value of its recurring subscriptions. As adjusted: BlackRock's non-GAAP earnings, which exclude acquisition accounting (amortization of acquired intangibles, remeasurement of share-settled deal payments, retention awards). Cost of equity (CoE): the return shareholders require; built here as the 10-year Treasury yield plus the stock's beta times an equity risk premium. Sum of the parts (SOTP): valuing each business on its own terms and adding them up. Terminal growth: the growth rate assumed after the explicit forecast. Growth stages: the explicit forecast is split into periods, each lasting as long as the thing that drives its growth; every length is stated and argued in §9. Gordon-equivalent growth: the single perpetual growth rate a given multiple implies, g = (M × CoE − payout)/(M + payout).

The other side, first

The case for the price

The strongest case that $1,134 is right, in the terms its proponents would use.

  1. The record is better than the house's growth stages allow. Organic base-fee growth has been at or above the 5% target for nine straight quarters and at or above 8% for five. Trailing twelve-month ETF organic asset growth ran 11–14% in every one of the last nine quarters. iShares' organic growth averaged 11% a year from 2015 to 2025, and 11–12.5% in the last two, as its assets grew five-fold. And the ETF fee rate rose, from 17.0 to 17.4 basis points, while ETF assets grew 57%. The shift from mutual funds to ETFs and the move of active management into the ETF wrapper (iShares active assets doubled in a year to more than $160 billion, the chief executive said) are decade-long, not cyclical. A fee drift of −3% a year assumes compression resumes after two years in which it stopped.
  2. BlackRock is cheap against its own history and its own analysts. At 20.4 times trailing adjusted earnings it sits below its 2015–25 year-end median of 21.3 times. Twenty of the twenty-one analysts on Robinhood's tally rate it a buy, and the lowest price target on file, $1,212, is 7% above the price.
  3. The 2030 plan is running ahead. In June 2025 management set out to roughly double operating income to $15 billion and market value to $280 billion by 2030 before market gains, with private markets and technology rising from 15% to over 30% of revenue. Five quarters in, organic base-fee growth is 10% over twelve months against a 5% target, and the operating margin is 47.8% against a 45% floor. The margin excluding performance fees rose 230 basis points in a year — operating leverage a model held at today's margin does not credit.
  4. Private markets is worth more than its current fees. $96 billion is not yet earning fees — up to about $0.76 billion a year at today's rate, though the figure can include leverage and capital that earns only performance fees — and $139 billion is available to invest. GIP and HPS run at fee-related margins above 50%. The chief executive says the firm is "in the mix of almost every conversation" on financing AI infrastructure. And the deferred deal shares are paid only if GIP and HPS hit their milestones, so the dilution comes with the growth.
  5. The cost of equity is too high. BlackRock's earnings are among the most diversified in finance: by asset class, client type and region, with technology and private markets beside index. At a 10% cost of equity — a beta of about 1.0 instead of the 1.20–1.34 measured — the house's own three cases give a weighted $1,194 a share, above the price.
  6. The move to index funds has further to run. Active funds still hold 48% of US long-term fund assets, and index domestic-equity funds own only 19% of US stocks. If active funds fall to about 14% rather than 25% — fourteen years at the current pace — the weighted value is $1,136; base growth at a 10% cost of equity with that runway gives $1,254; the strongest growth case with both gives $2,054.
  7. Digital assets are an option the model leaves out. IBIT holds 60.7% of US spot-bitcoin ETF assets, and iShares took about 90% of 2025's inflows into US digital-asset ETFs by BlackRock's count. BlackRock manages most of the reserves behind Circle's USDC stablecoin and runs what it calls the largest tokenized fund in the world. The chairman says tokenization may be roughly where the internet was in 1996. A sum of the parts built on today's fees cannot see that option.

Of these, this note tests:

  • (1) in §5, §9 and §10, where the ETF platform's growth and fee rate are laid against the growth the price needs and against the decade's record;
  • (2) in §10, where the trailing multiple is set against the 10-year Treasury yield at each year-end;
  • (3) in §1 and §9, where the plan's milestones are scored and margin expansion is run as a sensitivity;
  • (4) in §7 and §8, where the uninvested commitments, the fee rate and the deal decks are reconciled to the run-rate and the contingent shares are scaled with performance by case;
  • (5) in §9, where the cost of equity is built in the open and every result is shown at 10%, 11% and 12%;
  • (6) in §9, where the length of every growth stage is argued from what drives it and the value of each alternative length is shown;
  • (7) in §6, where bitcoin's weight in BlackRock's flows, fees, growth and value is measured and the tokenization pieces are sized.

It cannot test:

  • Sell-side price targets, which are opinions it reports but does not test.
  • Whether the 2025–26 flow pace is the new normal, which only the next several years can settle.
  • How far the move to index funds finally runs, which the stage table lets the reader judge.
  • How large tokenization becomes, which no record yet measures.
  • Aladdin's margin and the cost split between the businesses, which BlackRock does not publish.
  • Whether the market's lower discount rate on BlackRock is a durable judgment on its risk.
How it got here

How BlackRock got here, and where it says it is going

From bonds to iShares to Aladdin. BlackRock was founded in 1988 as a fixed-income manager. It merged with PNC's asset manager in 1995, listed in 1999, bought Merrill Lynch Investment Managers in 2006 and, on 1 December 2009, bought Barclays Global Investors, with iShares, for about $15 billion in cash and stock (the history is drawn from Part 1 of the house's bank series, US Banking Before the Print). Aladdin, the risk system the founders built for their own bond portfolios, became a business sold to other investors. Assets under management grew from $4.6 trillion at the end of 2015 to $14.0 trillion at the end of 2025 and $15.5 trillion at the end of September. As-adjusted earnings per share went from $19.60 in 2015 to $48.09 in 2025, 9.4% a year, through two down years (2016 and 2022).

Three acquisitions in eighteen months. BlackRock then bought into the businesses public markets were losing to private capital. Global Infrastructure Partners closed on 1 October 2024 for $3 billion in cash and about 12 million shares, a nominal $12.55 billion: about 7 million shares at closing (6.9 million as issued) and up to about 5 million more after five years on performance milestones. Preqin, a private-markets data provider, closed on 3 March 2025 for £2.5 billion (about $3.2 billion) in cash. HPS, a private-credit manager, closed on 1 July 2025 for 8.5 million units of a BlackRock subsidiary exchangeable one for one into shares ("Subco Units"), worth $8.5 billion at closing, plus 2.8–4.4 million more units in about five years. The cash legs were largely funded with $5.5 billion of notes issued in 2024. The chairman's 2026 letter calls the work — HPS, Preqin and the smaller ElmTree closing in 2025, following GIP — "our largest integrations in more than 15 years".

The plan. At its June 2025 Investor Day management set two through-the-cycle targets: organic base-fee growth of at least 5% and an as-adjusted operating margin of at least 45%. It also set a 2030 aspiration before market gains: operating income from about $8 billion to $15 billion, revenue from $20 billion to more than $35 billion (10% a year), market value from about $140 billion to $280 billion, private markets and technology from 15% to more than 30% of revenue, and $400 billion of private-markets fundraising from 2025 to 2030. Five quarters in, twelve-month organic base-fee growth is 10% and the third-quarter margin 47.8%. Twelve-month private-markets fundraising is $68 billion, against the roughly $67 billion a year the plan needs, and market value is $184 billion.

Rivalry: high, and fought on price. iShares remains the largest ETF provider, but it is losing share. Its share of global ETF assets slipped from 28.3% to 27.3% in the year to August, its lead over Vanguard in the US narrowed from 1.0 point to 0.1, and in 2026 Vanguard has gathered more new money than iShares, globally and in the US. Vanguard made what it called its largest-ever fee cut in February 2025 and another in February 2026, from an average expense ratio of 0.06%. Schwab and State Street (in Europe) cut in 2026. In the US, the asset-weighted fee on index equity ETFs fell from 0.21% in 2017 to 0.14% in 2025 (ICI). BlackRock's own 10-K warns that competition "may lead to additional fee compression".

Suppliers: concentrated, but the dependence runs both ways. BlackRock relies on a relatively concentrated group of index providers and says the structure may limit its ability to renegotiate. Yet MSCI earns 11.8% of its revenue from BlackRock (first half of 2026), almost all of it on BlackRock's fund assets. In January the two extended their ETF licence to 2035 under a revised fee structure. MSCI's fee on the ETF assets linked to its indexes fell from 2.43 to 2.28 basis points over the year to June, though MSCI does not say how much of that is the new terms. BlackRock also reported investment discretion over about 7% of MSCI's shares at the end of 2024.

Buyers: intermediaries and the largest institutions. Retail money reaches BlackRock principally through brokers, banks and advisers whose terms are renegotiated periodically; distribution and servicing costs rose to $2.46 billion in 2025. Institutional index mandates are 29% of long-term assets but 6% of base fees, and 2025's $119 billion of institutional index outflows came primarily from one client. More than 30,000 advisers used BlackRock's model portfolios at the end of 2024; BlackRock does not disclose how much money they steer.

New entrants and substitutes. Starting an ETF is easy; scaling one is hard. The number of ETF providers worldwide rose from 896 to 1,044 in the year to August, and a record 2,141 ETFs launched in the first seven months of 2026. Yet the three largest providers still take about half of all net inflows. The substitutes — direct indexing (BlackRock bought Aperio in 2021), separately managed accounts, collective investment trusts, tokenized funds and private credit in place of public bonds — are mostly businesses BlackRock also runs.

What it means for the value. Three findings matter for the inputs. First, rivalry is the main risk to the base case's 10% organic growth and 3% annual fee fall, which §5 argues from the record. iShares now takes about a fifth of new ETF money while owning more than a quarter of the assets. Over the same two years, though, its ETF fee rate rose, mostly through mix, and its trailing organic growth held at 11–14%. Second, the scale gap in private markets, and the growth gap it shows up as (§7), is why the house does not pay the listed managers' multiple on the evidence so far. Third, Aladdin's rivals that report it grow recurring revenue at 6–8% (FactSet, State Street's software, MSCI's analytics), below the 12% the base case gives it.

The print and the call

What the print and the call said

What printed. Revenue was $7,752 million: base fees $6,036 million (+20% on the year), performance fees $630 million and technology services $588 million (+14%). Of the performance fees, $409 million came from liquid alternatives, largely one hedge fund whose measurement year ends in September, and $110 million from HPS. As-adjusted operating income rose 27% to $3,330 million and as-adjusted net income 37% to $2,621 million. GAAP diluted earnings were $10.57 a share. The $5.36 gap to $15.93 is almost all acquisition accounting, and two items make up $4.64 a share of it: a $556 million after-tax charge ($564 million before tax) as the fair value of the shares still owed to GIP and HPS rose with BlackRock's own share price, and $207 million after tax ($276 million before) of amortization of acquired intangibles. Buybacks were $550 million; the dividend $5.73.

What management stressed. On margins, the chief financial officer said: "Excluding the impact of all performance fees and related compensation, our adjusted operating margin for the third quarter would have been 48.6%", up 230 basis points on a year. On fees, he said that on an equivalent day count "our annualized effective fee rate was approximately flat compared to the second quarter". On growth, he said organic base-fee growth has been "nine consecutive quarters at or above our 5% long-term target", and that BlackRock can sustainably deliver 6–8%, with more in strong markets. Of the $524 billion of flows so far this year (the release shows $527 billion over the nine months), he said about $270 billion went into products charging 10 to 60 basis points — active ETFs, country funds, diversifiers. The chief executive: "We raised $25 billion in private credit in our first full year with HPS." On syndication, the CFO said such revenues are "incremental to our model, not the primary driver of value creation"; on evergreen private-credit funds sold to wealthy individuals, "We still have redemptions in the queue." The fourth-quarter buyback will be at least $550 million.

What went unasked. Six analysts asked about organic-growth drivers, rates and AI financing, tax-aware accounts, margins and syndication. None asked about the fee rate on private markets, the gap between private-markets fee-paying assets and fees, the $564 million share-settled charge, the 6.8–9.6 million deal shares still to be issued, or the end of the GIP sellers' two-year lock-up.

The four businesses

Four businesses, one income statement

The split. BlackRock does not publish costs by business, so the house allocates and says how. Third-quarter recurring revenue, after distribution pass-throughs and before performance fees, was $6,337 million; the CFO's 48.6% margin on it is $3,080 million of recurring operating income. Private markets is given a 45% fee-related margin (management says GIP and HPS run above 50%; BlackRock's older private-markets funds are assumed lower). Technology is given 40%, above SS&C's adjusted operating margin (38.5%) and FactSet's (34.5% for its 2026 fiscal year) and well below MSCI's operating margin (56%); BlackRock does not disclose Aladdin's. The public-markets businesses take the rest, a 50.1% margin, split between ETF and index and active and cash at that one rate. The $371 million of advisory fees BlackRock passes on to distributors and its $84 million of other revenue are both placed in active and cash management, where the retail mutual funds sit. Performance fees are taken at their last-twelve-months level, $1,961 million, at the 40% contribution the CFO's two margins imply.

The result. At a 25% tax rate (management's guide for the fourth quarter), run-rate after-tax fee earnings are $9.75 billion: ETF and index $4.71 billion, active and cash $2.86 billion, private-markets fee earnings $0.89 billion, Aladdin $0.62 billion, Preqin $0.08 billion (on an estimated $280 million of revenue; the 10-K gives about $210 million for the ten months of 2025), private-markets performance fees $0.27 billion and the rest $0.32 billion.

iShares

iShares: the fee rate against the growth

17.03 → 17.36bpthe ETF base-fee rate, third quarter 2024 to third quarter 2026, while average ETF assets grew 57%

No compression in the aggregate. Over the eight quarters to September, average ETF assets rose 57% and ETF base fees 60%. The all-ETF fee rate went from 17.03 to 17.36 basis points. Split three ways, the rise is mostly mix: equity ETFs, at 17.8 basis points the larger and dearer group, grew from 73% to 75% of ETF assets, and digital-asset, commodity and multi-asset ETFs earn 27 basis points. Of the 0.33-point rise, 0.20 is mix, 0.08 is rates within each group and 0.05 the interaction of the two. Within the groups, equity ETFs went from 17.65 to 17.82 basis points while fixed-income ETFs fell from 14.38 to 13.94, a 3% cut. BlackRock includes securities-lending revenue in base fees without splitting it by product; lending revenue rose from $149 million to $253 million a quarter. If every dollar of it were ETF revenue, lending would explain at most 0.12 basis points of the rise; the rest, at least 0.21, is management fees.

What it means for the growth stages. The index business is valued on rates that hold across a ten-year stage, not on the last year. The base case takes 10% organic growth (iShares averaged 11.0% from 2015 to 2025, with a trough of 6.4% in 2023) and 4% market drift. It lets the fee rate drift down 3% a year, between the two records measured the same way. In 2020–25 the all-ETF rate fell 4.2% a year; split into mix and rates within each group, mix added 0.34 basis points and rates within the groups took off 4.36, a like-for-like fall of about 4.5% a year (equity ETFs 4.7%, fixed-income ETFs 4.5%). In 2024–26 rates within each group added 0.08 basis points, about flat, and mix 0.20 of the 0.33. Mix added to the rate in both periods; the −3% applies to the blended rate, which fell 4.2% a year in 2020–25 and rose about 1% a year in 2024–26, so it sits between the raw records as well. The house does not carry the flat recent record forward because fixed-income ETFs, the one group getting cheaper, are where compression would resume. Weighted 87% ETFs and 13% non-ETF index (−2.4% organic, the past year's rate), that is 9.75% a year, a little below the decade's 10.7%.

The bear and bull rates. The bear case takes 6.5% organic growth (the 2022–23 trough averaged 6.6%), 2% market drift and the 2020–25 compression. That leaves no faster stage at all: it holds the decade's two weakest years for ten years running, when no other year since 2015 was below 8%. The bull case takes 2024–25's 12% organic growth, 6% market drift and compression easing to −1.5% a year: 15% a year for ten years, near the top tenth of ten-year growth among large US companies. Two things to watch. The CFO expects the fourth quarter, which brings 30–40% of iShares' annual flows, to continue the shift toward higher-fee categories. And institutional index outflows were $104 billion in nine months — the third quarter's $27 billion of them, the CFO said, concentrated in low-fee index equities.

Bitcoin and digital assets

Bitcoin and the digital-asset bet

The franchise. iShares Bitcoin Trust (IBIT) began trading on 11 January 2024 at a 0.25% fee. By 15 October 2026 it held 803,871 bitcoin, worth $66.2 billion. On the trusts' own filings it had 60.7% of US spot-bitcoin ETF assets at the end of June, up from 59.4% at the end of 2025, while the category's assets fell 37%. BlackRock calculates that iShares took about 90% of 2025's inflows into US digital-asset ETFs. Around IBIT sit an ether trust (ETHA, $8.4 billion), a trust that stakes its ether (ETHB, March 2026), a bitcoin income fund that writes calls on IBIT (BITA, June 2026), IBIT options (November 2024) and bitcoin products in Europe, Canada and Australia. BlackRock has filed no Solana or XRP products. In all, its digital-asset products held $79.6 billion at 30 September.

The value

The value

How each business is valued. Each business's run-rate after-tax earnings are grown through stages set by what drives them. A first stage runs at the business's own growth rate for as long as its driver runs; growth then converges in a straight line to a terminal rate of 1.5–4%, at which the final year is capitalized. Each business is discounted at an 11% cost of equity, with 90% of its earnings paid out. By the house convention, explicit years are discounted at mid-year and the terminal value from the end of the last stage (discounting it from mid-year too would add $27 a share). The multiple that results is an output, not a choice. Performance fees are capitalized at 8, 10 and 12 times after related compensation; the balance sheet is carried at mark and the notes at face.

Why these lengths. Once its driver stops, faster-than-average growth rarely survives long. Across US companies from 1951 to 1998, sales growth stayed above the median five years running for 6.3% of firms and ten years running for 0.9%; for operating income, such runs were barely more common than chance would give (Chan, Karceski and Lakonishok, 2003). Hence the five-year convergence wherever a faster stage ends. By that standard, fifteen years of faster growth in the index business is an exception. It is granted because the driver is measurable and has already run fifteen years: the index share of US fund assets rose from 19% in 2010 to 52% in 2025, and active funds lost 1.8 points of share a year in 2010–15 and 2.4 in both 2015–20 and 2020–25, with no sign yet of slowing. Aladdin's five years at 12% is itself a run the same base rates put at about one firm in sixteen, granted for the same reason: a contract book whose renewals can be counted.

Where a reader can disagree. The 25% floor — about half of today's active share survives — is the house's assumption: it rests on the base rates, not on a slowdown in the data. The clock is also US-only. It leaves out iShares' funds outside the US and the move of index mutual funds (21% of US fund assets, about flat since 2020) into ETFs; a reader who thinks either extends the runway can read the value off the length line. The price needs a floor of about 14.5%. A reader who expects active funds to fall to about 14% gets $1,136, about $25 for each year of the move; one who expects them to stop at about 36% (five years) gets $910. The cases vary the growth rates and hold the lengths, so an optimistic rate and an optimistic length are never stacked; lengths move separately in the sensitivities.

Two caps, and the plan. Ninety per cent of fee earnings is paid out. Seed capital, co-investment and general-partner commitments are the reinvestment the growth requires, and the 90% is the capacity of a business whose growth needs little capital, not its recent practice (BlackRock returned about 61% of its last four quarters' adjusted earnings in cash). Terminal growth is capped at 4%, the house ceiling for every valuation, because no fee pool outgrows the economy it draws on forever. The proponent's $2,054 keeps both caps. Every case reaches or nears management's 2030 aspiration of $15 billion of operating income, though the cases include market drift and the plan does not: pre-tax operating income in years four and five (performance fees grown 2% a year) is $14.3–14.7 billion in the bear case, $17.2–18.4 billion in the base and $20.0–22.3 billion in the bull. One ten-year straight-line fade on every business, a common shortcut, would give $878 on the same growth rates.

What would change it

What would change our view

Each condition is read on the three quarterly releases from the fourth quarter of 2026 to the second quarter of 2027 (the last lands in mid-July 2027), and each pair is stated in the same form, so the record can move the house either way.

The house view moves up — toward the price, or past it — if:

  • (a) the ETF platform's trailing organic asset growth averages 12% or more over the three quarters, or the all-ETF fee rate averages 17.4 basis points or more. The base case is then rebuilt on the bull case's index-business growth, which alone lifts the weighted value to about $1,220.
  • (b) private-markets base fees — $668 million in the third quarter — average $730 million a quarter or more, the uninvested commitments converting. The base is rebuilt on the bull case's private-markets growth: about $1,051. The threshold sits beyond the bull path's own three-quarter average of about $710 million, so firing needs more than the case it re-bases to.
  • (c) ACV growth runs at 14% or more in two consecutive quarters: about $1,038.
  • (g) the as-adjusted margin excluding performance fees reaches 50% in a quarter when the S&P 500 is flat or down, the operating leverage the proponent argues for: about $1,059.

The house view moves down, further from the price, if:

  • (d) ETF trailing organic asset growth averages below 8% over the same three quarters, or the all-ETF fee rate averages below 16.8 basis points. The base is rebuilt on the bear case's index-business growth: about $905.
  • (e) private-markets base fees average below $650 million a quarter: about $1,020. The threshold sits beyond the bear path's own three-quarter average of about $661 million, so firing needs more than the case it re-bases to.
  • (h) ACV growth runs below 10% in two consecutive quarters: about $1,020.
  • (f) the as-adjusted margin excluding performance fees falls below 47.2% in a quarter when the S&P 500 is flat or up: about $1,003.

If conditions on both sides fire, the base case is rebuilt on the measured values.

Where the thresholds sit. The ETF organic-growth thresholds sit two points either side of the base case's 10%. The fee-rate thresholds sit 0.3 basis points either side of the base path's three-quarter average of 17.1 basis points (17.36 in the third quarter, falling 3% a year), beyond both the bull path's 17.2 and the bear path's 17.0. The margin thresholds sit 1.4 points either side of the third quarter's 48.6%. On organic growth the last nine quarters (11–14%) already sit above the base and the latest is at the 12% threshold, so (a) is the condition closest to firing.

The annual check. The length of the move to index funds — the assumption the price turns on — is read once a year instead. ICI's Fact Book reports the active share each spring in whole points; at the 2020–25 pace it would be about 46% at the end of 2026. A reading of 47% or more (the move nearing its floor) would shorten the stage, worth about $25 a share for each year removed; 46% keeps it; 45% or less (the move running faster than assumed) would lengthen it by the same measure.

Tokenization, rates and silence. Tokenization has its own trigger: if BlackRock begins to report tokenized-fund or stablecoin-reserve revenue as a line, the house values it as a business rather than an option. So does the discount rate: a 10-year Treasury yield averaging below 4.5% for a full quarter lowers the cost of equity by about 0.6 points and lifts the weighted value to about $1,121; one averaging above 6% takes it to about $918. Silence counts: a release, supplement or call that stops reporting ETF organic growth, private-markets base fees, ACV or the margin excluding performance fees is scored as the condition not met.
