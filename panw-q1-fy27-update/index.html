Palo Alto Networks (PANW) — The Raise Was Real. The Price Needs a Decade of It.

Palo Alto Networks beat its first-quarter guide, raised the year, raised its recurring-revenue guide two quarters early, and agreed to buy an AI start-up for $4.5 billion of stock, as a wave of serious software flaws made national news. We value the stock at $124.97, against $361.73. The quarter strengthens the company's case for the next two years. With our other base-case inputs, the price needs about 28% revenue growth every year for eleven years.

TON618 Capital Research · As of 19 November 2026 · Download PDF · Download model

In September we valued Palo Alto Networks at $119 against a $328.47 close, and called the gap a disagreement about how long growth lasts: that price needed organic growth near 36% from the start (39% at the $362.09 close before the print), against a guide of 14.8% on a like-for-like base. This quarter, in the middle of a national story about a surge of serious software flaws, the company moved toward the price on every line it controls. Revenue grew 23% pro forma. The year's guide rose to 17.99% on the same base, a hair under the 18% line we froze in September, and the recurring-revenue guide went up two prints early. The same evening it agreed to buy Applied Compute, a 70-person maker of open-weight AI models, with 13.3 million new shares. Our number moved little, to $124.97, and for different reasons than it held in September: the quarter's data alone would have lifted September's model to $131.33, and rebuilding that model took most of it back. The gap is about as wide as in September. What moved is where the argument sits: next year is now common ground, and the dispute is over years five to eleven.

The view. We value Palo Alto Networks at $124.97 a share; the $361.73 close of 19 November is 189% above it and above even our bull case ($195). The quarter was good and it moved our cases: the base case starts from the raised guide, and we moved five points of weight from the bear case to the bull, because a like-for-like recurring-revenue guide of +26–27% makes the bear case's halving of growth within a year less likely. Terms used throughout, defined once. NGS ARR: Next-Generation Security annualized recurring revenue, the company's measure of the yearly value of active contracts for its newer products, excluding hardware and legacy subscriptions. RPO / cRPO: remaining performance obligations, contracted revenue not yet recognized (the backlog); cRPO is the part due within twelve months. Pro forma: restated as if acquired businesses had been owned in both periods. Organic: growth excluding acquired businesses altogether. Adjusted FCF: the company's free cash flow measure (operating cash flow less capital spending, with acquisition and some one-off payments added back). SBC: stock-based compensation; the company's "SBC-related charges" include the payroll tax on it. Open-weight model: an AI model whose trained parameters are published, so a customer can run and modify it on its own hardware. Post-training: adapting a trained model to a customer's data and tasks after the original training run. Fiscal year: Palo Alto Networks' year ends 31 July, so fiscal 2028 runs from August 2027 to July 2028.

The two cases

The cases on either side of the price, in their own terms

The case that $361.73 is fair or low. It does not need our cases to be wrong about next year. It needs the next decade to look like the last three years.

  1. Like-for-like recurring revenue is growing 26–27%. NGS ARR is guided to $11.50 billion for fiscal 2027, up 26–27% on a base that already includes CyberArk and Chronosphere. On the company's own guides NGS ARR is about four-fifths of revenue ($11.5 billion against $14.5 billion), and revenue follows it with a lag: if revenue converges on the ARR rate, mid-20s growth is the recurring business's own pace, not a stretch. Pro forma revenue grew 23% in the first quarter.
  2. Consolidation is accelerating. Net new platformizations, customers moving several security products onto the company's platforms, hit a first-quarter record of about 170, more than the last two first quarters combined. Platformized customers hold two-thirds of NGS ARR and expand at more than 120% net retention (company figures that exclude identity and observability). The company's argument is that AI-driven attacks make fragmented tools untenable; the vulnerability count the company cites ran at nearly seven times last year's monthly average.
  3. The company is buying the next market before it exists. Prisma AIRS, its AI-security product, reached about $170 million of ARR in five quarters, the fastest in its history. Applied Compute adds model training and inference for enterprises that want open-weight models under their own control; the company targets $1 billion of added NGS ARR by fiscal 2029 and calls it "our next multi-billion dollar ARR business."
  4. Customers pay in advance. Deferred revenue of $14.6 billion exceeds every operating asset other than acquired goodwill and intangibles; before the January and February 2026 deals, invested capital was negative. A business its customers fund can grow without outside capital; our model credits that cash at 0.70 per dollar of revenue growth.
  5. The steelman-max reaches the price. With fiscal 2027 revenue at the bull case's $14.70 billion, a 31% non-GAAP operating margin in fiscal 2028, fiscal 2028–29 at the like-for-like ARR rate (27% and 26%), a twelve-year fade, a 45% terminal operating margin, 5% SBC, 0.85 of each dollar of revenue growth funded by customers, no acquisition spending, a 50% return on new capital in the terminal and a 9.2% cost of equity (the stock's own 60-month beta), our model is worth $426.00, above $361.73. On those inputs the price needs a cost of equity of 10.0% or less.

The case that $361.73 is too high. It does not deny the quarter. It says the quarter is one data point in an eleven-year requirement.

  1. The organic rate is about 18–23%, not 39%. Reported growth carries a full quarter of CyberArk and Chronosphere that a year earlier did not exist in the accounts. The fiscal 2027 guide is 17.99% pro forma.
  2. Revenue was raised and the backlog was not. RPO grew 35% against revenue 39% and fell $0.2 billion from July. The fiscal 2027 RPO guide moved up $0.1 billion while revenue went up $395 million. Like-for-like the backlog still leads revenue, by 1.8 points, less than half September's lead.
  3. The fiscal 2028 cash target was cut. In September the deck read "Reaffirming 40%+ FY'28 Adj. Free Cash Flow Margin." This quarter it reads "Targeting 38%+ Adjusted Free Cash Flow Margin, Excluding Data Center Capex, for FY'28": two points lower, before the cost of the compute build.
  4. Acquisitions have not yet earned their cost. Capital including what the deals cost earns 5.0%, about half the 10.0% WACC. In the year $23 billion of acquisition capital arrived, trailing operating profit after tax moved −$0.05 billion. SBC-related charges were 18.1% of revenue in the first quarter.
  5. Growth at this scale has not lasted. Salesforce and Adobe, from about $15 billion of revenue, each roughly halved their growth rate within three to four years. The price asks for 28% for eleven.

Both cases are tested below, not equally. In the first, the model runs claim 1 directly (fiscal 2028–29 growth is set from the ARR rate), claim 2 only through the length of the fade, the Applied Compute half of claim 3 as its own valuation leg (Prisma AIRS is not modeled separately), claim 4 through the share of growth funded by prepayments, and claim 5 as the steelman itself. In the second, the model runs claims 1–4 directly; claim 5 is a base rate, not a test, from the two software companies chosen because both crossed about $15 billion of revenue while still growing more than 20% (Salesforce in fiscal 2020–21, Adobe in 2021).

The print

Organic growth was 18–23%, and the raise came from the business

23%pro forma revenue growth in Q1, against 39% reported

Revenue was $3.445 billion, up 39% and $140 million above the midpoint of the company's own guide; non-GAAP earnings were $1.05 a share against a $0.96–0.98 guide. The reported rate includes a full quarter of two large businesses the company did not own a year ago, CyberArk (identity security, closed 11 February 2026) and Chronosphere (observability, closed 29 January), and of smaller ones (Koi in April, Embrace and Console in August and September). The 10-Q gives no pro forma table for the quarter, but the company gave pro forma rates for the two segments the deals sit in, and the third segment had no acquisitions.

The fiscal 2027 revenue guide rose from $14.10–14.20 billion to $14.52–14.57 billion, a midpoint 17.99% above the $12.327 billion pro forma base we used in September. Our rule said 18%. It is not crossed, by $1 million of revenue; on the 10-K's own pro forma figure ($12.312 billion, which aligns CyberArk's June year) the midpoint is 18.1%, but we grade on the rule as frozen. The raise came from the business: Chronosphere is in the base for the full year, and the company called Embrace and Console, the two small deals that closed in the quarter for $685 million, immaterial to the guide in September. Of the $395 million raise, $140 million is the first quarter's beat and $255 million was added to the remaining three quarters.

Three smaller items move the profit lines. GAAP net income ($0.57 a share) includes a $457 million tax benefit; $369 million of it is a discrete federal benefit on a fiscal 2018 transaction that now passes the accounting threshold "based on recent judicial developments" (10-Q), which we add once as an asset and keep out of the run-rate. Other expense includes a $157 million loss on the fair value of the convertible notes assumed with CyberArk ($141 million net of the capped calls bought against them), now current because the stock traded above the conversion trigger. And a $71 million Israeli research-and-development tax credit (10-Q Note 15) reduced research expense by about two points of the quarter's 30.7% non-GAAP operating margin; the 10-Q does not say how much relates to earlier periods, so the model starts from the company's 29.5% margin guide for the year, not the quarter's margin. Non-GAAP gross margin was 75.2%, down 1.7 points, which the company tied to hardware component costs and the mix of younger software-as-a-service products.

The backlog

The backlog blinked again as reported; like-for-like it is close to level

−3.8 ptsRPO growth minus revenue growth, as reported

In September the backlog stopped outgrowing revenue, and we titled the note on it. This quarter the reported gap widened: RPO grew 35% to $21.0 billion while revenue grew 39%, and RPO fell $0.2 billion from July. In the same quarter NGS ARR rose 66% to $9.73 billion, with about $630 million of net new ARR, a first-quarter record.

Exhibit 2 · NGS ARR, backlog and revenue growth, year on year

How to read it. Each line is a year-on-year growth rate by fiscal quarter: NGS ARR (gold), RPO (black) and revenue (grey). The shaded quarters are the ones in which CyberArk and Chronosphere sit in the current figure but not in the year-earlier one, so all three rates are inflated by acquisition there, by different amounts. The point: as reported, revenue now outgrows backlog; the gap is partly the different weight of the acquired businesses in each measure.

We ran the boring checks first. RPO also fell from the fourth quarter to the first a year ago ($15.8 billion to $15.5 billion): the sequential dip is the calendar. The CFO said contract durations "remained flat year-over-year." Product revenue, mostly hardware recognized on delivery and outside the backlog, grew 37%, partly, the chief executive said, because customers are "accelerating purchases because prices are going up" as component costs rise. And the reported rates compare unlike bases: a year ago RPO excluded CyberArk's backlog, which the filings do not give. CyberArk brought $776 million of deferred revenue at the close, and RPO jumped $2.4 billion in that quarter against a usual $0.5–0.6 billion, which puts its backlog near $1.8 billion. With $1.2–2.2 billion of acquired backlog a year earlier, like-for-like RPO grew 18.6%–25.7% against pro forma revenue of 23%: a spread of −4.3 to +2.8 points, about −1.6 at the center.

The forward numbers agree. The fiscal 2027 RPO guide of $25.3–25.5 billion is 19.8% above July, against 17.99% for revenue on the pro forma base: the backlog is still guided ahead of revenue, by 1.8 points, but by less than half September's lead of about 4.5 points. Deferred revenue on the balance sheet fell $167 million in the quarter, mostly long-term; a year ago it fell $522 million. Our reading: the September claim holds as reported and is neither confirmed nor refuted like-for-like. The model carries it through the cash flow, not the growth rate: the base case funds 0.70 of each dollar of revenue growth from customers' prepayments, below the roughly 0.9 of fiscal 2026, and the bear 0.55.

The wave

The vulnerability wave: real demand, an overstated slide, and a company inside it

6.9×serious vulnerability disclosures at 21 large vendors in July 2026 against the 2025 monthly average (our count from Epoch AI's table)

The quarter landed in the middle of a national story. Since late October, critical flaws have been disclosed in software almost everything runs on: PHP and Node.js on 21 October, and Java, which the federal cybersecurity agency (CISA) told organizations on 17 November to patch immediately. Epoch AI counts 2,512 high- and critical-severity disclosures by 21 large vendors in July 2026, about five times the record month before cyber-capable AI models such as Mythos were released; on its own table that is about 6.9 times the 2025 monthly average. The fallout is what made the news. On 17 November the Director of National Intelligence tied an outage at the NSA to patching for the PHP flaw, in a week that also brought an FAA ground stop and a JPMorgan Chase payments outage. Coverage treats the wave as continuing.

The company's account is broader than the record. Its deck lists six incidents under the heading "The Vulnerability Apocalypse is Causing Real World Impacts", and the chief executive said many of the recent disruptions were partly the unintended consequence of patching and configuration changes made faster than IT teams can manage. That holds for the NSA, on the intelligence chief's own words. The FAA blamed a faulty network router and Chase a bug in its own hardware systems, not patching; we found no public report of two of the six items (Microsoft emergency patches on 16 November, an Expedia outage), and three of the slide's dates are wrong. The surge in disclosures is real; the slide is the company's case, not the record.

The company is inside the wave, not only selling into it. CISA added a PAN-OS flaw (CVE-2026-0322) to its list of actively exploited vulnerabilities on 14 October, and the company reports active exploitation of the PHP flaw against PAN-OS management interfaces exposed to the internet, which it says affected about 0.02% of its installed base. Rivals' products joined the same list in November, Check Point's among them.

What it means for the valuation.

  • Demand now. It is in the numbers: trailing firewall bookings up 28%, customers buying hardware ahead of price rises, and testing budgets that "have just come out of emergency funds." The raised fiscal 2027 guide and our base case's 19% for fiscal 2028 already carry it.
  • How long it lasts. By management's own account, heavy patching lasts at least another 18 months, and standalone AI-driven testing 12 to 18 months before platforms absorb it, while security budgets rise and consolidate over the next two years: a two-year tailwind against an eleven-year requirement. The strongest form of the opposite view is that AI makes finding flaws permanently cheaper, so a disclosure rate several times the old one is the new normal, not a wave, and the tailwind is structural. The price's case gains if that is so, or if the wave pulls consolidation forward for good, which the record first-quarter platformizations suggest and one quarter cannot prove.
  • The other side. The same wave raises the cost of a flaw in the company's own products, and consolidation concentrates that cost: a customer who moves everything onto one platform has one vendor to fail. We do not charge it separately; it sits in the bear case's margin and weight.

No model input changes. A new tripwire (pq1-vuln-wave, §8) reads whether growth outlasts the wave.

The deal

Applied Compute: a stock-funded move into AI infrastructure, worth about what it costs us

$2.21 billionweighted value of Applied Compute in our cases, against $4.5 billion paid

Applied Compute post-trains and runs open-weight AI models for enterprises. The company said it reached about $100 million of annualized revenue within 18 months of its founding, has about 70 employees and brings about $500 million of cash and prepaid computing commitments. Palo Alto Networks pays $4.5 billion in stock at $337.16, the 15-day average before the deal: about 13.3 million shares, 1.6% of the 821 million outstanding on 11 November, with closing expected in its fiscal third quarter, subject to antitrust clearance. The guide excludes it. The CFO said it lowers the operating margin by up to one point in fiscal 2027 and two in fiscal 2028, with capital spending on inference capacity of less than $3 billion through fiscal 2030. The chief executive's case: to supervise AI models you must be able to train them, so "we have to get in the training business to be able to build a good supervision business."

What it costs. The deal is paid in stock that we value at about a third of its market price, so to shareholders the cost is 13.3 million shares at our value, about $1.7 billion, not $4.5 billion. At our weights Applied Compute is worth $2.21 billion, and the deal moves our fair value by +$0.64 a share.

What it risks. It moves the company into owning computing capacity, a business whose cash needs and cyclicality differ from security software; this is one reason we set the explicit-year cost of equity close to the stock's own 36-month beta rather than at its lower 60-month read. And the executives' performance stock units pay on NGS ARR and earnings per share: the proxy excludes the ARR of acquisitions costing more than 4% of market value, and at about 1.5% this one falls under that line, so Applied Compute's ARR would count toward the targets.

The buyback. The board extended the $1 billion repurchase authorization to 31 December 2027; no shares were bought in the quarter. Used in full at about today's price it would retire about 2.8 million shares, a fifth of the deal's issue and about half a year of SBC-related charges ($1.95 billion over the last twelve months). Bought at about three times our value, it would lower our per-share value to $124.20.

Cost of capital. We use 10.0% for the cost of equity in fiscal 2027–29 (the 10-year Treasury at 5.07% on 18 November plus a beta of 1.10 times a 4.5% equity premium), converging to 9.6% (beta 1.00) by fiscal 2033 in the bull and base; the bear stays at 10.0%. The stock's own 60-month beta is 0.92, but that window starts when Palo Alto Networks was a firewall company with almost no acquired capital; the 36-month window, the platform company that now exists, reads 1.16, and we shrink it a little toward the longer read. Acquisition risk stays out of the rate: integration and overpayment risk diversify away, so they sit in the cases and weights. Applied Compute's move into computing capacity is different, which is why we go no lower. September used 9.84% at a 4.67% Treasury yield. The only debt is CyberArk's convertible ($1.93 billion at fair value), so the WACC, 10.0%, is close to the cost of equity.

Return on capital. Before the Chronosphere and CyberArk deals of January and February 2026, invested capital was negative or near zero: customers' prepayments exceeded every operating asset, so the return was not high but infinite, and meaningless. The capital the WACC test is now about is the $24.7 billion the acquisitions cost, so we count goodwill and acquired intangibles in capital.

How to read it. The return has been about 5.0% against the 10.0% WACC: a weakness regime. Over the deal year, trailing after-tax operating profit moved −$0.05 billion on $23 billion of new capital, a return of −0.2%, as SBC from replacement awards and integration costs absorbed the acquired profit. To earn the WACC on what they cost, the acquired businesses need about $2.5 billion of after-tax operating profit a year; Idira, the largest, runs at about $1.4 billion of revenue. Our base terminal assumes 15% on new capital (bull 25%, bear 10%): a bet that the core's organic reinvestment, not acquisitions, sets the marginal return. At 10% in every case the fair value is $103.29.

A market cross-check, at zero weight. At $361.73 the stock trades at 85 times the midpoint of the company's fiscal 2027 non-GAAP earnings guide ($4.26–4.30), which excludes SBC; on trailing GAAP earnings the multiple is above 500, distorted by the deals. Fortinet trades at 43 times and Check Point at 18.6 times trailing GAAP earnings; CrowdStrike and Zscaler have no meaningful GAAP multiple. The definitions do not match, so the cross-check carries no weight. It says only that the price is a growth price, which §6 measures.

The price

What $361.73 requires

28%revenue growth a year for eleven years that the price requires, base case

Holding everything else in the base case, the price requires revenue growth of about 28% a year from fiscal 2028 to fiscal 2038, then 4%; or 19%, the base case's fiscal 2028 rate, held for 20 years; or the like-for-like ARR rate, 26.5%, for 12 years. On the base case's growth no operating margin closes the gap: even with all revenue as operating profit in the terminal and no SBC, the base is worth $299.89. A cost of equity of 5.7% in every year would do it. The bull case alone ($195) is worth about half the price.
