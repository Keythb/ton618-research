What Was the Most Likely Cause of This Crash?

Bitcoin fell 21.3% between 4 October and 13 November, where this note's measurements stop, and fell further over the weekend that followed. To that date the record splits the fall in two. For five weeks it fell with the Nasdaq, by about what its usual link to the Nasdaq implies. Then, over four days in which the Nasdaq ended where it began, interest-rate expectations jumped, the largest stock offering of the autumn set its price range, gold fell with Bitcoin, and borrowed positions built up during the decline were closed by force.

Every sharp fall in Bitcoin arrives with a dozen confident explanations, and this one has more than most: a record exchange theft, an index provider deleting the largest corporate holder, a chief executive's resignation at the most valuable chip company, an election, eleven straight days of withdrawals from the largest fund, a hot inflation report, a very large stock offering said to be draining cash, an exchange said to be selling its reserves. Each is true as an event. Whether any of them moved the price is a different question, and it can be tested. This note fixes the dates of the decline from the price alone, asks first whether the move is unusual and how much of it is simply the stock market, then lays each proposed cause against what it would have to leave behind in the data: in the hour it happened, in who sold, and in what else fell at the same time. The measurements stop at the close of 13 November; the fall continued past it, and §1 reports what followed. This is an account of what happened to that point. It says nothing about where the price goes next.

The questions

What this note answers

  1. What happened, exactly? The dates, the two phases, and where this decline ranks among Bitcoin's others.
  2. How much was just the market? The share that Bitcoin's ordinary link to technology stocks accounts for, and the share it does not.
  3. Who sold? Funds, long-held coins, miners, corporate holders and futures traders, in bitcoin and dollars on one scale, with the lines nobody can measure named.
  4. What started phase two, and what made it bigger? The sequence, hour by hour.
  5. Which popular explanations fail? The ten most repeated and twelve further arguments, each tested.
  6. What is the most likely cause, how confident is that, and what would change it?

The read. At the close of 13 November, where the measurements stop, this was not an unusual decline by Bitcoin's own record; it has gone deeper since (§1). To that close it had two causes in sequence. Phase one (4 October to 9 November, −13.1%) was the stock market: the Nasdaq-100 fund fell 10.2%, and Bitcoin's prior-year link to it reproduces 85% of Bitcoin's fall. Confidence about 70%. Phase two (9 to 13 November, −9.4%) was not: the Nasdaq-100 fund ended the four sessions 0.1% higher. Two triggers lead, at about a third each, and cannot yet be separated: the jump in interest-rate expectations after the 10 November inflation report, and cash raised ahead of Anthropic's stock offering, whose price range was printed the same day. The first rests on one week, since Bitcoin's standing link to yields is weak; the second on one earlier case, SpaceX's offering in June. Neither has a measured seller. Gold fell 6.3% in the same four sessions, which both predict and the explanations specific to Bitcoin do not. The amplifier is clearer than the trigger (about 85%): futures positions grew over three days as the price fell and were liquidated on 13 November. The largest measured selling group in both phases was long-term holders; about three-quarters of it cannot be tied to any group this note can measure, and it cannot be ruled out as a cause. Terms used throughout, defined once. Spot ETF: a US-listed fund that holds bitcoin itself; money leaving the fund means the fund sells coins. IBIT is the largest. Beta: how much one asset has typically moved for each 1% move in another; here, Bitcoin's fund against the Nasdaq-100 fund (QQQ), measured over the year before the decline. Residual: what is left of a move after the beta share is taken out. Long-term holder: a coin that has not moved on the blockchain for about 155 days or more; the count falls when such coins are spent. Perpetual future: a futures contract with no expiry, the main way to hold bitcoin with borrowed money. Open interest: the number of futures contracts outstanding; it rises when new positions open and falls when they close. Funding rate: the fee paid every eight hours between futures buyers and sellers; positive means buyers are paying to stay in. Liquidation: an exchange closing a borrowed position by force when its collateral runs out. Basis point (bp): one hundredth of a percentage point. Log points: returns measured so that they add across periods; over small moves, one log point is about 1%.

The other side, first

The case that Bitcoin had its own seller

The strongest case against this note's reading, in the terms its proponents would use.

  1. The largest seller by far was inside Bitcoin, and it sped up. Long-term holders' supply fell by 317,552 coins in forty days, about $25 billion at each day's price, against $3.2 billion that left the US spot funds. The pace was 4,280 coins a day from the series' peak on 24 July to the price peak and 7,939 a day after it. No stock-market story explains why coins held for five months or more would be spent at nearly twice the rate.
  2. Bitcoin took the falls and half the bounces. On the twelve days the Nasdaq rose during the window, compounding to +16.1%, Bitcoin's fund gained 8.0%. On the seventeen days it fell, compounding to −22.5%, the fund lost 25.8%. An asset that only followed the market would have come back when the market did. After 5 November the path the Nasdaq implied recovered about five points and Bitcoin's fund fell another eight.
  3. The buyers of the breakout left. The spot funds took in $3.06 billion in the eleven sessions of the September rally and gave back $3.16 billion over the window. The largest fund had withdrawals on eleven consecutive sessions through 13 November. The withdrawals were from Bitcoin funds, and at the largest fund they continued on every day the Nasdaq rose in November.
  4. The largest corporate holder was pushed out of the indexes. MSCI published its final rule on 15 October and its deletion list on 11 November, effective at the close on 30 November. Strategy's shares fell 32% over the window against 20% for Bitcoin's fund. The company holds 850,364 coins, pays dividends on preferred shares, and has already sold this year: its third-quarter report shows 5,552 coins sold in the quarter and 6,947 in the nine months to September, about $432 million, to fund those dividends and share repurchases. It is the one holder whose distress would land on the coin itself.
  5. Miners sold in size. One miner disclosed selling 13,597 coins after 30 September, about $1.1 billion, and said more would follow.
  6. In phase two it was Bitcoin, not crypto. The other large tokens usually move about 1.3 times as far as Bitcoin. From 9 to 13 November they fell less than Bitcoin did or about the same. Whatever hit the market that week weighed more on Bitcoin than its usual relationship to the other tokens implies.

What this note tests: claims 1, 3 and 5 are measured in §3; claim 2 in §2; claim 4 in §4 and §5, against the hour of each announcement and against every filing the corporate holders made; claim 6 in §4. What it cannot test: who owned the long-held coins that moved, and why; whether any holder sold through private dealing desks, which leave no public record; and what Strategy did in the week of 9 to 15 November, which it had not yet reported when this note was cut.

The decline

What happened, exactly

13th of 23by depth, among declines of 15% or more from a 90-day high since 2018; the median reached 23.0%

The dates below were set from the price path before any explanation was examined, by three rules. The peak is the highest daily close after the 30 June low: $86,521 on 4 October. Bitcoin first closed at that level on 21 September ($86,505) and spent two weeks beneath it; the decline is measured from the last close at the high. The cut, where the measurements stop, is the last settled daily close when the analysis was run: $68,096 on 13 November. It is not the end of the decline. The break between the two phases is the day on which a two-line fit to the path changes slope most cleanly: 9 November, $75,161. Before it the price lost about 0.3% a day; after it, 2.4% a day. One day stands out from the price alone and sits inside phase one: 5 November, when Bitcoin fell 5.8%.

Is this unusual? Not by Bitcoin's record, at the cut. Since 2018 Bitcoin has fallen 15% or more from its 90-day high twenty-three times, between two and three times a year, and eight of those have come since US spot funds launched in January 2024. The median such decline reached 23.0% before recovering. This one stood at 21.3% at the cut: thirteenth deepest of the twenty-three, fifth of the eight.

The speed of phase two is rarer than its depth. A four-day fall of 9.4% or worse has occurred in 6.3% of all four-day spans since 2013 and in 1.9% of spans since the funds launched, where it has now happened ten separate times, roughly once every three and a half months. Measured against how much Bitcoin had been moving beforehand (about 40% a year, annualized), the four days were a 2.3 standard-deviation move and the whole decline 1.8. The word in this note's title is the one readers are using. The measured description, to the cut, is a median-sized decline that sped up.

The decline sits inside a larger one. Bitcoin's record close was $124,824 on 6 October 2025. It fell to $58,525 on 30 June 2026, rallied 48% to the October peak, and by the cut had given back 66% of that rally. At the cut it stood 45% below the record and 16% above the June low. The decline did not stop at the cut; what followed is set out in the box below, and none of this note's measurements includes it.

After the cut. The fall did not stop at the close of 13 November. On Saturday 14 November Bitcoin closed at $63,821, down 6.3% from the cut. It traded as low as $61,946 at 01:00 UTC on 15 November, 9.0% below the cut and 28.4% below the 4 October peak, then recovered to close Sunday at $65,189, up 2.1% on the day, 4.3% below the cut and 24.7% below the peak. On the measure used in Exhibit 2 the decline at Sunday's close is 24.7% below its 90-day high, past the 23.4% median of the twenty-two earlier declines and the eleventh deepest of the twenty-three. Sunday's was a weekly close, below the 200-week average ($67,967), and the fourth close in a row below the 200-day average. Open interest in bitcoin futures on OKX rose 0.3% on Saturday and fell 3.5% over the two days; across venues, CoinGlass's tally, as reported by The Block, put liquidations from 8 a.m. New York time on Saturday to the same hour on Sunday at $2.02 billion, $1.68 billion of it positions that had bet on a rise, so forced closing continued past the cut. Implied volatility on Deribit rose from 39.9 to 47.3 on Saturday, its highest close since the peak, and closed Sunday at 44.0; stablecoin supply rose $0.1 billion over the two days. Binance's founder said on 15 November, as reported by The Block, that the exchange had not sold. In DefiLlama's readings Binance's disclosed wallets held 642,003 bitcoin at 00:00 UTC on 14 November and 633,334 at 01:28 UTC on 16 November, a fall of 8,669 coins (1.4%), about 4,200 a day, while its stablecoins rose $0.1 billion and the other nine exchanges' bitcoin fell by 160 coins. That is the direction the claim that Binance sold predicts, above the average size of its daily change since September, about 3,300 coins, and about a quarter of the 5% fall over ten days that the eighth finding in §6 sets as its mark; a sale on its own order book would not show in the wallets. Long-term holder supply and miner-wallet transfers for the weekend were not available when this revision was built. With the stock, bond and fund markets closed, the stock-market, rate and fund-flow tests cannot be run on either day, and neither of this note's two leading phase-two triggers can be tested in them. A fall on a day when those markets are shut is the kind of day the case that Bitcoin had its own seller points to. The fourth finding in §6 is the test it bears on, and it can first be read when the stock market and the funds reopen on 16 November. The ranking of causes in this note is a ranking for the period to the cut, and nothing here says whether the same causes are at work after it.
The market share

How much was just the market?

53%of the whole decline reproduced by Bitcoin's prior-year link to the Nasdaq-100; 85% of phase one, none of phase two

Bitcoin has traded with technology stocks for most of the past two years, so the first question for any fall is how much of it is that link and nothing more. The test is simple. Over the year before the window, Bitcoin's largest fund moved about 1.09% for each 1% move in the Nasdaq-100 fund. The link is loose day to day: the Nasdaq explains about a fifth of the fund's daily variation (R² 0.22). Hold that number fixed, apply it to what the Nasdaq did on each day of the window, and compare the result with what Bitcoin did.

Exhibit 3 — Bitcoin against what the Nasdaq implied

How to read it. Both lines start at 100 on the 5 October close, the first session after the weekend peak. The black line is the price of the largest Bitcoin fund (IBIT). The dashed gold line is where that fund would have been had it moved each day by exactly 1.09 times the Nasdaq-100 fund's move, the link measured over the prior year. The vertical dashed line is the break on 9 November, and the cream area is the gap between the two lines from 5 November on. Where the lines run together, the stock market accounts for Bitcoin's move; where they part, something else does. They run together for a month and part in the final week.

In phase one the two lines are hard to tell apart. The Nasdaq-100 fund fell 10.2%, semiconductor shares fell 23%, and the link implied an 11.0% fall in Bitcoin's fund against an actual 12.9%. That is 85% of the move, and the remainder is well inside ordinary daily noise. The broader market barely moved: the S&P 500 fund lost 2.6% over the whole window and bank shares rose. This was a fall in the technology and artificial-intelligence trade, and Bitcoin fell with it. Its daily correlation with the Nasdaq Composite was 0.73 inside the window against 0.46 in the year before.

The largest day makes the point most plainly. On 5 November the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.5%, its worst session since April 2025, after election results and a chipmaker's forecast raised doubts about spending on data centers. Bitcoin's two worst hours of that day were the hour of the US stock-market open and the hour after it. Its fund lost 5.1% on the day, less than the 7.1% the link implied.

Phase two is the opposite. From 9 to 13 November the Nasdaq-100 fund rose 0.1% and Bitcoin's fund fell 8.0%. The stock market explains none of the four-day move. It is not absent from every day of it: the broader Nasdaq Composite lost 0.5% over the four sessions, and on 13 November alone the Nasdaq-100 fund fell 1.2% and semiconductor shares 3.0%, which by the usual link accounts for about 1.3 of the 4.5 points Bitcoin lost that day. The Nasdaq's gains earlier in the week cancel it.

Two cautions belong beside the 85%. First, the link is not a constant. Measured over only the sixty sessions before the peak it was 0.79, not 1.09, and at that value stocks account for about 62% of phase one and 38% of the whole decline, not 53%. Second, the second point of the opposing case holds in full: Bitcoin captured 1.17 times the Nasdaq's losses on down days and 0.52 times its gains on up days. Most of that asymmetry comes from the week of 9 to 13 November, when stocks recovered and Bitcoin did not. Before 5 November the two lines never differed by more than about three and a half points. From 26 October, when the prospectus discussed in §4 became public, to 9 November the fund fell 4.3 points short of the link, all of it from 6 November on.

In log points, which add up: the Bitcoin fund lost 22.1 over the window. The Nasdaq link accounts for 11.6 (53%). Phase one's remainder is 2.1 (9%), too small to distinguish from noise. Phase two's 8.4 (38%) is entirely unexplained by stocks, and is the part the rest of this note is about.

The sellers

Who sold?

Someone sold every coin that changed hands, and several of the sellers can be measured. The ledger below puts each on one scale over the same forty days.

How to read it. Each bar is the net change, in bitcoin, in what one group of holders held over the window: bars to the left of the center line are net sales, bars to the right are net purchases, and the label gives the coins and, where a dollar figure exists, the value at each day's price. Bar lengths share one scale, so they can be compared directly. The rows overlap: a fund's or a miner's coins that were more than about 155 days old also count in the long-term holder row, so the bars must not be added together. The two lines at the foot name what cannot be measured. The chart shows one row far longer than every other seller, and its mirror on the buying side.

Long-term holders. The count of coins unmoved for about 155 days or more fell by 317,552 over the window, about $25 billion: 274,364 in phase one and 43,188 in the four days of phase two. The coins went somewhere: the count held by short-term holders rose by 335,680.

Two facts limit what this proves about cause. The series peaked on 24 July and fell by 308,193 coins between then and 4 October while the price rose from the mid-$60,000s to $86,521 (625,745 coins since July in all), so selling of this kind and size ran straight through a rally without stopping it. And its timing inside the window is mixed. The pace was 8,607 a day from the peak to 4 November, 1,510 a day over the five days before the break, then 10,797 a day in phase two, the fastest stretch of the window. Its two large falls in phase two, 26,767 on 10 November and 34,857 on 13 November, came on the leg's first and last days, both days the price fell; but it rose by 504 on 11 November and by 17,932 on 12 November, a day the price fell 3.4%.

The figure also measures a category, not a holder. Any wallet whose coins had sat for about five months counts, whether it belongs to an early holder, a fund's custodian, a miner or an exchange. It is not a count of sales, and it errs in both directions: a coin moved between one owner's wallets counts as spent, which overstates selling, and coins aging past the threshold over the same forty days offset spending, which understates the old coins that moved.

The named groups account for little of it. If every coin that left the spot funds (42,110), every coin miners disclosed selling (15,745), the miner-wallet transfers above their usual pace in phase two (about 7,100) and a transfer of 9,261 coins from wallets linked to the US government to Coinbase Prime on 6 and 7 October, reported by one outlet and not established as a sale, had been old enough to count, that is about 74,000 coins, under a quarter of the row. About 243,000 coins, roughly three-quarters, belong to none of the groups this note can measure; funds listed outside the United States, exchange wallets and treasury companies that do not file in the United States are among those it cannot.

Two further readings add detail without names. The profit ratio on long-held coins that moved was below 1, meaning sold for less than they cost, on 8 of the 35 days to 7 November, where the vendor's series ends; over the whole period they were sold at a profit (the ratio averaged 1.14). And the wallet-size bands show no exit by large wallets: wallets holding 1,000 to 10,000 coins ended the window about 20,000 coins higher, and those holding 100 to 1,000 ended it little changed after falling about 47,000 from a high on 2 November, with the caution that exchange and fund custody wallets sit in those bands.

Spot funds. The eleven US funds had net withdrawals of $3.16 billion, about 42,110 coins, on 18 of 30 sessions. The largest fund accounts for about half and had withdrawals on eleven straight sessions from 30 October. In phase two the funds lost $864 million, four sessions of four, 80% of it from the largest fund. The symmetry with September is exact enough to notice: $3.06 billion arrived in the eleven sessions of the breakout. The quarterly reports in which institutions list their holdings (Form 13F) show positions as of 30 September; they say nothing about who sold in November.

How much price can those withdrawals account for? This house's study of fund flows and price (605 trading days) found about 0.30% of same-day price change for each 1,000 coins of net flow. Applied here, the withdrawals line up with as much as 54% of the decline. That figure is a ceiling, and it overlaps the stock-market share; the two do not add. The same study found that flows follow the price by a day more strongly than they lead it, so part of every withdrawal is a response to a fall that had already happened.

Part of the withdrawals was not selling in the ordinary sense. Hedge funds commonly hold the funds' shares against a short position in CME bitcoin futures and earn the gap between the two; when they close the trade, coins leave the funds and futures are bought at the same moment. The regulator's weekly positioning report shows leveraged funds' net short in CME bitcoin futures falling by 22,416 coins between 6 October and 3 November, against 17,511 coins that left the spot funds over the same span. Three cautions apply. The weekly match is looser than the totals: the short fell in weeks of fund inflow too, and not every short is one leg of this trade. The usual reason to close was not present until the end: the gap between CME futures and spot ran at an annualized 4.6% to 5.4% through October against a 3.9% cost of overnight funding, and fell to or below that cost only over 10 to 13 November (2.9% on 13 November). And the reports cover 17,511 of the roughly 30,050 coins that left in phase one and none of phase two; the later reports were not yet released. For the weeks covered, much of the fund withdrawal is consistent with a hedged trade closing, which is less pressure on the price than an outright sale, though not none: the redemption still places coins on the spot market while the offsetting purchase is in futures. That takes some weight off the third point of the opposing case for phase one and leaves it open for phase two.

Miners. Companies that mine bitcoin disclosed 15,745 coins of sales, about $1.3 billion. MARA reported selling 13,597 after 30 September, at about $81,039 each, in filings dated 9 November, and used $750 million to repay loans secured on bitcoin; Hut 8 sold about 2,148 in October to buy back shares. Neither gives dates within those spans. MARA's average price points to October, when Bitcoin traded near that level, and a small part could fall on 1 to 3 October, before the window. All of it is dated on or before 9 November, so none of it belongs to phase two. No miner had filed an October or November production report by the cut, so this row is a floor.

A second source reaches into phase two. Coins leaving wallets identified as miners' ran at about 660 a day through the three months before the peak and through phase one, then 4,325 on 10 November and 4,260 on 13 November, the first and last days of the fast leg and, of its four days, the two on which long-term holder supply fell most. That is about 7,100 coins above the usual pace in four days, around $500 million, against about 12,000 leaving the spot funds. A transfer out of a miner's wallet is not proof of a sale, and on the 68 earlier days in the vendor's four-year record with outflows this far above normal, Bitcoin's moves were ordinary (median −0.05% on the day and +0.2% over the next five).

Corporate holders. The companies that hold bitcoin as a treasury asset bought. Strategy reported purchases in four of the five weeks from 5 October to 8 November, 2,364 coins in all, and wrote of the fifth week that it "did not purchase or sell any bitcoin". Strive bought 13,691 over the same weeks, though its buying fell from 6,182 coins in the week to 23 October to 29 in the week to 6 November. No company of this kind disclosed a sale in the window. Strategy is not a holder that never sells: its quarterly report shows 5,552 coins sold in the third quarter, about $349 million. But its weekly reports give its holdings at each week's end, and from 14 September on those holdings rise by exactly the coins bought, which places every one of those sales before the window. The treasury companies' purchases (16,055) almost exactly offset the miners' disclosed sales.

Futures. Futures traders do not sell coins, but their positions can be measured. On OKX, one of the largest venues, open interest in bitcoin perpetual futures rose from 31,108 coins on 21 October to 37,052 on 12 November, a 19% increase while the price fell 15%. The ratio of accounts positioned for a rise to accounts positioned for a fall went from 0.98 on 5 October to about 2.9 in November, and on Binance it also tripled and on Gate more than doubled. The count of accounts is not the weight of money, though. The funding rate on OKX averaged positive on 39 of the window's 40 days, but it sat below its neutral setting on about three readings in four at OKX, Binance and Gate, and on Binance the perpetual traded below the spot price in nearly every hour. Futures were under net selling pressure while the number of accounts betting on a rise grew. On Binance even the largest accounts' positions leaned long, about two to one, so the data do not show who was on the other side. Positions were being added into the decline on both sides. The same build shows on every venue with a public history: over 9 to 12 November open interest rose on OKX, Binance, Gate and Kraken, 6.6% taken together.

What cannot be measured. Private dealing desks, funds listed outside the United States, coins held on exchanges, and any holder who sold coins younger than 155 days. The marginal seller in any given hour may be in this row.

The sequence

What started phase two, and what made it bigger?

Phase two is where the stock market stops explaining anything, so it is where the other candidates have to be tested. Four dated events fall inside it.

How to read it. Two panels share one time axis, hourly from 9 to 13 November 2026 in UTC. The upper black line is Bitcoin's price. The inflation report (1). At 13:30 UTC on 10 November the October consumer price index came in at +0.4% for the month and +0.3% excluding food and energy, each a tenth above forecasts. The Federal Reserve had raised its policy rate in September, the first increase since 2023, and held it in October with two officials voting for another increase. Bitcoin fell 0.9% in the hour of the report and 2.0% over four hours. Between 9 and 12 November the two-year Treasury yield rose 30bp to 4.86%, its highest of the window, and the ten-year rose 22bp to 5.10%. On Polymarket, the probability that the Federal Reserve leaves rates unchanged in December fell from 22.5% on 9 November to about 1.5% on 13 November, most of it between the 10th and the 11th, and the probability of an increase of half a point or more rose from about 0.5% to about 10%.

Rates alone would be a thin case; yields rise on many days when Bitcoin does not fall. What makes it more than thin is gold. The largest gold fund fell 6.3% over the same four sessions, while the Nasdaq-100 fund ended the four sessions slightly higher and credit spreads narrowed. Bitcoin and gold pay no income, so both lose appeal when the return on cash and bonds rises. A fall in both at once, with stocks steady, is what a rate shock predicts. It is not what an index deletion, a miner's sales or fund withdrawals predict; none of those reaches gold. Adding gold to the stock-market yardstick lifts the explained share of phase two from nothing to 22%. In the prior year there were fourteen days on which the two-year yield rose 8bp or more; Bitcoin fell on ten of them, with a median move of −1.9%.

The limit of this reading should be stated as plainly. Over the prior year the day-to-day correlation between Bitcoin and changes in yields was about zero. Rates have mattered to Bitcoin on shock days, not as a steady relationship, and one week is one week.

The index deletion (2). MSCI's list became public on the evening of 11 November: The Block reported it by 23:09 UTC and the press release is stamped 23:32 UTC. Bitcoin rose 0.1% in the hour that contains both times and fell 0.2% over four. The following session Strategy's shares fell 6.5% against 4.5% for Bitcoin's fund: the announcement landed on the stock. That is what the mechanism implies. Funds that track the index must sell Strategy's shares by 30 November; nothing in the rule requires anyone to sell bitcoin. The coin would be affected only if the company itself sold, and its filings through 8 November show purchases.

The resignation (3). Nvidia's chief executive resigned before the US open on 12 November, after several days of reports about a board investigation, so part of the news was known earlier. Nvidia fell 13% at the open and closed down 4.3%; the Nasdaq-100 fund lost 0.3% on the day. Bitcoin fell 1.2% in the opening hour and 3.4% on the day. A fall in technology shares can explain a fall in Bitcoin. A day on which technology shares as a group barely fell cannot explain much of one.

The fourth day (4). The sharpest stretch began at about 02:00 UTC on 13 November, late evening in New York, with no dated event in that hour. The price fell 1.0% in the 02:00 hour, 1.8% in the 03:00 hour and 0.6% in the 05:00 hour, and open interest on OKX fell across those hours. By the end of the day open interest was down 9.2% and the price 4.5%. CoinGlass, as reported by The Block, counted $1.47 billion of positions liquidated across crypto in the 24 hours to 05:10 UTC, $1.31 billion of them bets on a rise.

This is the clearest finding in phase two, and it settles a question the popular account gets backwards. A liquidation cascade is often named as what started the fall. For three days the opposite was true: open interest rose 6.7% from 9 to 12 November, up on two of the three days, as the price fell 5.2%. Traders were adding positions into the decline, and on 13 November open interest fell on all four venues with a public history, 5.9% taken together, which removed almost exactly what the three days had added. Those positions were the fuel, and on the fourth day they were closed. Across the whole window, hourly changes in open interest follow price changes; they do not lead them. The build-up came first, and liquidation did not begin the leg, which was already three days old. Whether forced closing started the 13 November slide or followed its first hour cannot be read from hourly data.

The second candidate: cash for a very large stock offering. The explanation that spread fastest in the week of 9 November is that Bitcoin is doing what it did before SpaceX listed in June. In its strongest form it has three parts. Investors who want shares in a heavily subscribed offering sell what is most liquid first, and Bitcoin trades around the clock. Up to a fifth of the deal may go to retail buyers, who overlap heavily with holders of Bitcoin, so the two compete for the same money. And the pattern has happened before: Bitcoin fell about 19% in the fifty days before SpaceX's shares began trading, made its low nineteen days after the offering priced, and then recovered. This note tests the third part (Exhibits 6 and 7) and bears on the first only indirectly. It cannot test the second: no data set identifies subscribers who hold Bitcoin.

The two prospectuses fix the dates and sizes. SpaceX filed publicly on 20 May, printed its price of $135 on 3 June, priced on 11 June and raised $86.25 billion, the largest offering on record. Anthropic filed publicly on 26 October, printed a range of $70 to $78 on 10 November and expects to price on 18 November and trade on the 19th under the ticker ANT. It is selling up to $64.35 billion of shares, or $74.0 billion if the underwriters' option is used, with a further $26 billion placed privately with suppliers. The company expects retail allocations of up to 20%, about $12.9 billion at the top of the range, with final amounts set by demand, and its offering site tells applicants to be ready to pay for every share they request. Each company printed its price eight days before pricing.

  • The price fell from a dated event in both cases. In the 27 days before each prospectus became public Bitcoin fell 1.9% and 4.2%, the second in line with the Nasdaq. From the filing to pricing it fell 17.4% and 14.9%, with the Nasdaq-100 fund up 2.2% and down 3.5%.

Anthropic's filings through 15 November keep the range at $70 to $78 and the offering at 825 million shares. This confirms the premise of the explanation, a deal heavily subscribed while Bitcoin fell, but not its mechanism: institutional orders in a book this oversubscribed can be inflated in expectation of cutbacks and need not be funded before allocation, so the multiple bounds the cash that might have been set aside without measuring it.

What limits it:

  • Nobody has measured subscribers selling anything. No brokerage, exchange or data provider has published a figure for either deal. The one published test of the crypto leg, by CoinDesk on 6 June, found stablecoin and exchange flows inside their normal range. Retail money-market fund assets slipped about $17 billion, or 0.5%, around SpaceX's pricing and have been flat across this decline.
  • Buyers may have borrowed. Margin debt at brokerages rose $86.5 billion in June to a record and fell $84.8 billion in July. That is consistent with the SpaceX deal being financed on credit, which needs no sale of Bitcoin, though the rise began in April and the series cannot be tied to the deal.
  • The selling did not stop at pricing. A simple raise-cash-then-stop account puts the low at pricing. After SpaceX, Bitcoin fell another 7.8% and the spot funds lost a further 39,386 coins before the low.
  • The two run-ups differ in what else happened. Over the fifty days the Nasdaq-100 fund rose 10.1% before SpaceX and fell 7.9% before Anthropic; semiconductor shares rose 26.5% and fell 19.8%. Long-term holder supply rose before SpaceX and fell before Anthropic. Across the sixteen assets, the two fifty-day patterns are close to unrelated (a rank correlation of 0.11).
  • The pattern is thin. A fifty-day fall of this size has occurred in about one in ten such windows since the spot funds launched. The two price paths track closely (a correlation of 0.81, matched by about 3% of random pairs of windows), but their day-to-day changes barely correlate (0.18). Across sixteen large US offerings since 2012, Bitcoin fell in the fifty days before seven and rose before nine; the three that raised $20 billion or more, Alibaba in 2014, SpaceX, and SK hynix in July 2026, were each preceded by a fall of 18% to 24%, but the last two share one episode.
  • June had other explanations, and this time rates rose. Bitcoin's shortfall against the stock market predates SpaceX's filing, and the weeks that followed held a long run of fund withdrawals and concern about Strategy. From Anthropic's filing to the cut the two-year yield rose 17bp, and 30bp over the four days from the printing of the range, so in this episode the two leading explanations point the same way.

On this evidence the offering explanation stands level with the rate shock. It has what the rate shock lacks, a second episode in which Bitcoin fell by a similar amount over the same stage of an offering, that time with yields falling, and it lacks what the rate shock has, a price move in the hour of its event. Neither has a measured seller. The reported demand for Anthropic's offering confirms its premise and measures no seller, so it does not move this. The two are not exclusive: a market short of buyers because cash is committed elsewhere falls further on a rate shock. They make different predictions from here, which §6 sets out.

Three readings bear on who was selling in those four days. More than half of phase two (54%) occurred in Asia trading hours, and 12% in the hours the US stock market is open, which is when the funds trade. Coinbase, the main US venue, traded at a smaller discount to offshore venues than in phase one. And the other large tokens fell less than their usual multiple of Bitcoin's move, the reverse of phase one. The leg was heavier in Bitcoin than in crypto generally, and heavier outside US hours than inside them. That fits a shock to Bitcoin as a rate-sensitive asset and a forced unwind in offshore futures; it does not fit a US fund-led sale, and it leaves the spot seller in Asia hours unidentified. The same facts limit the rate reading: 23% of the leg printed in the wider US session (13:00 to 21:00 UTC, which includes the hour of the inflation report and most Treasury trading; the 12% counts only stock-market hours), and a rate shock does not by itself explain why other tokens, which also pay no income, fell less than Bitcoin.

Asia hours do not point to an Asian cause. Over the four days the yen weakened 1.0% and the Nikkei rose 4.0%, the opposite of the August 2024 unwind of yen-funded positions, and the premium Korean buyers pay over US prices rose to 1.5% from about 1.0% in phase one, so there was no sign of regional selling (Exhibit 8). On 13 November Bitcoin was already down 0.7% at 02:00 UTC while Japanese equity futures were still up and US ones flat, and the equities that followed fell less.

The Binance theft

The theft of about $6.3 billion began, by the tracing firms' accounts, at about 20:05 UTC on 29 October, inside the second-worst hour of the window (−2.5%), and the largest discount of US prices to offshore prices came over the next three days. But the same two sessions held earnings disappointments at two large technology companies: the Nasdaq-100 fund fell 4.8%, and Bitcoin's fund fell 6.0% against 5.3% implied. Whatever the theft added is less than a point.

A market that believed the exchange insolvent would leave marks that are absent here. Bitcoin on Binance never traded more than 5bp below its price on OKX, and during the halt in withdrawals it traded at a premium of about 4bp. Binance's own token fell more than Bitcoin on the theft days (7.3% against 4.9%), but 20.8% over the window against Bitcoin's 21.1%, and less than Bitcoin in phase two. Polymarket's contract on a major exchange being declared insolvent in 2026, which names Binance among five exchanges, stood near 3% before the theft, rose to 4.45% on 1 November and was at 2.45% through 11 to 13 November.

One mark is present, and it bears on the related idea that the episode served to discredit Binance and move business to US venues. Across Binance, Coinbase and OKX together, Binance's share of spot Bitcoin volume fell from about 58% before the theft to about 47% afterwards and stayed there, and Coinbase's rose from about 22% to about 28%. The shift dates from the theft, not from the selling. Activity moved onshore; coins did not: the US funds lost about 42,110 coins and US prices stayed at a small discount to offshore ones. A fall in Bitcoin's price hits every venue alike, so it is a poor instrument against one of them.

A stronger version holds that Binance is itself the large seller, short of the reserves it claims and selling bitcoin for cash. Five records bear on it.

  • What was stolen. By TRM Labs' tracing, 18,270 of the stolen coins were bitcoin, about $1.47 billion of the $6.36 billion, and none of it was frozen. Making customers whole on that part meant supplying bitcoin, not selling it.
  • What Binance said. Its co-founder Yi He wrote on 30 October that the company was buying back the stolen tokens with holdings that "include a large amount of assets such as BTC and ETH". Its founder wrote on 2 November that "Binance did not sell assets." The two statements do not sit easily together, and the company has not said how much bitcoin, if any, it converted.
  • The emergency fund. Half of the fund's 15,000 bitcoin left its published address on 31 October. Followed on-chain, about 4,800 of the 7,500 coins were consolidated on 3 November into a wallet that now holds about 173,000 bitcoin and that one tracking site labels a Binance cold wallet; the rest went out in separate transactions. Whether any were sold cannot be read from the transfers. The fund has not been refilled.
  • The wallets. As tracked by DefiLlama, whose readings are taken at 00:00 UTC, Binance's disclosed wallets held 640,832 bitcoin on 4 October and 644,676 on 13 November, 0.6% more with the theft in between, and the balance rose by 12,920 coins between the 9 and 13 November readings. Most of that rise, 9,246 coins, came in the day before the 12 November reading, the moment of the reserve report's snapshot, and may be stolen bitcoin being replaced. Binance did lag: nine other exchanges tracked the same way (OKX, Bybit, Bitfinex, Bitget, Gate, KuCoin, HTX, Deribit and Robinhood; Coinbase and Kraken are not covered) gained 7.1% together. Between the 1 and 3 November readings, as withdrawals reopened, Binance lost 25,997 coins (4.0%), and on 9 November it was still 13,291 below its 1 November level (2.1%) while the others had gained. Dollars left in larger size: stablecoins in its wallets fell from $53.0 billion to $41.3 billion, against falls of about $1 billion each at OKX and Bybit. Wallet data cannot split that between the theft and customer withdrawals, and cash raised to pay a reimbursement would not stay in the wallets, so the fall in dollars does not rule a sale out. From the 13 November reading to early on 15 November, the last day of the window and the day after it, Binance's bitcoin fell 6,071 coins (0.9%) and its stablecoins rose $0.6 billion while the other nine gained 964 coins. That is the direction the claim predicts, at about 3,000 coins a day, the average size of its daily change since September.
  • The reserve report. Binance's own report for 12 November, as carried by crypto.news, shows 688,283 bitcoin held against 687,912 owed to customers, a ratio of 100.05%. The report is the company's own and the margin is thin.

Taken together: Binance has said that its holdings, which include bitcoin, funded the buyback; whether any bitcoin was converted is not disclosed, and nothing located shows a sale at a size that matters beside the 317,552 coins long-term holders shed or the 42,110 that left the funds. The test has limits. Wallet balances combine customers' coins with the company's, and a sale by Binance to its own customers on its own order book, or from a wallet it has not disclosed, moves nothing the trackers can see. That part of the claim cannot be tested.

The argument that the fall was engineered

One explanation circulates after most sharp falls: that someone pushed the price down on purpose to force liquidations and collect the proceeds, the charge usually made under the word "manipulation". In its strongest form it runs as follows. Accounts positioned for a rise on borrowed money had tripled relative to those positioned for a fall, so a large pool of forced sellers sat just below the market. The two sharpest moves with no news behind them, the 21:00 UTC hour on Sunday 8 November and the 02:00 to 05:00 UTC hours on 13 November, both came outside US trading hours. On 8 November the densest cluster of long liquidations sat only 0.5% below the price, and a seller needs only to tip the nearest cluster; the forced selling does the rest. And $1.47 billion of positions were liquidated in the day to 13 November, money that went to whoever held the other side.

Intent cannot be read from prices, so the test is of footprints. A push designed to extract as much cash as possible has a recognizable shape: short positions are built first, so open interest rises while the funding rate turns negative; the selling leads in futures, where a small sum moves the price, and not in spot; the price is carried to the level where the most long positions are liquidated and stops near it; and once the forced selling has been bought, the price recovers and holds. A looser version, a push meant only to tip the nearest cluster and let the selling run, cannot be tested here, because it cannot be told apart from a cascade nobody started.

On those marks the record is mixed, and mostly against.

  • The size of the forced sale is as the argument says, and any liquidation produces it.
  • The timing is less telling than it sounds. Ranked by typical volume over the three months before, the Sunday 21:00 UTC hour sits near the middle of the week's 168 hours, and the four Friday hours from 02:00 to 05:00 UTC range from its busier third to its quieter third.
  • The build-up is ambiguous. Open interest rose over 9 to 12 November, and accounts positioned for a rise outnumbered those positioned for a fall about three to one. But funding, though positive, sat below its neutral setting on most readings, and perpetuals on Binance traded below spot in nearly every hour, so futures were under net selling pressure. That is what a short position being built looks like. It is also what hedging by funds and dealers looks like, and the two cannot be separated here.
  • Spot grew faster than futures. Average daily spot volume in phase two was about 80% above its level over the previous five weeks, and the ratio of futures to spot volume fell from 6.2 to 4.8, so coins were being sold.
  • The recoveries differ. After the 8 November slide the price recovered 1.8% in six hours and its daily closes stayed above that low for the next three days. After the 13 November liquidation it recovered 2.5% in six hours and fell below that low within a day.
  • Where each slide stopped is the sharpest mark. This house keeps a daily log of where estimated long liquidations cluster below the market. The rule was set in advance: a slide counts as aimed if its low lands at the densest cluster then below the price, or within 1% below it.

Nor do the clusters draw the price on ordinary days: across the 110 days the log covers, the day's low landed in that band on 8.2% of days, against about 10% for a level at the same distance given only how far the price fell on those days, and on the sixteen days with a fall of 3% or more it did so once, with a median overshoot of 1.7% among the nine that reached the cluster. Two of the five lows stopped just above a cluster; a low within half a percent of some cluster occurs on about a third of all days and on three of the sixteen days with a fall of 3% or more. The 13 November slide went through every logged level below it and kept going.

This does not show that nobody acted with that purpose, and the rule is narrower than the argument. A push meant to start a cascade, not to stop at a level, would look like 8 November and 13 November. A holder selling coins while short on a venue not measured here would leave the spot-led pattern that was seen, and the seller of spot bitcoin in Asia hours on 13 November is unidentified. The log is one reading a day of a vendor's estimate, positions grew about 4.5% on 12 November after that day's reading, and five events are few. What the record shows is that the level mark of a deliberate, cash-maximizing push is not there, that the recovery mark is there for one slide of the two, that the lean in futures is ambiguous, and that the moves can be accounted for without one. The finding would change if a later slide's low landed within 1% below the densest cluster then below the price after funding had turned negative with open interest rising and futures leading spot, or if an exchange or a regulator disclosed trading aimed at liquidations inside the window.

Against the rate shock: Bitcoin's weak standing link to yields; June, when Bitcoin fell after SpaceX filed with yields falling and recovered with them rising; and a leg of which only 23% printed in the wider US session, while other tokens, which also pay no income, fell less than Bitcoin. Against long-term holders: gold's fall, which that reading does not predict. It is largely untested rather than refuted.

The ranking sets the rate shock and the offering level, and both ahead of long-term holders, by judgment. The rate shock alone has a dated event inside the leg with a price move in its hour: the first fall came in the hour of the inflation report, and the repricing of the December decision came between the 10th and the 11th. The offering alone has a second episode: in June and again now, Bitcoin fell about a sixth between the public prospectus and pricing (this time measured to the cut) while the Nasdaq-100 fund rose 2.2% and fell 3.5%. Its price range was printed on the day of the inflation report, so that day's fall cannot be assigned to either, and both predict gold's fall. Long-term holders have timing by the day only: their two largest falls came on the leg's first and last days, but the series rose on 12 November as the price fell and did not lead the price over the window. Among the smaller candidates, miners were added on their wallet transfers on the leg's first and last days, which overlap the long-term holder row, and fund withdrawals were not lowered, because the positioning data that argue for a hedged trade stop on 3 November; the seventh finding below decides it. The percentages are a judgment, not a measurement, and the four smaller candidates are not ranked against one another.

The shares. The stock market accounts for 53% of the decline (38% on the lower estimate of the link), phase one's unexplained remainder for 9%, and the four days of phase two for 38%. Inside that 38% the trigger and the amplifier cannot be separated cleanly: 13 November was the largest of the four days, about 4.6 of Bitcoin's 9.9 log points on daily closes.

What the data cannot settle. Whether long-term holder selling was a cause or simply the supply that met whoever was buying; it is the largest measured group, and about three-quarters of it cannot be attributed to any group this note can measure (§3). Who sold spot bitcoin in Asia hours on 13 November. Whether miners' transfers on 10 and 13 November were sales, and what miners sold in October and November. Whether the fund withdrawals after 3 November were, like many before, a hedged trade closing. What Strategy did in the week of 9 to 15 November. Whether Binance sold bitcoin of its own to customers on its own order book, which wallet balances would not show. Whether any seller acted in order to force liquidations: intent is not observable, and a push meant only to start a cascade cannot be told apart from one nobody started. And whether the rate shock or deal-related cash raising did more of the work in phase two: the range was printed on the day of the inflation report, so the four days cannot separate them.

This house's own record. The Bitcoin Quarterly dated 22 September put a 22% probability on a new record high by year-end against 9.9% in the market, and 70% on $100,000 against 46%. It named a weekly close below the 50-week average as the condition on which that 22% would be withdrawn to the market's range, and a ten-session net outflow from the funds with every close above $80,000 as a signpost pointing the same way. Both were met during this decline: the outflow signpost on 8 October, four days after the peak, and the withdrawal condition on the 8 November weekly close ($74,968 against $75,488). The house's number was the higher of the two, and the condition set against it fired. The year-end contracts are unresolved; from the cut, the $126,200 high those contracts are settled against would require +85% in 48 days. A third signpost from July, a sustained premium on US venues over offshore ones, has not been met on any day since the September note. The June-low condition from July, a daily close below $58,525, has not been met.

What would change this view. Eight findings would move the ranking, and each is observable.

  1. A sale by a corporate holder. If Strategy's report for 9 to 15 November, or any treasury company's filing, shows bitcoin sold inside the window, the corporate-stress explanation moves from unsupported to the leading candidate for phase two. Fear of a forced seller and an actual sale are different findings; only the second is in the price mechanism.
  2. Miner reports. If a miner's report or filing shows that the transfers of 10 and 13 November were sales, or if November reports show sales for the month above the usual pace by about the 7,100 coins moved, miners move up for phase two. If not, the transfers stay unestablished.
  3. The rate link failing in reverse. If yields and rate expectations fall back while gold recovers and Bitcoin's gap to the Nasdaq does not close, the rate reading was a coincidence of one week and a seller specific to Bitcoin remains unidentified.
  4. The gap widening. If Bitcoin's shortfall against the Nasdaq-implied path, 10.5 log points at the cut, grows beyond 15 without a rate shock, the stock-market reading of phase one was the smaller part of the story.
  5. Long-held coins leading. Over the forty days the daily change in long-held supply moved with the same day's price (correlation 0.22) and did not lead the next day's (−0.20, the wrong sign for a lead); four days are too few to test the leg alone. If the decline extends past the cut and the daily series leads the price by a day over ten or more sessions, or an identified holder's filing dates sales ahead of the 13 November fall, long-term holder selling moves from a measured seller to the trigger.
  6. The offering against the rate path. Anthropic's pricing is reported for about 18 November and the Federal Reserve decides on 9 December. If Bitcoin's shortfall against the Nasdaq-implied path narrows in the weeks after the listing while the two-year yield stays at or above its 9 November level, the stock-offering explanation moves ahead of the rate shock. If the shortfall narrows only once yields fall, the rate shock stands and the June resemblance was coincidence.
  7. The positioning reports. If the regulator's reports for 10 and 17 November show leveraged funds' net short in CME futures still falling by about as many coins as left the funds, the fund withdrawals of the last ten days were a hedged trade closing and fall out of the ranking. If the net short rose, those withdrawals were outright selling and move up.
  8. Binance's bitcoin. If the bitcoin in Binance's disclosed wallets falls by more than 5%, about 32,000 coins, over any ten days while its stablecoins rise and the other nine exchanges' bitcoin does not fall, or if a reserve report, auditor or regulator shows the company's own holdings down by a comparable amount, the claim that Binance is a large seller moves from not supported to open.
