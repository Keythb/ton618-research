US Banking After the Print

Part 1 asked whether the first hike of the cycle would turn the banking system's paper loss on its bond books into a real one. Twelve prints later, on the banks' own documents, the forced-sale branch did not open beyond one sale of size and the capital branch ran as computed: the books were run off and hedged rather than sold, the mark took 10 to 38 basis points of CET1 where its reach is printed and the two ranges Part 1 said it would breach held, deposits stayed, and the managements that gave a deposit beta — the share of a rate change they pass on to depositors — gave last cycle's. Their story moved the other way, to two or three more hikes and more net interest income, and the one thing that changed in behaviour was the pace of capital return, slower at three of the twelve for reasons each gave itself. What the calls worried about was not the bond book but the assets the banks finance: six managements named a risk in the AI build-out and none sized it, and the loan category with the largest dollar increase in the system went unmentioned at the three banks Part 1's test tracks. The market sold nine of the twelve on the day; tangible book per share fell at six of nine, the route by which the mark reaches a share price whether or not it reaches a ratio. Of Part 1's thirteen tests two fired, three held, Part 1 missed twice on its own rules, and the rest wait for the 10-Qs and the FDIC.

Terms used throughout, defined once. AOCI: accumulated other comprehensive income, the equity line where unrealized gains and losses on available-for-sale (AFS) bonds sit after tax, alongside cash-flow hedges, pension items and currency translation; held-to-maturity (HTM) bonds are carried at cost and their loss is a footnote. The mark: the unrealized gain or loss on a bond book. CET1: common equity tier 1, the core regulatory capital ratio. Tier 1 leverage: Tier 1 capital over average assets, no risk weights; Schwab's "adjusted" version puts AOCI back in. ROTCE / P/TBV: return on tangible common equity, and price to tangible book value, the two axes of the line in §5. Deposit beta: the share of a change in the policy rate that a bank passes through to the rate it pays on deposits, quoted cumulatively over a hiking or cutting cycle and usually on interest-bearing deposits; at a 50% beta a 25-basis-point hike raises the deposit rate about 12.5 basis points once repricing is complete. "Last cycle's" betas are the pass-through the managements saw over the last hiking and cutting cycle, which they now cite as the path for this one: "about a 50% or so" at U.S. Bancorp, "in the mid-50s" at PNC. NDFI: nondepository financial institutions — private-credit funds, mortgage companies, broker-dealers, insurers. Fired / held / disclosed / none / pending: a tripwire's condition was met; was tested and not met; a management stated the thing asked about; the documents were silent, scored as the silence branch Part 1 froze; cannot be tested until a later document.

The other side, first

The case that the week was worse for the sector than this note reads it

Part 1 read the sector as healthy and the paper loss as an earnings drag, and tested the case that it was less healthy than that. The reading of this week most favorable to that case, in its own terms:

  1. The marks that are visible moved more, not less. Total AOCI fell $12.1 billion at JPMorgan, $8.2 billion at Wells Fargo, $3.2 billion at Bank of America, $2.5 billion at U.S. Bancorp and $2.3 billion at Citigroup — at JPMorgan 1.7 times and at Wells Fargo 1.6 times the after-tax AFS projection. JPMorgan's roll shows $7.9 billion of that was securities; scoring Wells Fargo, U.S. Bancorp and Citigroup as "pending" because theirs is not broken out is generous: the money moved out of equity whatever line it sits on, and Wells Fargo's own slide says it took 38 basis points off CET1 and left the ratio at the bottom of its 10–10.5% range, with the buyback now sized, in its words, considering "the impact on accumulated other comprehensive income".
  2. The loss sales are not an accounting footnote. JPMorgan's $658 million is its second consecutive quarter of selling bonds at a loss and two-thirds larger than the first; it cut its buyback from about $7 billion a quarter to $664 million in the same quarter; Wells Fargo, U.S. Bancorp, BNY and PNC all booked losses. Five of eleven banks selling at a loss in a quarter the 5-year rose 90 basis points is the first step of Part 1's §8 chain, not the absence of it.
  3. The cost of money is already outrunning the funds rate. The average funds rate rose 3.6 basis points in the quarter; the money centers' interest-bearing deposit costs rose 5 to 7, BNY's 10. The hike had fourteen days in the quarter; the deposits had the whole of it. Noninterest-bearing deposits lost share at Wells Fargo and State Street, deposits fell 2.5% at State Street and 2.2% at Schwab's bank, Schwab's Home Loan Bank borrowings quadrupled, and Schwab's "transactional sweep cash" rose only because it counts short credits on long/short strategies.
  4. None of the three tracked banks disclosed the nonbank book, because nobody asked. Silence at JPMorgan, Wells Fargo and Morgan Stanley is scored as silence, but fifty-nine analyst questions, as the archive counts them, and not one on the fastest-growing loan category in the system is not evidence the book is fine; it is evidence the visibility gap Part 1's first steelman point rested on is intact after the quarter that was supposed to test it — and the one bank that did print a page on it, U.S. Bancorp, is outside the test and is trimming balances in it.
  5. The market read the week the other way. Nine of twelve fell on their print days, eight underperformed the financials index, Citigroup lost 7.3% over two sessions, Bank of America 4.2%, State Street 3.7%; tangible book per share fell at six of the nine that print it. JPMorgan and Bank of America missed the archived consensus on every basis, Schwab and BlackRock on the reported figure, and returns on tangible equity fell quarter on quarter at nine of the ten that print a comparable one (Goldman's being its return on equity).
  6. The two ranges held on one-offs. State Street's leverage ratio rose, in its own words, "primarily reflecting the preferred share issuance completed during the quarter"; Schwab's on an AOCI that did not move for reasons it has not yet explained and a buyback cut in half. A range held by issuing capital and buying back less is the mark reaching capital, which is what the tripwire was built to detect.
  7. The managements named the AI build-out as a credit risk themselves, and none sized it. JPMorgan's chief financial officer would be "surprised if anyone frankly was immune" to a change in sentiment, says the exposure is "increasingly" in "working capital revolvers" and "kind of everywhere", and none of the twelve put a number on it; the one bank that said it has "very hard limits" did not say where they are.

Of these, this note tests (1) in §1 and §4, where the six banks that print or roll the securities mark are scored against it and the total-AOCI banks are shown with their components where any bank gives one; (2) in §1, §2, §4 and §6, where the loss sales are sized against the books and the buyback cut against the chairman's stated reason; (3) in §1, on both readings of the funds rate, with the betas the managements stated; (5) in §5, where the reactions and the return moves are put against the line; (6) in §1 and §4, where both ratios are bridged component by component; and (7) in §1, finding five, and §3, on the words. It cannot test (4) beyond the silence branch Part 1 froze — the banks' own documents are the only admissible source, and they are silent — nor whether the AOCI moves at the three total-only banks (and the two that print no line) are securities or hedges, which only the 10-Qs settle, nor whether a quarter's reaction is information or positioning, nor the size of the AI-financing exposure in (7), which no release prints and only a 10-Q or a fourth-quarter breakout could.

What the week taught

What the week taught about the industry

6findings the twelve documents support together: the bond book, the cost of money, the earnings story, capital, the AI cycle, credit

One. The bond book was run off and hedged, not sold, and the mark reached capital by about the amount Part 1 computed. Part 1's question was whether the hike would turn the system's paper loss into a real one. The managements answered it with what they are doing with the books. Bank of America "funded that growth by reducing securities balances" — $34 billion of loan growth — and runs its held-to-maturity book off at about $9 billion a quarter; U.S. Bancorp's portfolio "has been coming down, and we have been doing more in cash"; BNY has "roughly about $5 billion of securities rolling off per quarter" reinvested 150–200 basis points higher; PNC lengthened its duration to 4.1 years from 3.6 with $5 billion of 2.65% paper rolling off in the fourth quarter; Schwab still has "$190 billion roughly of securities that are yielding sub 2%" and is letting them roll into lending; State Street's available-for-sale duration is "around one year". The self-heal Part 1 sized at $21 billion a quarter for the system is the earnings story each of them told: higher reinvestment yields on a shrinking book. Five banks sold at a loss and one in size — JPMorgan's $658 million, which its release calls "discretionary" with no reason given — and no one disclosed a transfer into or a sale out of held-to-maturity, so the branch of Part 1's chain that runs from crystallization to liquidity did not open. Where the mark is printed and the book is unhedged, the quarter moved it 0.90 to 1.15 times Part 1's projection (Bank of America 1.15, JPMorgan's securities line 1.09, State Street 0.99, PNC 0.90); BNY's hedged book moved 0.55 times the unhedged projection, and Schwab's whole AOCI moved $164 million against $1.4 billion projected, the one number in the week Part 1 got wrong in size. Where the mark's reach into capital is printed it matched: about 36 basis points of CET1 at JPMorgan against 29 computed, 38 at Wells Fargo against 36, about 10 at Bank of America against 10. Three banks print only a total AOCI line — Wells Fargo's fell $8.2 billion, 1.6 times the after-tax projection, U.S. Bancorp's and Citigroup's 1.4 times — and two print none, and the two that print both lines show why the totals are not scored: at Bank of America "a little less than half" of the fall was cash-flow hedges that never reach capital. The bank-by-bank scoring, the totals and the loss sales are in §4.

Two. Deposits stayed, and the cost of money rose by less than the hike and more than the average funds rate. The average rate paid on interest-bearing deposits rose at every bank that prints it — JPMorgan 2.18% from 2.11%, Bank of America 2.03% from 1.98%, Citigroup 2.76% from 2.71%, Wells Fargo 2.05% from 1.99%, BNY 2.43% from 2.33% — and period-end deposits rose at eight of the eleven with bond books, 2.5% at JPMorgan, 7.2% at Morgan Stanley, 3.7% at PNC; they fell 2.5% at State Street and 2.2% at Schwab's bank, and BNY's quarterly average fell 0.9%. None reached the −3% that, with a loss sale, fires the thirteenth tripwire. The hike came on 16 September, fourteen days before the quarter closed, and added 25 basis points to the target; on the quarter's average the effective funds rate rose 3.6 basis points, from 3.631% to 3.667%. Against the hike, deposit costs rose a fifth to two-sevenths as much, two-fifths at BNY; against the average they rose more, which the managements that explained it put down to mix rather than to the policy rate — PNC to brokered certificates and commercial balances, U.S. Bancorp to about 2 basis points from the hike itself, with no reason given for the rest. Part 1 wrote "rising more than the funds rate did" and did not say which funds rate. The house reads it as the hike, because the observable was written about the hike's pass-through and a 25-basis-point step cannot show in a 3.6-basis-point average; on that reading it did not fire, on the other it fired, and both are scored in §6 so that the fourth quarter, the first full quarter at 3.75–4.00%, settles it with a reading rule that is no longer open. The cheapest money held its share at JPMorgan (24.0% of average deposits from 23.7%) and Bank of America (26.5% from 26.4%), slipped at Wells Fargo (24.3% of period-end deposits from 24.7%) and fell at State Street (13.4% from 14.5%); the uninsured shares, the second half of the funding test, are 10-Q and Call Report figures and are pending.

The betas, as stated. None of the money centers gave a figure. JPMorgan's chief financial officer said consumer deposit betas have run "lower-than-modeled" in what he called "a different cycle", so the firm is "a little bit more asset-sensitive than the EaR shows", and expected the low-beta consumer money to "behave roughly the way it has been behaving"; Citigroup's said betas are "performing in line with our expectations"; Wells Fargo expects this cycle's to match the last cycle's component for component — "not … that different versus what we saw last go around" — and "slightly higher" in aggregate because of the commercial mix; Bank of America's chief financial officer, asked to unpack the betas behind a 2027 outlook that carries three more hikes, said "we came from 4.5% down to 3.75% – 3.5%. Now we're retracing steps, I would anticipate it will look the same going back up as it did coming down". The regionals gave numbers: PNC expects a cumulative beta "in the mid-50s", its 7-basis-point rise to 1.98% "primarily driven by mix"; U.S. Bancorp assumes "about a 50% or so" on the hikes ahead and puts "approximately 2 basis points of impact from the September Fed rate hike" in its 7. BNY cited "deposit margin compression" and gave no figure; State Street said it would be "very attentive to deposit betas and repricing"; Schwab's sensitivity table assumes a zero deposit beta and its chief financial officer said the September cash move "is not cash sorting in any way"; Goldman Sachs was not asked and did not say; Morgan Stanley gave no figure. Nothing in the week contradicts Part 1's reading that the betas the July calls leaned on are intact; nothing in it tests them either, because the hike had two weeks.

Schwab's cash. Transactional sweep cash ended September at $492.8 billion, $7.1 billion above June's $485.7 billion and $19.8 billion above the $473 billion that fires the tripwire. The release attributes the rise to "continued demand for long/short strategies and organic growth, partially offset by client asset allocation decisions", and footnote 7 defines the measure to include "short credits related to certain client long/short strategies", which Schwab's deck puts at $63.3 billion after a $19.6 billion build in the quarter. On the balance sheet, bank deposits fell to $244.1 billion from $249.7 billion (−2.2%) and Federal Home Loan Bank borrowings rose to $2.0 billion from $0.5 billion; the chief financial officer told the call that "even with those higher rates, this is not cash sorting in any way". The tripwire, as frozen, is held and the deposit question stays closed this quarter; the steelman's reading, that the measure rose on short credits while the bank's deposits fell, is correct on the balance sheet. There was no stand-alone September activity release; the figures are the release's own monthly table, and the October report is the next read.

Four. Capital return slowed for three different stated reasons, and the firm that kept buying most says the final rule sets its pace from here. The quarter's rate move cost CET1 30 basis points at JPMorgan (to 13.9%: $7.9 billion of securities losses, $51 billion more risk-weighted assets and distributions), 37 at Bank of America (to 10.9%, 90 basis points over its minimum, with a step-up to 10.50% due on 1 January), 30 at Wells Fargo (to 10.0%, the bottom of its 10–10.5% range, 38 of it the AOCI decrease) and 50 at Goldman Sachs (to 12.4%, on $26 billion more risk-weighted assets, 100 basis points over its requirement); it left Citigroup and Morgan Stanley flat, and lifted State Street 20 on lower risk-weighted assets. The responses were not of a kind. JPMorgan cut its buyback to $664 million net from a cadence an analyst put at $7 billion, its chairman giving the reason as redeploying "probably $30 billion or something of excess capital" at better returns than buying the stock at "a 6% or whatever the earnings yield would be", and saying the cut had "nothing to do with AOCI or guessing about the environment or even Basel III"; U.S. Bancorp paused repurchases after $12 billion of risk-weighted-asset growth and a 40-basis-point AOCI hit to its AOCI-inclusive ratio, and expects to resume "over the next quarter or two"; Schwab halved its buyback to $0.5 billion and "allowed capital to build"; Wells Fargo will size repurchases quarter by quarter and "continue to consider the interest rate environment and the impact on accumulated other comprehensive income when determining how much stock we repurchase"; State Street issued $496 million of preferred, held its buyback at $400 million and guided it up to about $700 million; Bank of America returned $8.3 billion and called the pace "business as usual … with one exception. We need to wait for the final rules"; Goldman returned $4.49 billion and said it is hopeful the endgame and surcharge proposals are finalized by year-end; Morgan Stanley bought back $1.5 billion on a 300-basis-point buffer and looks to "upcoming transparency as our regulators finalize the bank capital regulatory framework"; PNC held at $0.6 billion with the fourth quarter guided the same; BlackRock at $550 million. Three managements dated the final rules to the fourth quarter or year-end (Goldman Sachs, Bank of America, U.S. Bancorp), three more referred to their finalization without a date (Wells Fargo, Morgan Stanley, PNC), and at JPMorgan and Citigroup the date came from the analysts' questions; Wells Fargo put the relief at about 7% of risk-weighted assets, U.S. Bancorp at about 5%, and U.S. Bancorp said it is "fully ready to transition into" Category II regardless and is "not counting on any benefit". The finding has two halves that the documents keep apart. The three that cut or paused gave reasons of their own: JPMorgan redeployment, U.S. Bancorp risk-weighted-asset growth and the mark, with regulation named among the drivers of its restart, Schwab a capital build against volatile rates. Of the firms that kept buying, Bank of America said the final rules set its pace from here, Wells Fargo named rates and AOCI, Goldman gave a hope of year-end finalization and no pace, and Morgan Stanley no pace at all. The two ranges Part 1 watched are the sharpest case; §4 bridges both. What separates the two readings is whether the buybacks resume when the rule lands with the marks unchanged, which the reversal block in §6 sets as the test.

Five. The worry has moved to the assets the banks finance: six managements named a risk in the AI build-out, none sized it, and the deposit side of it was dismissed, discounted or conceded as not-yet at every call that asked. The AI cycle reached the calls from two directions, and the managements answered them differently. As the thing the banks finance, it came up on nine of the twelve calls — at eight because an analyst asked, Goldman Sachs taking three questions and PNC two, and at Wells Fargo from both sides, an analyst asking and the chief executive raising it himself: "The scale of investment here increasingly resembles prior infrastructure booms, creating both significant opportunities but also risks given the significant leverage used to finance the buildout". Six managements named a risk in it. JPMorgan's chief financial officer, asked about concentration risk, said the theme "is so broadly diffused into the marketplace, it's not just traditional construction lending. The pricing test rolls Part 1's June tangible common equity forward by each company's own reported change (tangible common equity where printed; tangible book per share times shares at Citigroup, Wells Fargo and BNY; book value per share less June's goodwill and intangibles at Goldman; total equity less June's preferred, goodwill and amortizing intangibles at Schwab) and re-strikes price to tangible book at the 15 October close; the return is the nine months' net income to common, annualized, over the average of year-end 2025 and September tangible common equity; the fitted value is Part 1's line. PNC's swap leg is the AOCI change less the securities change after tax at 23%. The deposit-cost test is read against the 25-basis-point hike as frozen and against the 3.6-basis-point change in the quarter's average funds rate as a second reading.
