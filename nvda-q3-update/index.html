NVIDIA (NVDA) — Priced for One More Year

NVIDIA's third quarter beat our July top case and the company raised next year's growth to about 75%. The same week its founder resigned as chief executive, and the stock closed at $155.46, below our fair value for the first time since we began covering it. At that price the market pays for the year the company has guided and bought supply for, and for almost nothing after it. We value the stock at $170.43, from $162 in August.

TON618 Capital Research · As of 17 November 2026 · Download PDF · Download model

In July we called NVIDIA "priced for the Clock not breaking": at $202.81 the stock needed about $1 trillion of revenue by fiscal 2031, and the risk sat outside the company, in its customers' capital budgets. In August, at $227.98, the price sat above even our top case. Since then the operating numbers have run ahead of every case we wrote. Third-quarter revenue was $115.8 billion, up 103% on a year earlier; fiscal 2027 is tracking about $424 billion, against the $400 billion our July top case assumed; and on the call the company raised its fiscal 2028 growth outlook to about 75%. The stock went the other way. It closed at a record $239.24 on 6 October, fell 9% in the 5 November chip rout, and fell again in the week Jensen Huang resigned as president, chief executive and director, on 11 November. It closed at $155.46 on 17 November, the day of the print, down 35% from the high. This note asks why, and what the price now assumes.

The view. We value NVIDIA at $170.43 a share, 9.6% above the $155.46 close of 17 November. The print beat; the price fell for other reasons: higher rates, a sector rout, and a fall of about 16% beyond the sector's in the weeks the founder left and the board opened its investigation. In our base case, $155.46 is what the company is worth if revenue grows about +70% in fiscal 2028, a little under the company's own guide, and then only with the economy: one more year of supernormal growth, the year the company has guided and bought $203 billion of supply for, and none after it. We think that is too little, but the gap is not wide. Our value rests on four years of fading growth after fiscal 2028 and on a cost of equity that steps down from 13.2% to 11.6% (12.4% in the bear) as the business matures. Hold 13.2% in every year and the value is $149.72, below the price; weights of 40/40/20, which raise the bear (demand breaking in calendar 2027, the commitments turning into charges) to 40%, give $153.10. At the peer-median terminal beta the bear already uses, applied to every case, it is $159.88. Two single-input changes in the sensitivity table put the value below the price.

Terms used throughout, defined once. Fiscal year: NVIDIA's year ends in late January, so fiscal 2028 is roughly calendar 2027. 14-week quarter: NVIDIA's fiscal 2027 has 53 weeks; the extra week falls in the fourth quarter, which is why we restate that quarter on 13 weeks. Supply and capacity commitments: purchase obligations to suppliers, most of which NVIDIA must pay whether or not it needs the parts (its 10-Q notes some can be canceled, rescheduled or adjusted); this quarter's increase was mostly memory. Credit support: a guarantee that NVIDIA pays a lender or landlord if a partner defaults. Private book: NVIDIA's stakes in private companies, carried at cost adjusted for impairments and observed funding rounds. Neocloud: a company that rents out GPU capacity and is not one of the large cloud providers. NOPAT: operating profit after the tax it would bear. FCFF (free cash flow to the firm): NOPAT less what the business reinvests in plant and working capital. ROIC: NOPAT over the capital the operations use. WACC: the blended return the company's lenders and shareholders require.

The two cases

The cases on either side of the price, in their own terms

The case that $155.46 is fair or high. It does not deny the print. It says the print is the top of a capital-spending cycle, priced for what follows.

  1. Growth is slowing into a commitment stack that keeps growing. On 13 weeks the fourth-quarter guide is up about +4% on the third quarter, after 20%. Gross margin steps down about three points, to 71–72%. In the same quarter supply commitments rose by $111 billion to $390 billion, $203 billion of it due in fiscal 2028. Guarantees rose to $118.0 billion, net additions to the private stake book were $15.4 billion, receivables rose $18.1 billion and raw materials quadrupled from January. Each is a bet that fiscal 2028 arrives as guided.
  2. The demand is financed, in part, by NVIDIA. NVIDIA holds about $118 billion of stakes in customers and partners, guarantees $118.0 billion of their obligations and has $32 billion of contingent neocloud agreements. It recorded a $1.8 billion impairment on the private book this quarter. Its interim chief executive told analysts it would take "a very strong look" at whom it helps finance, because "a lot of things have changed over this last quarter" (one transcription).
  3. The founder's departure is a real loss. Jensen Huang ran the company for 33 years and set the yearly architecture cadence in public. The interim chief executive is also the chief financial officer. The company says an independent committee of the board is investigating, and the board has formed a committee named for national security and export control. In the same quarter, revenue from customers headquartered in China rose nearly fourfold, to $11.05 billion.
  4. The discount rate went up. The 10-year Treasury yield was 5.12% on 16 November, against 4.55% on 17 July; July's model used a 4.30% risk-free rate. Moving that input to today's yield at July's beta takes $16.56 a share off July's method.
  5. Margins are a peak, not a plateau. Frontier labs are leaning on custom chips (ASICs), and memory costs are rising. A 75% gross margin on systems others build cannot hold once the market stops being supply-constrained.

The case that $155.46 is too low. The company is supply-constrained in a market it says is still accelerating. It has bought the components for its guided year, and that year alone carries most of today's price.

  1. The year is bought. Fiscal 2028's $203 billion of commitments is about the cost of goods of the ~75% the company guides, at the 72–73% gross margin it reaffirmed. The four largest buyers' third-quarter capital spending rose 108% on a year earlier; every forward statement they gave pointed up, Microsoft's on its fiscal year to June 2027, and none cut 2026 (their third-quarter results and calls).
  2. The product cadence held through the change at the top. Vera Rubin generated "nearly $20 billion" of revenue in its first production quarter, which the company calls its fastest ramp. Rubin Ultra is "on track" for next year and Feynman for 2028.
  3. The price already assumes the plateau the other side fears. In our base case, $155.46 is what the company is worth if growth stops at the guided year. Every year of growth after fiscal 2028 is value the price does not include.
  4. Returns on capital are extraordinary. NVIDIA earned 206% on its operating capital over the last twelve months, against a WACC of 13.15%.
  5. Capital is coming back to shareholders. The quarterly dividend went from $0.01 to $0.25 a share in the second quarter of fiscal 2027. The company returned $28.1 billion in the quarter and plans to use the remaining $227.3 billion authorization by the end of fiscal 2028; at $155.46 that retires about 6.0% of the shares.

This note tests the first case's claims 1 and 2 in full, the rise in the rate in claim 4, and claim 3 as far as the price and the filings allow (§3); it sizes claim 5 through the terminal margin and the cases. Whether the rate stays high as the business matures it cannot test: that is a judgment, argued in §4, with its own tripwire. Of the second case it tests claims 1, 3 and 4 and sizes claim 5; claim 2 is the company's statement. Before the 10-Q it cannot test what the new $9.5 billion guarantee backs, how much of the commitments are memory prepayments, or what the board's investigation will find. Those are tripwires in §7.

The print

The quarter: a beat, then the calendar, the memory bill and the launch

Revenue was $115.8 billion, up 20% on the second quarter and 103% on a year earlier. Data Center revenue was $107.6 billion (+110%). Within it, the company's new split shows Hyperscale at $60.1 billion (+71%) and AI Clouds, Industrial and Enterprise at $47.5 billion (+195%). Edge Computing was $8.2 billion (+42%). The new names map onto the old segments without a gap: Data Center is the same line, at $51.2 billion a year ago under either name, so Edge Computing ($5.8 billion a year ago) is everything else, the old Gaming, Professional Visualization, Automotive and OEM lines. Gross margin was 74.8% (GAAP), operating income $77.5 billion and diluted earnings per share $2.70 (GAAP) and $2.68 (non-GAAP), above consensus estimates for non-GAAP earnings that ranged from about $2.47 to $2.52 (Benzinga, Zacks, Chartmill). GAAP earnings include $1.8 billion of net gains on equity stakes: $2.8 billion on public stakes, less a $1.0 billion net loss on private ones that includes a $1.8 billion impairment. Free cash flow was $43.8 billion, 38% of revenue, against 22% in the second quarter.

Four things shape the guide before it reads as a slowdown.

  • The calendar. The fourth quarter has 14 weeks. The company gave no 13-week figure; pro rata, $130.0 billion is $120.7 billion on 13 weeks: +4% on the third quarter, not the +12% the headline implies.
  • The memory bill. The guided gross margin of 71–72% is about three points below the third quarter's. The company called the fourth quarter "the bottom" in the Q&A (one transcription) and said in its prepared remarks that it expects "Q4 to mark the bottom of gross margins before improving to the 72% to 73% range in fiscal year 28" (both transcriptions). That is the path it gave in August. In the Q&A it tied the trough to honoring purchase orders it had already accepted before moving to new pricing (one transcription). Separately, the supply commitments rose "primarily" on "the multi-year procurement of memory supply" (CFO commentary).
  • The launch. Inventory rose $8.1 billion to $39.7 billion "to support our Rubin product ramp". Raw materials were $15.8 billion, against $3.8 billion in January. Receivables rose $18.1 billion to $81.0 billion, 64 days of revenue against 60. The company gives two reasons: shipments came late in the quarter at the Rubin launch, and it gave "extended payment terms on large, multi-quarter agreements with certain investment-grade customers". The first reverses next quarter. The second is not new: the second-quarter 10-Q already disclosed longer terms, of 90 days to a year, for investment-grade customers. It does not reverse with the launch, so it is a tripwire.
  • The cash. The company says the sequential rise in operating cash flow, to $48.3 billion, was also affected by the timing of federal cash tax payments. Over nine months, working capital and other operating balances absorbed $46.8 billion. Free cash flow moved from 22% to 38% of revenue in one quarter, and part of that swing is timing, not profitability. That is why our model carries working capital as its own line and no longer uses a flat cash margin (§4).

The commitments

The commitments: the guided year is bought, and the stack is geared to it

The CFO commentary's commitment table is the most important exhibit in the release. Supply and capacity commitments were $390 billion at 25 October: $69 billion due in the rest of fiscal 2027, $203 billion in fiscal 2028, $92 billion in fiscal 2029, then $10 billion in fiscal 2030. Add cloud-service agreements ($28 billion), data-center leases not yet begun ($74 billion, $63 billion of it from fiscal 2032), equity-investment commitments ($20 billion) and capital spending ($6 billion), and the total is $518 billion. Contingent on top of that are $32 billion of neocloud agreements, which "take effect only if the neoclouds secure financing", and $118.0 billion of guarantees.

The gearing is simple arithmetic. At the reaffirmed 72–73% gross margin, the company's guided fiscal 2028 (about $741 billion of revenue at +75%) needs roughly $200 billion of cost of goods. The fiscal 2028 commitments are $203 billion. So the purchases are sized to the guide. If revenue arrives, the stack is ordinary working capital. If it does not, the stack becomes inventory, then write-downs.

Exhibit 2 — Supply commitments due each year against the cost of goods each case needs

How to read it. For each period, the black bar is supply and capacity commitments due, in billions of dollars, from the CFO commentary table at 25 October. The three bars beside it are the cost of goods each case's revenue requires at its gross margin: light grey for the bear case, grey for the base, gold for the bull. Where the black bar stands above a case's bar, that case buys more than it sells and carries the excess as inventory. The base case roughly uses what is bought; the bear case does not, and its excess peaks in fiscal 2028.

We model that directly. In each case, purchases contracted through fiscal 2029 are set against that case's cost of goods. Any front-loaded excess beyond a normal inventory buffer is written down at the case's loss rate. The base case's excess is about $3 billion and costs almost nothing. The bear case has fiscal 2028 revenue of $483 billion and fiscal 2029 of $362 billion. Its peak excess is $75 billion, written down at 25%: a $15.6 billion after-tax charge. That is the commitment write-down mechanism our July note named, sized.

The guarantees are smaller than their headline. The SB Energy credit support ($105.0 billion) begins building by building when each building is ready, the first in fiscal 2029, and then runs down over 20-year leases to OpenAI. On the call the company described the newly added neocloud wording as how the guarantees always worked: "Is it new? No. Did we change anything? No." (one transcription). The new $9.5 billion line, "power and systems credit support", is not explained in the release; its counterparty is a 10-Q item. The company also still holds, rather than has used, its option on about 3.8 more gigawatts at the site. We charge the stack as expected losses on its exposure path: annual default probabilities of 0.5%, 1.5% and 4% in the bull, base and bear cases, with recoveries on the land, power and shell. The cost is $2.5 billion in the base case and $9.3 billion in the bear. No guarantee payment has been made to date.

The stake book. NVIDIA now holds about $118 billion of stakes. Public stakes total $52.4 billion: $44.2 billion of marketable equity, $4.7 billion of long-term public holdings locked up through December 2027, and a $3.5 billion convertible note that the balance sheet counts among "marketable debt securities". Private stakes are $61.65 billion on the rollforward table, or $65.5 billion on the balance-sheet line; the $3.9 billion difference is not explained until the 10-Q. The book has tripled since January. The 13F for 30 September shows a SpaceX stake (130.5 million shares, $19.7 billion) and an Intel stake of 161.1 million shares, down 25% in the quarter.

The book could be read as operating capital, as vendor financing that buys demand, or as a separate asset. We treat it as a non-operating asset carried below its mark, the same way in every calculation. It is excluded from the capital in ROIC and from the WACC weights. In the equity bridge, public stakes are counted at their mark less 15%, for lock-ups on $25.7 billion of them, the tax on embedded gains and concentration in two names. The private book is counted at half its carrying value. Its marks come from funding rounds NVIDIA often joins, and part of what NVIDIA paid bought demand that is already in our revenue forecast; counting both in full would count it twice. The $20 billion of future equity commitments cost half their face for the same reason. The $1.8 billion impairment is the first observed instance of the write-down mechanism, at about 3% of the book. Valued at carrying value, as in August, the private book adds +$1.27 to fair value; valued at zero, −$1.27. Either way the stake book moves fair value by about a dollar a share. It matters more as a sign of how the demand is being financed than as a number.

Hugging Face ($11.9 billion plus up to $1.0 billion of retention awards, expected to close in the first half of 2027 subject to regulatory approval) is about a fifth of cash and debt securities and 0.3% of market value. We treat it as zero net present value: the price paid equals the value received. The 8-K commits NVIDIA to "keep Hugging Face's platform open" and to "support other silicon vendors".

The key person

The chief executive and China: what we can observe, and what we price

What the filings say. On 11 November the board accepted Jensen Huang's resignation as president, chief executive and director, effective immediately (8-K, filed 12 November). Colette Kress, chief financial officer since 2013, is interim chief executive and remains CFO. Harvey C. Jones, a director since 1993, is the board's first chair. The search covers internal and external candidates. The filing gives no reason. On the call, the chair said the resignation followed "disclosure of personal behavior inconsistent with the company's code of conduct, standards, and cultural norms" and that the board "does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO" (both transcriptions). He said the board had retained Heidrick & Struggles for the search and had formed a National Security and Export Control Oversight Committee, and that it would not take questions on the circumstances or "the ongoing investigation". The earnings release names "the ongoing investigation by an independent committee" of the board; it does not state the scope. We do not characterize the personal matters, and nothing about them enters the model.

What management added to its risk factors. The release's cautionary statement changed this quarter. Against August's, it adds two risk factors: the company's "ability to successfully transition NVIDIA's leadership", and "the scope, timing and outcome of investigations, litigation and government and regulatory actions and proceedings". Among the matters it calls forward-looking it adds the leadership transition and chief-executive search, "litigation, government and regulatory inquiries", the investigation, and the quarter's own 10-Q. In each of the previous nine quarters, NVIDIA filed its 10-Q or 10-K the same day as the release. This time the CFO commentary says only "in the coming weeks". The deadline is 4 December, and a tripwire watches it (§7).

What matters for value. Four things:

  • Key-person and execution risk. A chief financial officer is running the company through the Rubin ramp. The observable test is the roadmap the company committed to on this call: Rubin Ultra next year, Feynman in 2028.
  • Whether the investigation reaches export-control, sanctions or disclosure exposure. Revenue from customers headquartered in China, including Hong Kong, was $11.05 billion in the quarter (9.5% of revenue), against $2.97 billion a year earlier. Separately, the company shipped $2.4 billion of licensed Hopper data-center products to China in the quarter; it has stopped shipping them, and its guide excludes them. The release does not say how much of the headquarters figure those shipments make up. If all of it, the rest is about $8.65 billion. Headquarters is not destination, and the supplemental table does not say where the products went; the 10-Q geography note is where to read it. The company also said "new export controls were released by the U.S. Commerce Department this week". We have not found the rule text and do not describe it.
  • Separation terms. No 8-K yet. They may come in an 8-K or the proxy.
  • Ownership and trading. We count and do not characterize. No Form 4 has been filed by any NVIDIA insider since 11 November. Mr. Huang's only Form 4 since our August note (filed 18 September) reports shares withheld for tax and a gift, with 70.1 million shares held directly afterward.

How much of the fall is NVIDIA's own. From the 4 November close to the anchor, NVIDIA fell 22.3%. The sector ETF SMH fell 8.8%, while AMD rose 3% and Broadcom (AVGO) 5%. Split the window and the picture is clear. The 5 November rout was a sector event: NVIDIA fell 9.3%, SMH 11.2%. From the 6 November close, before the first reports, to the anchor, NVIDIA fell 16.7% while SMH was −0.8%, AMD +12% and AVGO +16%. Against SMH scaled by NVIDIA's own sensitivity to it, the company-specific move is about 16%, or about $30 a share. On the 8-K day itself the stock opened at $151.01, traded down to $148.80 and closed at $166.19, off 4.3% on 748 million shares.

That residual is the closest thing to a market price for the key-person event, but it cannot separate the chief executive's departure from the China question attached to it, or from money moving into AMD and Broadcom, both of which rose while NVIDIA fell; and because SMH holds NVIDIA itself, it slightly understates the company-specific move. Our own charge is the ten points of weight the bull case loses to the base (§4), worth $7.29 a share: about a quarter of the residual, with no base rate to anchor it. We put the charge there and only there. The base case takes the company's +75% without a cut: the ten points the bull loses are our charge for the change at the top, and cutting the base's growth as well would count the same risk twice. The discount rate stays out, because key-person risk is specific to the company and can be diversified away; putting it in both the rate and the weights would count it twice.

The value

What it is worth

$170.43fair value a share against $155.46; from $162 in August

Method. We value the firm's free cash flow in three cases, weighted 30/45/25 (bear/base/bull), discounted from 17 November at mid-year, the terminal value on the same timing. Every piece of the July and August model was open to change; we kept what we could defend and replaced what we could not. We use free cash flow to the firm rather than to equity because debt is under 1% of capital, so the two converge and the stakes and debt stay out of the cash flows; a dividend model would value a payout policy rather than the cash the business can pay, and a sum of the parts would split one platform with one cost base. In order of effect:

  • Revenue by stage, not by hand. July set five revenue levels per case. We build revenue from what drives each stage. The table below states each stage's length and evidence. Fiscal 2028 starts from the company's guide and the supply it has bought. Fiscal 2029 is the last year the commitments cover. A four-year fade then brings growth down to the economy's rate, one GPU life after the 2026–27 installation wave. The base takes fiscal 2028 at the company's +75%. The supply it has bought for fiscal 2028 is about the cost of goods of that guide at the reaffirmed gross margin. That cannot tell +70% from +75%: the two need about $198 billion and $204 billion of cost of goods. We take the guide because no evidence supports cutting it, not because the purchases prove it; +70% is worth $2.37 a share less. The base also needs fiscal 2028 to re-accelerate: first-quarter revenue near $142 billion, the first of four quarters each growing about 18% from the 13-week fourth quarter, after +4%. The 13-week fourth quarter annualized, about $483 billion, is the bear case's fiscal 2028. The bull is +80%, the bear +15%.
  • Cash flow line by line. July and August used a flat free-cash-flow margin. We model operating margin, tax, capital spending and working capital (16.5% of revenue in the base, the current ratio). The terminal value reinvests what its growth requires at the return on new capital it earns. That return does not fall at a step: over the fade years (fiscal 2030–33) each year closes a larger share of the gap between today's capital needs and what the terminal requires, so in the base the return on new capital falls from about 79% in fiscal 2030 to 30% in fiscal 2033, the year the cash flows join the terminal value. Each case fades to its own terminal return: bull 40%, base 30%, bear 15%. Competition that takes margin also makes each new dollar of revenue cost more capital to win: customer financing, supply prepayments, stakes.
  • Margins. Operating margin is 66% in fiscal 2028 in the base case (72–73% gross margin less operating costs of about 6.5% of revenue). It fades to a terminal 50%: between NVIDIA's pre-AI 26–37% (fiscal 2020–22) and today's 67%, for a franchise that keeps its software and systems lead but faces custom chips and AMD at scale. The bull terminal is 55%, the bear 40%.
  • Terminal growth. 4.0%: the Federal Reserve's longer-run projections of 16 September 2026, 2.0% real growth plus 2.0% inflation. The CBO cross-check is about 3.8%.
  • Stock-based pay is expensed. GAAP operating income carries it, and since the first quarter of fiscal 2027 so do the company's non-GAAP figures. We do not dilute the share count for it as well. The share count is the third-quarter diluted average, 24,186 million.

How to read it. One row per stage of growth, in order. Each row gives the stage's length in years, what drives growth in it, and the dated observable that sets its length. Growth by case for each year is in the model's Stages sheet. The test of a stage table is whether each length follows from its driver; the price's own answer is in §5.

Cost of equity. 13.2% in fiscal 2028–29, converging in a straight line to 11.6% by fiscal 2033: the 10-year Treasury (5.12% on 16 November) plus a beta times a 4.5% equity risk premium. The measured betas disagree: own 60-month raw 2.23, shrunk toward peers 2.08, Blume-adjusted 1.83, 36-month 1.88, and a peer median of 1.61 across AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Qualcomm and TSMC. The 60-month window opens in November 2021, when gaming was NVIDIA's largest segment and the stock was about to lose more than 60% in the 2022 bust; that measures a different business. The 36-month window measures the AI-platform business alone; shrunk toward the peer median for its larger error, it gives about 1.8, which the Blume figure confirms. Hence 13.2%.

For the terminal we use 1.44, the average of TSMC (1.41) and Broadcom (1.46), the two peers that look most like what NVIDIA becomes in maturity: a dominant franchise with pricing power and a moat in software or process. That is the business the bull and base describe. The peer median, 1.61, is Qualcomm's own beta; it sits higher because Qualcomm carries handset cycles, and Micron, a commodity memory maker, and AMD, a challenger taking share, sit higher still. The bear describes that other kind of business: customers lose access to capital, revenue falls a quarter in fiscal 2029, and the terminal keeps 40% of revenue as operating profit and earns 15% on new capital. So the bear converges to the peer median instead, 12.4%. No case goes lower: no peer below the TSMC/Broadcom pair fits a mature franchise, and the 1.2 of the stable-growth convention is ruled out because mature chip franchises stay cyclical.

This choice carries the verdict, and no filing can test it: at the peer median in every case the value is $159.88, just above the price; at a flat 13.2% in every year it is $149.72. The stock fell on rates, on a sector sell-off, and on something specific to NVIDIA that took about $30 a share off it beyond the sector in the weeks its founder left and its board opened an investigation. In our base case, $155.46 pays for one more year of supernormal growth, the year the company has guided and bought supply for, and nothing after it. We think the evidence supports more: dated product cadence, buyers guiding up, supply bought through fiscal 2029. But the margin is thin. It rests on a discount rate that falls as the business matures, and on demand that does not break while NVIDIA is helping to finance it. July said the risk sat outside the company. This quarter part of it moved inside.
