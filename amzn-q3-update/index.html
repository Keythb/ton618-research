Amazon (AMZN) — Forty Percent, Paid in Advance

AWS grew 39.3% in the third quarter, its sixth straight acceleration, and management projects more than 40% in each of 2027 and 2028 on capacity that customers reserve in advance. The same quarter spent $75.9 billion on capital, trailing free cash flow fell to −$42.4 billion, and 2027 spending will be "meaningfully more" than 2026's $220 billion. We value Amazon at $175.67, from $142 in July, against $243.79 at the close before the release. Holding our other inputs, the price takes $1.77 of revenue per dollar of new plant, against the $1.06 to $1.23 the prior year's plant has earned, a cost of equity about 1.5 points below ours, or revenue growth about 2.0 times our base case; our bull case clears it at 1.2, on faster growth and wider margins.

Our July note described Amazon as two machines sharing one invoice: a store that keeps about 6 cents of each sales dollar and a utility, AWS, that keeps about 37. In the June filings, AWS's annualized revenue and Amazon's trailing twelve-month capital spending were both $169 billion. Three months later the run rate is $184 billion and the spending is $210.7 billion. Customers are reserving the utility's capacity before it is built, and every added point of growth is paid for in advance with capital. This note asks what the faster utility is worth once the invoice is counted.

Terms used throughout, defined once. AWS: Amazon Web Services, the cloud segment; retail means the North America and International segments together, advertising included. Capex: purchases of property and equipment net of proceeds from sales and incentives, the "cash capex" Amazon guides. Free cash flow: operating cash flow less capex, Amazon's own definition. Ex-gain: a figure with the cash-flow statement's "non-operating expense (income)" line removed at the 21% statutory tax rate; most of that line is marks on the Anthropic investment. Backlog: revenue under contracts of more than a year not yet recognized, primarily AWS (accountants call it remaining performance obligations). Free cash flow to equity (FCFE): net income plus depreciation of property and equipment, less capex, plus net new borrowing; stock compensation stays an expense. SOTP: sum of the parts, here AWS and retail valued separately on 2027 after-tax operating profit at peer multiples. Re-mark: our July estimate of earnings with servers depreciated over three years. Sales-to-capital: added revenue per dollar of added net property and equipment, the measure of how much plant growth requires. ROIC: after-tax operating income over invested capital (equity plus debt less cash), against the weighted average cost of capital (WACC). Terminal growth: the rate the business grows at forever after the forecast ends, set here from long-run GDP projections.

The case for the price

The case for the price, in the company's terms

By the company's own measures the quarter set a record margin. Operating income was $28.1 billion and the operating margin 13.9%, which the release calls Amazon's highest ever. AWS grew 39.3% on a $184 billion run rate, its sixth straight quarter of acceleration and its fastest growth in nearly five years. Chief executive Andy Jassy said the backlog is $578 billion, "growing triple digits year-over-year and well diversified." Large blocks of capacity, "hundreds of megawatts or sometimes up to 1 gigawatt," are "reserved well in advance," so Amazon has "very good visibility into '27 and '28 and some of '29 too." On that basis he projected growth "north of 40% in each of 2027 and in 2028" and an AWS of $1 trillion of revenue "in the next 5 to 10 years." AWS's margin was 38.7% while it built at record pace, which management attributes to custom chips, custom networking and efficiency work; Jassy said revenue commitments for Graviton and Trainium each rose more than 20 times, and Amazon will start selling Trainium outside AWS in late 2027.

The store improved too. North America's margin was 7.7% and its trailing margin 8.2%, against 6.6% a year earlier; units grew 12% in a quarter that lost Prime Day to the second quarter. Excluding non-operating gains, earnings per share rose 60% to $1.94. Return on invested capital is 16.0%, above the 10.85% cost of capital. On this view our cash-flow model charges the build to shareholders before the build earns, and the price is the market paying now for an AWS that is twice today's size by the end of the decade. Management's answer to the capital question is that efficiency returns as the build bills: Jassy said "We continue to accelerate our CapEx payback," and that the breakeven on infrastructure keeps moving earlier: "we're continuing to move that left." He put the larger argument directly: "would it be more convenient if this type of acceleration and opportunity happened over 25 years ... yes ... But that's not the world that we live in," and "we can fund this plan." The stake in Anthropic adds a second asset. On Amazon's books it is $239 billion before tax, and Anthropic's registration for a public listing puts a market price on it within months.

This note tests five of these claims: earnings once the marks are set aside, the basis of the 40% projection, the capital it requires and whether its efficiency returns, the value of the stake, and what the price requires. It cannot test, inside a seven-year model, whether AWS reaches $1 trillion of revenue: our base case reaches about $717 billion by 2033 and the bull about $847 billion, and reaching $1 trillion by 2033 would take AWS growth of about 28% a year from 2026. Nor can it value an external Trainium business that has no revenue until late 2027, or the Leo satellite and Zoox robotaxi businesses, whose costs sit in retail's margins in our model while their value is carried at zero; leaving them out cuts against the company. We carry the AWS path as a tripwire, not a verdict.

The print

What the quarter earned, and what the miss was made of

Net sales were $201.9 billion, up 12% (13% excluding currency); the release says the move of Prime Day into the second quarter cost about 400 basis points of growth. North America rose 6% to $112.9 billion, International 5% to $43.1 billion (8% excluding currency) and AWS 39% to $46.0 billion. Advertising grew 11% to $19.6 billion (12% excluding currency), held back, Jassy said, by an 850-basis-point Prime Day effect; the release expects high-teens growth in the fourth quarter. Operating income rose 61% to $28.08 billion, but that rate flatters the quarter: a year earlier "other operating expense" was $2.9 billion, against $0.1 billion now. Without that line operating income rose about 39%. North America earned $8.7 billion against $4.8 billion, International $1.6 billion against $1.2 billion, AWS $17.8 billion against $11.4 billion.

The miss, in two parts. Diluted EPS was $1.89, against $1.95 a year earlier and a consensus of $1.97 in our brokerage feed. The year-on-year fall is the investment line. A year ago the release booked $9.5 billion of pre-tax gains on Anthropic in non-operating income; this quarter "other income (expense)" was a loss of $703 million. Removing the non-operating line at 21%, EPS was $1.94 against $1.22, up 60%. Against the consensus the marks explain about 5 cents of the 8-cent gap, if analysts modeled none; the compilation does not say. Exhibit 1 shows why the reported line misleads in both directions: ex-gain EPS has run between $1.65 and $1.94 for four quarters, while reported EPS ran between $1.89 and $5.75.

The utility

The utility: what the 40% rests on

AWS revenue was $45.98 billion, up 39.3%, and $3.8 billion more than in the second quarter (Exhibit 2). Its operating margin was 38.7%, against 39.4% in the second quarter and 34.6% a year earlier. Management's projection of growth above 40% in 2027 and 2028 is not guidance, and chief financial officer Brian Olsavsky said Amazon is "not guiding at all on our margin for AWS." The call gave three supports for it. The backlog: $578 billion at 30 September, against $496 billion at 30 June, with a weighted-average remaining life of 6.1 years, from 6.4, in the 10-Q. The reservations: the largest blocks of capacity are reserved well before delivery, so management can see "'27 and '28 and some of '29." The plan: capacity planned "over the next, call it, 8 quarters or so, which is about as far out as we get 8 to 10 quarters."

Exhibit 2 — AWS revenue, growth and operating margin, by quarter

How to read it. Two panels on one time axis, Q2 2025 to Q3 2026. The line above is the segment's operating margin in percent; the bars below are AWS revenue in billions of dollars, with the growth on a year earlier printed inside each bar. Read the bars for size and the line for profitability. The one thing it shows: growth has accelerated for six quarters while the margin rose from 33% to the high 30s.

The backlog is large, but it does not set the growth rate. Spread over its 6.1-year life, $578 billion is about $95 billion of revenue a year, about half of AWS's current run rate. Capacity carries the projection. Revenue in a capacity-constrained cloud is capacity delivered times use, and Jassy said customers take capacity "almost as fast as we install" it. So growth in 2027 and 2028 is mainly a statement about how much capacity Amazon will deliver, and that is the bill in §3.

What we carry. Our base case takes AWS growth at 40% in 2027, inside the eight-to-ten-quarter horizon the plan covers, and 36% in 2028, at its edge, where some of the year rests on reservations not yet made. The bull carries 44% and 42%; the bear 30% and 22%, a case in which capacity arrives late or inference prices fall faster than use grows. July carried 30% in 2027 and then the consolidated growth rate; on July's method the faster path is worth +$18.11 a share. The extra revenue earns a 35% operating margin in the base case, below the quarter's 38.7%, because Olsavsky said the "outsized growth of AI puts margin pressure on the business as a whole" and named component prices as a further headwind; AWS also carries its own depreciation, 20% of its revenue in the first nine months. Retail grows 9% in 2027 and 8% in 2028 in the base case, below its trailing 11.7%, at a 7% operating margin (bull 11% and 10%, bear 6% and 5%). Taking management's projection literally, 40% in both years, moves fair value only to $183.16: in our model the growth arrives with the capital that builds it.

The invoice

The invoice

Capex was $75.9 billion in the third quarter, 37.6% of revenue, against $34.2 billion a year earlier. Operating cash flow was $42.4 billion, so the quarter's free cash flow was −$33.5 billion. Over twelve months, operating cash flow rose 29% to $168.3 billion and capex rose 82% to $210.7 billion, so trailing free cash flow fell from −$7.6 billion in June to −$42.4 billion (Exhibit 3). The release attributes the increase "primarily" to "investments in artificial intelligence." Trailing capex per dollar of ex-gain net income rose from 2.34 times in July to 2.62 times: Amazon now spends $2.62 on capital for every dollar it earns.

Exhibit 3 — The invoice: trailing operating cash flow against trailing capex

How to read it. Billions of dollars over trailing twelve months, at each quarter end from June 2025 to September 2026. Gold is operating cash flow; black is capex net of proceeds; the figure beneath each pair is free cash flow, the gap between them. The one thing it shows: operating cash flow kept growing, but capex grew faster and crossed it in the second quarter of 2026; the gap widened by $34.8 billion in one quarter.

The 2026 guide leaves an odd fourth quarter. The release repeats "approximately $220 billion" of 2026 cash capex. Nine months have spent $172.2 billion, which leaves $47.8 billion for the fourth quarter, about 37% less than the third. Either spending falls sharply for one quarter or 2026 ends above $220 billion; the call did not say which. Jassy said Amazon expects "to spend meaningfully more in 2027." He gave no figure. We carry $280 billion in the base case, about 27% above 2026 and below the $304 billion annual pace of the third quarter; the bear carries $310 billion, the bull $265 billion, because in the bull more revenue arrives for the same build. On July's method the 2027 figure costs $8.29 a share.

The funding. Long-term debt is $173.1 billion, from $128.9 billion at June and $65.6 billion at the start of the year, after $48.1 billion of new notes in the quarter. Interest expense was $1.69 billion, from $0.54 billion. Net of cash and marketable securities, Amazon now owes $58.9 billion. Property and equipment, net, rose $62.8 billion in a quarter to $508.9 billion. Depreciation of that base is the invoice's second payment: depreciation of property and equipment was $15.1 billion in the quarter, 39% above a year earlier, and AWS's allocated share $9.2 billion. The 10-Q discloses no change in useful lives.

The stake

The stake

Amazon holds two kinds of Anthropic securities: nonvoting preferred stock, carried at the last observable price adjusted for its rights and a discount for lack of marketability, and convertible notes, carried at fair value with the gains in other comprehensive income. At 30 June the preferred was carried at $92.5 billion and the notes at $97.9 billion. In the third quarter Amazon's unrealized gain on its available-for-sale debt securities rose by $48.2 billion before tax; the 10-Q puts the notes at $146.2 billion and the preferred at $92.5 billion at 30 September. Amazon's cost for all of it is about $18 billion. Beside it sit $50 billion of OpenAI preferred stock and smaller warrants and stakes.

Our July model valued the investment book once, as an asset, at Amazon's carrying value net of the tax the gains would bear. Anthropic filed to list its shares on 26 October, and on 27 October we published our own valuation: $50.72 a share, 14% below the Series H price of $58.90. The 10-Q gives the count for the first time: 164 million preferred shares, and about 184 million more from the notes, each convertible into ten nonvoting common shares, so 3,480 million common-equivalent shares, subject to Amazon's 12% ownership cap on the notes; the S-1's 1,840 million Class N shares for the notes match. That is roughly 21% of Anthropic's 16,427 million economic shares. On that count Amazon's books carry Anthropic at about $68.59 a share (the notes at $79.46, the preferred at $56.40), 35% above our fair value and 16% above the last private round (Exhibit 4).

Exhibit 4 — The investment book under three marks for Anthropic

How to read it. Billions of dollars, net of a 21% tax on the gain over Amazon's cost, with the value per Amazon share at the end of each bar. Grey is the rest of the book (OpenAI, warrants and other stakes) at Amazon's carrying value, the same in every row; the colored part is the Anthropic stake at Amazon's own mark, at the Series H price of $58.90, and at the house's fair value of $50.72. The one thing it shows: the choice of mark moves Amazon's value by a few dollars a share, not tens.

Which mark the fair value uses, and why. We use our published fair value. Amazon's mark would import a price 35% above that view into this one, and the Series H price is a private round that predates the registration, the S-1's financial statements and our model of them. This changes July's method, at −$4.50 a share. At Amazon's carrying value fair value would be $180.16; at the Series H price, $177.73; with the stake at zero, $162.65. The listing does not change the rule. Amazon expects a customary lock-up, and the 12% cap could leave part of the notes unconverted, so a first-day price is a data point for the next update and not a mark (tripwire 6).

The value

What Amazon is worth after the print, and why the number moved

The model. Free cash flow to equity in three scenarios weighted 25/50/25, stock compensation expensed, a depreciation add-back limited to property and equipment, borrowing modeled and its interest charged, and the investment book added once. Every input and method below was set as the most defensible choice on today's evidence. To show what moved, we first re-ran July's configuration, which reproduces its fair value, $141.84 (published as $142): free cash flow to equity $117.70, the re-marked sum of the parts $178.05, blended 60/40. Each factor in Exhibit 5, and in the attribution table later in this section, re-runs that blend with one more change, so the factors sum to the move.

Exhibit 5 — From July's $142 to $175.67, one factor at a time

How to read it. Fair value in dollars a share. The first black bar is July's value; each bar after it is one factor applied to the same blend, gold for a rise and grey for a fall, labeled with its effect in dollars; the gold bar at the end is today's value. The dashed lines separate the quarter's data, the cost of equity, the six cash-flow changes, the stake and the weights. The one thing it shows: the quarter's data alone raised value by about $23; the segment fades and the capital tied to them nearly offset; and the weight we stopped giving the sum of the parts takes back about a third of the data's gain.

The quarter's data, on July's method: $165.10. Rolling the valuation date to 29 October, with the borrowing already done, adds +$0.55. The investment book at its 30 September carrying value adds +$6.16. Booking nine months of results with the fourth-quarter guide midpoint adds +$0.37: revenue of $827.6 billion and operating income of $110.4 billion for 2026. AWS and retail as their own lines add +$18.11 (§2). The 2027 capex figure takes −$8.29 (§3). Updating the sum of the parts for this quarter, and scaling the depreciation re-mark to the capex now planned, adds +$6.37.

The growth gets its own runway: +$25.23. July faded one consolidated rate and ended in 2032 at 7.0% growth against a 3.5% terminal; a single rate fading in a straight line to terminal growth would have cut AWS from 36% in 2028 to about 13% in 2029. AWS now falls in a straight line from its 2028 rate to terminal growth by 2033, one more forecast year, and retail from its own 2028 rate; margins blend by the resulting mix, so AWS reaches 42% of revenue by 2033.

And then pays for its plant: −$22.22. From 2028, capex replaces what depreciates and adds plant at one dollar of revenue per dollar of new net property and equipment in the base and bear cases, 1.2 in the bull. Amazon's own record brackets that level (Exhibit 6). The 2025–26 readings (0.76 and $0.58) understate it because plant is built before it bills: the revenue added in 2026 (the fourth quarter at the guide midpoint) is $1.06 per dollar of plant added in 2025, and on a trailing basis the plant added in the year to September 2025 earned $1.23 on the following year's revenue. That lagged reading is about to fall: 2026 adds 1.8 times 2025's plant, and the base case's 2027 revenue gain is $0.70 per dollar of it. The 2017–24 readings (1.3 to 4.4) overstate it for what comes next, because they came from retail-led growth, and AWS carries about $1.94 of net plant per dollar of trailing revenue, including plant not yet billing, against retail's $0.29 (10-Q segment property and equipment). The base takes 1.0, just under the lagged $1.06 to $1.23 and above the $0.70 the 2026 build earns in 2027. The bull takes 1.2 for Trainium's cost advantage and a faster payback; the bear keeps 1.0 because it is a demand case, not a capital-efficiency case. Depreciation runs on the rolled-forward plant at a rate that rises from 2026's 13.0% to 16%, close to the 17% Amazon ran in 2020–21, and the terminal takes the plant per dollar of revenue the path actually lands ($0.87 in the base case) instead of July's outside $0.55. That leaves a terminal return on capital of 15.6% in the base, 22.5% in the bull and 9.6% in the bear, about the 9.9% stable cost of equity: a bear in which growth no longer creates value.

Exhibit 6 — Revenue per dollar of new plant: what Amazon has earned, and what each lens assumes

How to read it. Bars are Amazon's added revenue in each year divided by the increase in its net property and equipment that year (sales-to-capital), 2017 to 2025, and the twelve months to September 2026; higher means less plant per dollar of growth. The dashed lines are what each lens assumes from 2028: our base case, the sum of the parts' implied level and one route to the $243.79 price. The one thing it shows: every year from 2017 to 2024 sits above the base case's line, the AI-era readings below it; holding our other inputs, this route to the price takes a capital efficiency Amazon last reached in 2023, while the sum of the parts needs only a modest gain on the base case.

Four smaller changes to how the model ends. Terminal growth is set from long-run nominal GDP: the Federal Reserve's projections of 16 September 2026 give 2.0% real growth and 2.0% inflation in the longer run, so 4.0% in the base and bull cases and 3.0% in the bear, against July's 3.5%, 3.5% and 3.0%: +$2.87. The terminal adds the debt that grows with the business, rather than holding it flat forever: +$4.53. It is taxed at 22%, the trailing effective rate, while the forecast years keep 19%, the cash rate while full expensing of new equipment shelters income: −$3.94. The 2026 stub counts only fourth-quarter flows, not a share of a year whose borrowing already sits in cash: −$0.69. With the runway and the plant, these six changes add +$5.77 net.

The cost of equity: 11.3% for the forecast years (−$5.77), 9.9% for the terminal (+$22.87). We argue the rate for the company and show the standard reads beside it. The starting point is the 10-year Treasury, 5.05% on 28 October against 4.67% on 29 July, plus a beta times a 4.5% equity risk premium. For 2027–28 we use a beta of 1.38: Amazon's own 60-month beta against the S&P 500, 1.43, blended slightly toward the 1.09 median of Microsoft, Alphabet and Walmart in proportion to its noise. The estimate is precise (standard error 0.20) and stable (the 36-month beta is 1.46), so the data give no reason to pull it toward 1.0, as the Blume adjustment would (10.9%, worth $178.09), and a build financed partly with new debt argues against a lower read. Relevering the beta for that debt (the Hamada adjustment) would add about 0.2 points by 2033, inside one standard error of the beta, about 0.9 points either way, so we show it ($174.55) rather than add it. The rate goes from July's 10.84% to 11.3%. The terminal value describes a mature company, so we discount it at 9.9%, reached in a straight line over 2029–33 as growth fades. AWS is about 65% of segment value in the sum of the parts on 2027 profit. Weighting the average beta of Microsoft and Alphabet, 1.14, by that share, with retail at the market's 1.0, gives 1.09, within 0.1 point of the 9.9% we use. That rate sits between two bounds: Walmart's beta for all of retail gives 9.3% ($193.70) and Alphabet's beta for the whole company, a cloud-only terminal, gives 10.4% ($163.20). One rate throughout, 11.3% with no stable rate, would give $145.03.

What changed in the weights, and why (−$7.82). July weighted free cash flow to equity at 60% and the re-marked sum of the parts at 40%. We now carry the sum of the parts as an unweighted cross-check, a separate judgment from the changes above, for two reasons. First, conversion: it prices 2027 segment profit at peer multiples, AWS at 22 to 30 times and retail at 14 to 20, and that profit is not reaching shareholders as cash. Trailing free cash flow is −53% of ex-gain net income, against −11% when July set the weights; we stopped weighting Meta's earnings multiple in our Meta update published on 26 October, when free cash flow was 21% of nine-month net income. Second, the sum of the parts was where July valued AWS separately, and AWS is now its own line in the cash-flow model. The two lenses now differ by one lever: the re-marked sum of the parts, $195.21 a share, is our cash-flow model at about $1.16 of added revenue per dollar of added plant from 2028, against 1.0 in our base case, the lagged $1.06 to $1.23 it sits just under, and $0.58 and 0.76 unlagged (Exhibit 6). On the lagged readings the gap is small, so the weight rests on conversion and on AWS now being its own line, not on that lever. It returns when the observable shows it (tripwire 5). At July's 60/40, fair value would be $183.48; at 20%, $179.58. The reported-schedule sum of the parts is $246.47; the $51 between it and the re-marked figure is the three-year depreciation re-mark.

Return on capital. Trailing return on invested capital is 16.0% against a 10.85% WACC, and the regime is fading: the spread fell 6.3 points in four quarters, and the return is down from a 27.9% peak in Dec ’24 (Exhibit 7). What would change our view

We concede the valuation case if the evidence tracks the whole bull path: AWS growth of 44% in 2027 and 42% in 2028, 2027 capex guided at or below $265 billion, AWS operating margin at or above 38% in each quarter of 2027, and trailing sales-to-capital, on the model's same-year definition, at or above 1.2 in two consecutive quarters of 2028, the first year the model applies it. That path is worth $282.02, above today's price, and conceding means the bull's inputs become the base case in the next update. The two observables alone, in the base case, lift value to about $193; sales-to-capital at 1.5 from 2028, near the lower end of Amazon's 2020–24 range (1.3 to 3.5), moves it to about $217 on that input alone. The case hardens the other way on the mirror: AWS growth of 30% and 22%, 2027 capex at or above $310 billion, AWS operating margin at or below 31% in each quarter of 2027, and that 2028 reading at or below 0.8 in two consecutive quarters, the bear's path, worth $73.75; on that path the bear's inputs become the base case in the next update. The two observables alone take value to about $143.

The call

The call

We value Amazon at $175.67 a share, from $142. The utility ran faster than July assumed, and that alone would have lifted value by about $23. The invoice ran further: trailing free cash flow is −$42.4 billion, the company is borrowing to build, and 2027 will cost more. A model in which the plant grows with the revenue it serves gives the faster AWS real value, but not the price. Holding our other inputs, the price takes $1.77 of revenue per dollar of new plant, a capital efficiency Amazon last reached in 2023 (its 2022–25 aggregate is 1.3), costs of equity about 1.5 points below ours, or revenue growth about 2.0 times our base case; our bull case clears the price at 1.2, on faster growth and wider margins. The fourth-quarter call, with the 2027 capex number, is the next test. The Fund holds no position in AMZN.
