CoreWeave (CRWV) — The Floor Moved to the Fleet

The stock fell a third from July while CoreWeave raised prices twice, lifted its operating margin from 1% to 10% and re-contracted older GPUs above their first prices. The signed book no longer covers the debt plus the build it still needs, so the value now rests on the fleet: new capacity earning near today's quoted rates, old GPUs re-contracting and asset-level lenders staying open. We value the stock at $48.57, from $60 in July, against $48.73 at the close of 10 November.

In July we called CoreWeave the neocloud thesis in a single security: revenue more contracted than any peer's, a floor under the equity worth a seventh of the price, and everything above it a levered bet on how long a GPU earns its keep. Four months on, the price has done what that framing feared, falling from $72.91 to $48.73, within a dollar of the $40 IPO price at its 5 November intraday low ($40.58; it closed that day at $43.66), with the company's unsecured bonds trading near 15% and its credit default swaps past 1,000 basis points in mid-October. The business did the opposite. Revenue rose 42% in the quarter, prices rose about 25% in July and 10% more later in the quarter, older GPUs re-priced above their original contracts, and both guides went up. This note asks which of those two moves is better argued, and finds that each is right about a different thing: the credit market about the signed book, the business about the fleet. At $48.73, the price holds both.

Terms used throughout, defined once. RPO (remaining performance obligations): contracted revenue not yet recognized; CoreWeave's revenue backlog is RPO plus a small amount of other committed revenue ($132.3 billion and $0.2 billion at 30 September). Take-or-pay: a contract under which the customer pays for reserved capacity whether or not it uses it. Prepayment: cash a customer pays before the service starts, booked as deferred revenue. DDTL (delayed-draw term loan): a loan drawn in pieces as GPUs are bought, secured on them and on the customer contracts they serve, held in a subsidiary; most are non-recourse or guaranteed but asset-level. Parent-level debt: CoreWeave, Inc.'s own unsecured notes and convertibles, serviced from the company's free cash flow. Convertible note: debt the holder can swap for shares at a set conversion price; a capped call is an option the company buys at issue that offsets dilution between the conversion price and a cap. ATM (at-the-market) program: shares sold into the market over time. ARR (annualized run-rate revenue): the last month of a quarter times twelve. Active power: the megawatts (MW) or gigawatts (GW; 1,000 MW) of data-center capacity in service; contracted power includes capacity leased but not yet built. Adjusted EBITDA: earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization, also before stock pay; adjusted operating income: after depreciation, before stock pay. CIP (construction in progress): assets bought but not yet in service, so not yet depreciated. CDS (credit default swap): insurance against a borrower's default, quoted in basis points (bp) a year. FCFE: free cash flow to equity, cash left for shareholders after debt service and investment.

The two cases

The cases on either side of the price, in their own terms

The credit market's case. The case against paying more than $48.73 is not that CoreWeave lacks demand. It is that the equity is the last claim on an asset base that loses value fast, funded by debt that just repriced to distress levels, at a moment that may be the top of a rental cycle.

  1. The credit market has already called it. In mid-October CoreWeave's 9.625% 2032 notes traded below 80 cents, about a 15% yield (16–16.5% at the intraday low), and its five-year CDS passed 1,000bp, roughly a 60% risk-neutral chance of default over five years. On 10 November the 9.75% 2031 notes still yielded about 15.5%. High-yield spreads widened only 9bp on the day the stock fell 15%, so the market priced CoreWeave's own risk, not the market's. The company itself now says it will not issue unsecured debt "at current yields."
  2. The signed book no longer covers the debt. Total debt is $46.9 billion ($10.6 billion due within a year) against $4.1 billion of unrestricted cash. Run the $132.3 billion book off at its contract margin, pay for the 2027 build the company says that book needs, and repay the net debt: the equity is worth −$29.30 a share. Everything above zero depends on revenue CoreWeave has not yet signed.
  3. The margin turn is pricing at a cyclical peak. Prices rose about 25% in July and another 10% later in the quarter; Ampere and Hopper GPUs re-priced 30–50% in a quarter. That is what a supply squeeze looks like. Hyperscaler, sovereign and neocloud capacity ordered in 2025–26 lands in 2027–28, the same years 300,000 of CoreWeave's GPUs come off contract and the 4.6 GW of contracted power has to be filled.
  4. The reported economics do not support the equity. On July's own formula with today's measured inputs (62% adjusted EBITDA margin, capex of 2.6 times a year's revenue, interest at 18% of revenue), the business breaks even only if a GPU earns its full rate for six years, the book life. Return on capital has been about zero for the four quarters the history allows.
  5. Dilution is the release valve. Officers, directors and a large holder (Magnetar, 43% of the shares) filed 96 notices of proposed sale and sold 10.1 million shares ($0.97 billion) between the July note and this print; a 35-million-share ATM program is in place; and equity sold at $48.73 costs nearly twice the dilution it would have at the $90.32 of 11 August.
  6. Free cash flow is not in sight. It was −$7.5 billion in Q3 and −$17.9 billion for nine months, capex is guided to $37–39 billion this year, the CFO said 2027 capex would stay in line with 2026 if the company pursued only its committed backlog, and no free-cash-flow date was given.
  7. The customers are few. At 30 June one customer was 36% of revenue and two were 32% each of receivables (Q2 10-Q); OpenAI is named among expanded relationships; and $35.5 billion of leases had been signed but not yet begun. A lost anchor would strand both the capacity and the rent.

The case for the stock. CoreWeave's case is that the scarce input in AI compute is not the GPU but the powered, cooled site. It has more than 4.6 GW of contracted power, on data-center leases with initial terms of five to fifteen years, some extendable for up to ten more, in a market where new grid connections take years. On that view the margin turn (1% to 10% in three quarters, prices up about 25% and then 10%, Ampere and Hopper GPUs re-contracted above their first prices) is that scarcity being priced, and it lasts. Its scale and software keep customers who have built on them; a backlog of $132.5 billion and an exit-2027 run-rate target above $30 billion (February outlook presentation, p. 4) say demand is not the question. Asset-level debt about to be priced at investment grade, 7–7.5%, funds the build without the parent's unsecured market, so October's repricing of the notes is a cost of capital on a shrinking slice, not a verdict on the business.

This note tests the credit market's claims 1, 2 and 4, claim 3 as far as what the price requires of pricing, and claim 5's arithmetic; it states claim 6 and leaves claim 7 for the Q3 10-Q's customer and lease notes. Of the case for the stock, it tests the pricing, the renewals and the funding (§4–§5). It cannot test, inside a ten-year model, whether GPU rental prices hold through the 2027–28 supply wave, whether the next investment-grade facility prices where the company says, how much of the renewal revenue the company describes is realized, or whether sites stay scarce past 2036. Our answers are the tripwires in §7 and, for the last, the terminal judgment argued in §5.

The print

The quarter: the margin turned, and four boring checks explain it

Revenue was $3,661 million, up 168% on the year and 42% on the quarter, an increase of $1,086 million. Adjusted EBITDA was $2,256 million (62%), adjusted operating income $379 million (10%) against a guide of $200–260 million, and GAAP operating income $207 million. Net interest expense was $846 million, so the net loss was $709 million, or $1.27 a share. The company booked an $83 million tax expense on a $626 million pre-tax loss, $74 million of it deferred; the −$0.95 a share some feeds show is the adjusted net loss ($529 million) over 559 million weighted shares, not GAAP. The backlog rose to $132.5 billion from $104.2 billion: the early-Q3 signings of more than $25 billion, less the quarter's $3.7 billion of revenue, account for all but about $7 billion of that.

Both guides rose. Full-year revenue is now $13.0–13.3 billion and adjusted operating income $1.275–1.375 billion, which implies fourth-quarter revenue of $4.69 billion–$4.99 billion (up 28–36% on the quarter) at an adjusted operating margin of 15.9%–17.0%. Capex is guided to $37–39 billion, the low end raised. Exit-2026 ARR is guided to $20–21 billion, from $18.5–19.5 billion.

July's thesis was that CoreWeave's margin is a function of how long its GPUs earn, and that depreciation was catching up with it: adjusted operating margin had fallen from 17% to 1%. That reversed: 1% in Q1, 5% in Q2, 10% in Q3 and about 16–17% guided for Q4. Before crediting it, the boring checks.

  • Price. Prices rose about 25% across SKUs in July and about 10% more later in the quarter, and "pricing has continued to move higher in Q4, more than offsetting cost increases" (CEO, Q3 call). This is most of the turn.
  • Mix. The company is allocating more capacity to short-term contracts, which it prices at more than $40 million per MW a year, against $20–25 million for new four-to-five-year contracts. Short contracts raise margin now and shorten the backlog's average life.
  • Assets entering service. Construction in progress fell $2.6 billion in Q3 as assets were placed in service, so depreciation steps up in Q4; the guide's margin is struck after that step, not before it. The margin turn is not an accounting lag.
  • Revenue per MW. Annualized Q3 revenue was about $8.6 million per MW of active power; exit-2026 ARR over the 1.85 GW guided for year-end is $10.8–11.4 million. The fleet is earning more per MW as newer, higher-priced capacity comes in.

The useful life was not changed: GPUs are still depreciated over six years, extended from five in 2023, and the Q3 depreciation share fell to 51.6% of revenue from 54–55% because revenue grew faster than the in-service base, not because of a policy change.

The debt

The debt: twice July's, priced in two markets

Debt was $46.9 billion at 30 September, up $11.8 billion in the quarter and from $24.9 billion at July's anchor; $10.6 billion of it is due within twelve months, against $4.1 billion of unrestricted cash and $2.3 billion restricted. Net debt is $42.8 billion, from $22.6 billion in July. The stack has two layers that the market now prices very differently.

  • Asset-level debt, about $26.5 billion. Delayed-draw term loans and equipment financing, each secured on GPUs and the customer contracts they serve, amortizing over the contract. Their spreads widened within the year, from SOFR+2.25% (DDTL 4.0, March) to +4.50% (May) and +5.50% (DDTL 5.5, August), and they were marked at 97.75–99.5 on 22 October (PitchBook marks, one source). The company says its next facility, "in the coming weeks," will be investment grade, price at a 7–7.5% yield and finance more than 90% of the capex it funds.
  • Parent-level debt, about $20.8 billion. $10.0 billion of senior unsecured notes (8.5–9.75% coupons, due 2030–32, the 8.5% issue in euros) and $10.8 billion of convertibles at 1.75–2.875% (due 2031–33). The convertibles convert at $97.85–$119.60, about twice the price, so we treat them as debt. The notes are where October happened.

October, verified. On 14 October the stock fell 15.5% to $65.78 on 111 million shares. Reuters, relaying Bloomberg, reported the 2032 notes below 80 cents, about a 15% yield, and on 15 October reported CDS around 1,000bp, roughly 63% implied odds of default over five years. A company memo dated 14 October, reported by CNBC and Reuters, said the market can "misunderstand and act irrationally." Nebius fell 10% and IREN 12% the same day; Oracle fell 8.8%. High-yield spreads widened 9bp and the ten-year Treasury yield fell, so the move was specific to CoreWeave and its peers. The wires gave no single cause; the strands they reported were the debt load, comments that the company was exploring investment-grade borrowing, and peer financing strain. Nothing was missed or defaulted: this was the unsecured market refusing to fund the parent at old prices, not a payment event.

Was the market shut? In late October Elon Musk wrote that debt markets had "almost entirely shut off" for companies without revenue independent of AI data centers. The prints say otherwise for secured credit: Lambda closed $1.0 billion of secured, investment-grade-rated GPU debt at 6.78% (1 October), Nebius a $3.5 billion secured facility at SOFR+275 (22 October), and smaller borrowers priced wider (WhiteFiber at SOFR+600, 9 November). Unsecured and high-yield take-outs did stall. The market split by security, not by sector, which is the split our July thematic note predicted.

What the stack costs. At 30 September the company's weighted average cost of debt was 7.9%. On the asset-level debt it is about 9.5% contractual; on the notes, their coupons; on the convertibles, under 3%. Q4 interest is guided to $1.05–1.15 billion. The cost that matters for value is the cost of refinancing: in our base case, new asset-level money at 8.0% (above the company's 7–7.5% guide for its next facility) and the parent notes and convertibles, as they mature in 2030–33, at 11.5%. When the convertibles roll, their sub-3% coupons become market coupons; that is about $1 billion a year of extra interest from 2031 that July's percent-of-revenue line could not see.

Leverage, on a matched basis. July's "about 7 times" was net debt over 2025 adjusted EBITDA (7.3 times). Over trailing EBITDA it is 7.3 times now; on the latest quarter annualized it is 4.7 times, against 4.9 times for the quarter July annualized. Leverage is unchanged on either basis: debt and EBITDA both roughly doubled.

The floor

The floor: the signed book no longer covers the debt; the fleet does

July valued the contracted book at about $10 a share, 14% of the price: $98.8 billion of RPO run off at a 56% margin and an 11% discount, less $9.6 billion of construction in progress and $22.6 billion of net debt. That subtraction charged only the assets already being built. Delivering a book that size needs more capacity than CIP represents, and the CFO has now sized it: if the company pursued only its committed backlog, 2027 capex would stay in line with 2026's, about $38 billion.

We rerun it on the 30 September book: RPO of $132.3 billion recognized 36% within 24 months, 38% in months 25–48 and 26% after (deck, read from the page image), at the Q4 implied adjusted EBITDA margin of 66%; less the prepayments already received ($10.0 billion of the RPO is cash in hand and spent), plus the ~$9 billion arriving in Q4; less the 2027 build; less net debt; discounted at the company's own 7.9% cost of debt, since the contracts repay the lenders first. The $35.5 billion of leases signed but not yet begun (Q2 10-Q) are not subtracted again: they are the sites the backlog will run in, and we assume their rent costs what the fleet's does, so it sits inside the 66% margin we apply to that revenue. If half of those payments were stranded instead, rent paid on sites the book does not fill, the floor would be −$46.28.

The crux

The crux, corrected: how long a GPU earns, and at what price

July's table made the steady-state net margin a function of economic GPU life: 21% at six years, 14% at 4.5, 1% at three. Its levels rested on one input we can now measure. July charged capex of 1.4 times a year's revenue, derived from a management claim of a 2.5-year EBITDA payback. The 2026 build cost about $36 million per MW of active power added (nine-month capex of $30.65 billion on the company definition, construction in progress roughly flat, more than 850 MW added), and the capacity added in 2026 earns about $13.8 million per MW a year (the rise in annualized revenue from the end of 2025 to the end of 2026 over the gigawatt added). That is 2.61 times revenue, close to twice July's figure. The in-service asset base gives the same answer: depreciation was 52–55% of revenue all year at a six-year life.

That is the real crux now. It is less the life of a GPU than the rate new capacity earns, because a high rate pays back the GPU inside its contract. The evidence on rate since July:

  • New contracts. Four-to-five-year contracts on new infrastructure are "moving to $20 million–$25 million per megawatt," and short-term capacity sells at more than $40 million (CEO, Q3 call, in all three transcriptions). These are quotes for contracts signed now, mostly for capacity that comes online in 2027–28.
  • Re-contracts. An A100 (2020 silicon) contract renewed for three years into 2029 at pricing "in line with typical 1-year terms," and an H200 three-year renewal at a "premium to original contract" (September investor presentation). Ampere and Hopper pricing rose 30–50% in Q3 "at prices above their original contracts," and more than $3 billion of Q4's prepayments is for re-contracted, fully paid-off GPUs sold "at meaningful premiums to their original price."
  • What is not yet shown. None of the renewal revenue is reported. The company sizes it at "approaching $4 billion" of annualized revenue from 130,000 GPUs coming off contract in 2027 and "nearly $10 billion" including 170,000 more in 2028; the company gave GPU counts, not power, so the share of the fleet is not stated. A price in a squeeze is not a price through a cycle.

The base case takes $18 million per MW on new contracts, below the quoted range, and $16 million on backlog capacity; GPU contracts of five years and one two-year renewal at 55% of contract pricing (an economic life of 6.1 years); legacy renewals at 60% of the company's figure; and pricing that holds through 2029, when the contracts signed in 2025–26 run out, then fades in a straight line to 2036, when a new GPU earns only the fleet's 10.5% after-tax cost of capital. The bear takes $15 million, a little above the 2026 build's $13.8 million, because part of the capacity coming online in 2027–28 is sold under contracts signed in early Q3, the quarter of the increase; renewals of two years at 40%; and legacy renewals at 35% of the company's figure. The distress case takes $14 million on backlog capacity and $12 million on new. Because a new GPU earns more than an old one renewed when pricing is strong, the model refreshes GPUs early in the base and bull, so a shorter book life is read through renewal pricing, not refresh timing (tripwire 7).

The value

What it is worth

Method. A levered equity whose secured debt is cheap and whose unsecured debt yields 15% is valued on its financing path, not on a multiple of a distant year. We build free cash flow to equity for 2027–2036 from the fleet: active power by vintage, each vintage earning its contract rate, then a renewal rate, then refreshed with new GPUs only if a new slot clears its cost of debt (the company calls this success-based capex), with stranded rent on power left idle. The debt runs off its ladder at its own coupons, and maturities and funding gaps are refinanced at the scenario's rates; customer prepayments enter as deferred revenue. Above a leverage cap, new shares are sold at the market price ($48.73 compounding at the cost of equity) in the base and bull, and at the case's own value in the bear and distress, a raise after the market has seen the bad news; distributions start only once net debt is at or below twice EBITDA. Stock pay is expensed, not also counted as dilution. July's two legs are retired: its P/E on fiscal 2028 earnings holds interest at a fixed share of revenue, so it cannot see the debt mature or the convertibles reprice, and its DCF at one 12% rate ignores how differently each layer of the funding is priced. The change of weighting is shown on its own line.

The terminal. The 2036 enterprise is valued at the fleet's unlevered return of 10.5%, and in every case that survives, the price of new capacity fades by then to where a new GPU earns about that return (9.9%–10.9% on capital across the cases), so growth adds or destroys little. That is a judgment about the business, applied to both tails alike: GPU capacity is rented, any operator with power and chips can add it, and no operator holds a cost or contract edge that outlasts a GPU cycle, so in the long run capacity is added until it earns its cost and withheld when it does not. The strongest objection is that the lasting asset is the powered site, not the chip. CoreWeave mostly leases its sites: operating lease liabilities were $18.7 billion at 30 September, up from $8.2 billion at the end of 2025, with more signed but not yet begun, and the 10-K gives data-center leases initial terms of five to fifteen years and a weighted remaining term of 11 years at the end of 2025, a figure that will move as the new leases begin. A scarcity rent on power goes to whoever controls the site at renewal: CoreWeave keeps some of it past 2036 where it holds options to extend at set rents, and the landlord takes it where leases reprice. We judge that most of it has repriced by the terminal. If CoreWeave kept it, the bull's 2.5-point edge forever would be worth $55.34 (1 point: $51.25); relative to keeping the edge, our rule takes $6.77 from the weighted value through the bull and adds $0.56 through the bear, whose equity is a call far out of the money. The cases differ in the decade to 2036 and in the debt they carry out of it, not in a permanent edge.

The equity in 2036. It is not simply enterprise less debt. With debt more than twice the equity in the base and above the enterprise in the bear, the equity is a call on the enterprise with the debt as its strike, and we value it that way. The volatility inside a case is 20% a year to 2036, the level of a mature contracted-compute or data-center lessor, less the spread the four cases already carry (a lognormal spread of 0.56 over the decade). What would change our view

We would move toward the bull case if the evidence tracks it: the next DDTL at or below 7.5% with an investment-grade rating; deferred revenue of $20 billion or more at the end of 2026; annualized renewal revenue from the 2027 roll-off reaching $4 billion; exit-2027 revenue per MW of active power above $15 million; 2027 capex at or below $40 million per MW; the depreciable life lengthened to seven years or more; and the unsecured notes below 11% by mid-2027. That path is worth $132.11. The mirror moves us toward the distress case: a DDTL priced above 9% or not at all, renewal revenue below $1.2 billion annualized, exit-2027 revenue per MW below $11.5 million, deferred revenue below $13 billion, capex above $50 million per MW, the life cut to five years or less, the notes above 15% at mid-2027, or an adjusted net loss in the first half of 2028, when the company has said it will be positive. Most of these move the base's inputs; two move the weights, each on its own. The notes above 15% at mid-2027, or a loss in the first half of 2028, takes the distress weight to 30% and the bull to 15%, and fair value to $39.05. The mirror: the notes below 11% at mid-2027, or adjusted net income positive in 2027, takes the distress weight to 10% and the bull to 25%, and fair value to $58.09. Because our value sits at the price, any of these moves it off the price. Two alone take our base case down to the price: new contracts on capacity added in 2027 earning $16.9 million a megawatt a year or less (backlog capacity $2 million less), read as the rise in annualized revenue over 2027 divided by the megawatts added in 2027 (the measure that gave $13.8 million for 2026), or new asset-level money at 10.8% or more.

The call

The call

CoreWeave's price fell through July's fair value while its business improved, and now sits on ours: $48.57 against $48.73, with our base case at $58.34. Each market is right about a different thing. The credit market is right about the signed book, which no longer covers the debt plus the build it needs, and about the parent's unsecured claim, priced at 15%; that is a case inside our distribution, and with the bear, 45% of our weight sits at or near zero. The business is right about the fleet. The equity's value rests on three things the October selloff priced as unlikely and the quarter made more likely: new capacity earning near the rates the company now quotes, the paid-off fleet re-contracting, and asset-level lenders funding the build at single-digit rates. More than half of the $48.57 comes from the bull's fifth of the weight; the history is short and the renewal revenue unreported, which is why the cases run from zero to $132.11.
